# The GWAS Changed the Logic for Dopamine in ME/CFS — But Not the Caution

Before the DecodeME GWAS — the largest genetic study of ME/CFS to date — giving dopamine-targeting drugs to patients was largely empirical. You tried something and hoped. The GWAS has shifted the rationale: the genetic risk variants that emerged cluster in neurons that are dopamine-responsive, which means dopamine-focused treatments now rest on a mechanistic foundation rather than guesswork. That is a genuine scientific advance. But it is not, on its own, a treatment recommendation.

Methylphenidate (Ritalin) remains the most-studied dopamine drug in this population. The Blockmans randomised controlled trial found that it improves cognition and fatigue scores — but notably, it does not improve physical functioning. It isn't treating the disease; it's masking the brain fog that the disease produces. Stimulants suppress the signal that tells you your energy is running low, and in a condition defined by impaired energy production and post-exertional malaise, that creates a real hazard. The patient feels capable, exceeds their energy envelope, and crashes harder. No stimulant creates energy.

A handful of other dopamine agents have theoretical appeal but zero ME/CFS trials to date: bupropion, a dopamine/norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor that also carries anti-inflammatory properties; levodopa/carbidopa, the direct dopamine precursor used in Parkinson's; rotigotine, a transdermal dopamine receptor agonist patch that would bypass any storage deficit; and the D2/D3 agonists pramipexole and ropinirole, though these come with a 5–15% risk of impulse control disorders that should give anyone pause.

There is also an emerging finding worth watching. PET imaging in long COVID has shown reduced VMAT2 binding in the striatum — VMAT2 being the protein that packages dopamine for storage and release. The storage mechanism itself appears damaged. If the same lesion extends to ME/CFS, then drugs that restore packaging or bypass it entirely might be more rational than drugs that try to squeeze more dopamine out of a broken system. This has not been tested.

What the genetics gives us is not permission to prescribe — it's a better aim. When we now consider a dopamine drug in ME/CFS, we are no longer guessing which circuits matter; we have a map. But aiming at the right circuit doesn't make every drug that hits it safe. A stimulant that masks fatigue without fixing the underlying energy deficit is like disconnecting the low-fuel warning light in your car. The real problem continues whether you can see it or not.
