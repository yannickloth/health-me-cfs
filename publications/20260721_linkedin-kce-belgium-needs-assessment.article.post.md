# 749 Belgians with ME/CFS Told Their Government What's Broken — And the Data Is Hard to Look Away From

Belgium's Health Care Knowledge Centre (KCE) has just published what may be the most detailed needs assessment of ME/CFS patients ever conducted in Europe. The scale is significant: 749 patients surveyed, 19 in-depth interviews, all cross-referenced against government administrative data. And the picture it paints is sobering.

On the EQ-5D-5L, a standardised health questionnaire, the average quality-of-life score came in at 0.36. For context, that sits below the scores reported in comparable studies for multiple sclerosis, stroke, and cancer. Patients estimated a 57% decline from their pre-illness baseline. And since the most severely affected patients were unable to complete the survey at all, the true average is almost certainly lower.

The diagnostic delay alone tells a story. Half of all respondents waited more than two years for a diagnosis; 39% waited more than five. Each additional year of waiting was independently associated with worse outcomes (p = 0.010). Delay, in other words, is not just frustrating — it is causally harmful.

The comorbidity numbers reinforce a pattern that has been visible in the literature for years but rarely captured this comprehensively. Some 73.2% of respondents reported at least one additional diagnosed condition: fibromyalgia in 63%, irritable bowel syndrome in 57%, POTS in 17%, and mast cell activation syndrome in 10.5%. This is not "a complex patient with somatisation." It is a cluster of separately diagnosable conditions that share autonomic, connective tissue, and immune substrates — and it deserves to be treated as such.

On work disability, the government data were unambiguous: a mean duration of invalidity of 8.6 years, placing ME/CFS among the longest of any condition studied under the KCE's needs assessment framework.

A finding that will unsettle anyone who advocates for graded exercise therapy: a majority of patients reported harm from GET. But 26% reported improvement — a minority responder signal that persists, unexplained, and that the report rightly frames as demanding investigation rather than prescription.

And then there is the finding specific to Belgium, but with a lesson for every health system: French-speaking patients had significantly worse outcomes than Dutch-speaking ones, and the most plausible explanation is structural. Belgium's single ME/CFS reference centre is in Flanders. Same disease, same country, different access to care, and measurably worse results.

This is a government-funded report, addressed squarely to the institutions that control access to care and benefits: 285 pages of data that patients can cite to their doctors, their disability assessors, and their policymakers.
