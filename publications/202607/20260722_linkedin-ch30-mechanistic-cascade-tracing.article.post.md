# What Failed Treatments Tell Us About the Circuits That Are Broken

Every ME/CFS patient has that drawer — the one filled with medications they tried that didn't work. The antidepressant that made everything worse. The stimulant that cleared the brain fog for a few hours and then sent them into a crash. The beta-blocker that slowed the racing heart but turned the fatigue into something closer to collapse. Each one was stopped and replaced, and each time, in a sense, it was a diagnostic test whose results never got read.

The new chapter of my paper offers a way to read them — not as definitive diagnoses, but as tools for narrowing down which mechanisms are actually at play. If low-dose naltrexone doesn't help, then the bottleneck is probably not TLR4-mediated neuroinflammation or TRPM3 channel dysfunction. If pyridostigmine doesn't improve your heart-rate variability, the vagal efferent pathway may simply be non-functional — whether the problem sits at the ganglion, in central circuits, or in the nerve structure itself. Each null result eliminates a class of mechanisms from consideration. A positive LDN trial hints at neuroinflammation, or TRPM3, or endorphin signalling, or orexin — four possibilities. A null response says it's none of the four. You get more diagnostic information from a failure than from a success.

Sometimes the way a drug fails is more revealing than the fact that it did. Midodrine producing severe hypertension at 2.5 mg isn't a failed trial — that's denervation hypersensitivity, pointing strongly toward neuropathic POTS. Fludrocortisone making orthostatic symptoms worse isn't paradoxical either: the extra volume went to the legs instead of the heart, which means the real problem is venous pooling, not low blood volume. The drug that didn't work just helped you find the one that will.

Aripiprazole giving someone akathisia at a microdose tells you the D2/D3 receptors may be supersensitive, and the dopamine deficiency severe enough that even a partial agonist at minimal receptor occupancy is too much — so you start at 0.1 mg instead. Guanfacine improving brain fog but crashing blood pressure suggests the patient is sympathetically dependent: removing even a fraction of central sympathetic drive can collapse the hemodynamics. Add midodrine, and guanfacine might become accessible.

There are patterns like this everywhere in the response data, if you look. Midodrine raises the blood pressure but produces no goosebumps — possible cutaneous sympathetic denervation while vascular α1 receptors stay intact, a picture consistent with small-fiber neuropathy. Antihistamines don't make you drowsy: CNS histamine receptors may be downregulated from chronic mast cell activation. Guanfacine improves brain fog but worsens fatigue — the same drug pulling two symptoms in opposite directions, because they probably involve different mechanisms to begin with.

The beta-blocker that wrecked your energy suggests something specific: your metabolism may depend on β2-AR-mediated fatty acid oxidation, and the usual glucose-to-ATP pathway may be compromised, leaving your body on a metabolic fallback. Is that certainty? No. But it's a clue. And in a disease with no validated biomarkers and no established diagnostics, a drawer full of clues is better than a drawer full of failures.

---

All medications discussed are presented for mechanistic inference — not as clinical recommendations. Any treatment decision requires physician oversight. The chapter's framework carries a default certainty of 0.25–0.40. Nothing in this chapter has been validated prospectively in ME/CFS cohorts.

### Medication Classes

| Drug | Class |
|------|-------|
| LDN (low-dose naltrexone) | Opioid receptor antagonist at low dose; TLR4 antagonist; TRPM3 restoration shown in vitro |
| Pyridostigmine (Mestinon) | Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (peripheral cholinergic) |
| Midodrine | α1-adrenergic receptor agonist (vasoconstrictor) |
| Fludrocortisone | Mineralocorticoid (volume expansion, sodium retention) |
| Aripiprazole (low-dose) | D2/D3 partial agonist (atypical antipsychotic at microdose) |
| Guanfacine | α2A-adrenergic receptor agonist (central sympatholytic) |
| Beta-blockers | β1/β2-adrenergic receptor antagonists; β2 blockade impairs fat mobilization |
| Ivabradine | I_f channel blocker (heart rate reduction) |
