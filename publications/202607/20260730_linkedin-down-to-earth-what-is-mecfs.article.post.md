A meta-analysis of 45 studies puts the pooled prevalence of ME/CFS at 0.89% — one person out of every 112. Do the math on your own social circle and you get about 1.3 patients per person on average, which means roughly three out of four of us know someone with this disease. A colleague who stopped showing up, a cousin who's "not feeling well" every time there's a family gathering, a friend who vanished from the group chat and never came back.

The reason most people don't notice is that 84 to 91% are undiagnosed, and of those who do get diagnosed, the majority are invisible by sheer physical necessity. About 11% are severe — bedridden, averaging 2,000 steps a day — and 2% are very severe: they cannot swallow, cannot tolerate light, cannot speak, and 17% of them require tube feeding because their muscles literally lack the energy to move food down. For these patients, even the sound of someone whispering across the room is enough to trigger a crash that lasts days.

So what is ME/CFS? A disease of energy production failure — the mitochondria, your cells' power plants, don't make enough ATP — kept in a state of metabolic lockdown by a brainstem that's stuck in autonomic dysfunction and neuroinflammation. It's most often triggered by infections: Epstein-Barr, SARS-CoV-2, influenza. The RECOVER study found that 4.5% of COVID-infected people develop ME/CFS, which is nearly five times the rate in uninfected controls, and that's how Germany went from about 250,000 patients before the pandemic to over 650,000 by the end of 2024. France is estimated at around half a million patients — with five specialist doctors to serve them all. The EU total is roughly 4 million, and worldwide we're at about 71 million.

The severity spectrum makes the gap between "functional" and "destroyed" concrete:

- Mild (~29%, ~8,200 steps/day): You can work, but everything is dulled — your humor, your motivation, your ability to focus — because the energy deficit never lifts. You look normal, which means nobody believes you're sick, and that disbelief is its own kind of exhaustion.
- Moderate (~58%, ~5,200 steps/day): Mostly housebound. A shower or a meal — pick one, because you won't have the energy for both. Your career, your social life, your independence have all shrunk to the walls of your home.
- Severe (~11%, ~2,000 steps/day): Bedridden. Sitting upright is an achievement. A wheelchair is the only way to move at all.
- Very severe (~2%): Tube-fed, in the dark, in silence, 24 hours a day, year after year. 76% never receive a visitor. They cannot speak, so they cannot ask for help.

And between all these levels sits the same brutal threshold: post-exertional malaise. A patient might go from crashing after walking 100 meters to being able to sustain 500 meters before the wall hits. Walk 600, 700, and the body retaliates — three days in bed, unable to move. The difference between "I took a short walk" and "I'm bedbound for the rest of the week" is measured in meters.

This is not rare. It is just hidden.
