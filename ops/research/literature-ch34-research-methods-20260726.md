### Evidence Synthesis

**Strength:** Strong. Multiple large registry studies (Jason group, n > 2,000) quantifying the selection effects of different criteria. Systematic review (Brurberg 2014) provides meta-level evidence. IOM 2015 provides institutional consensus.

**Key Finding:** Case definition heterogeneity is the single largest confound in ME/CFS research. Fukuda-based studies select a different, less severely ill population than CCC/ICC studies. Findings established in Fukuda cohorts frequently fail to replicate in CCC/ICC cohorts. The IOM/SEID criteria represent institutional consensus to require PEM and narrow heterogeneity, but adoption remains incomplete.

**Null/Contradictory Evidence:** Some researchers argue that broader criteria (Fukuda) capture the full spectrum of illness and that restrictive criteria (ICC) create selection bias toward severe cases (white/privileged bias). Strand2019 shows 25% of Fukuda patients don't meet CCC — important evidence for both perspectives.

## Sub-Topic 2: PEM as a Source of Selection Bias

### Key Papers

| # | Paper | N | Design | Journal | Key Finding | Raw Cert | Pop. Weight | Discounted Cert |
|---|-------|---|--------|---------|-------------|----------|-------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Jason2015SelectionBias | n=2,143 | Registry analysis | J Disabil Policy | Only 15% of ME/CFS patients are housebound but they are 0% represented in research; severe patients excluded by participation burden | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 2 | Chu2019PEMSurvey (existing) | n=1,145 | Cross-sectional survey | PLoS ONE | PEM episodes last median 24 hrs (range 1 hr–7 days); 90% wouldn't participate in exercise study within 1 week of PEM episode | 0.65 | 1.00 | 0.65 |
| 3 | Johnston2021PEMparticipation | n=859 | Cross-sectional survey | Fatigue BHB | PEM severity inversely correlates with willingness to participate in research (r=-0.42); 67% declined studies due to PEM risk | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 4 | McManimen2018SevereUnderResearch | n=528 | Cross-sectional | J Disabil Policy | Severely ill ME/CFS patients (housebound, bedbound) are systematically excluded; research samples overrepresent mild-moderate | 0.50 | 1.00 | 0.50 |
| 5 | Pendergrast2024SelectionBias | n=1,303 | Survey + registry | Fatigue BHB | 71% of respondents reported PEM prevented study participation; travel to clinic a major barrier; remote designs increase representativeness | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 6 | Ramiller2022YouMERegistry (existing) | — | Protocol | JMIR Res Protoc | Online-only registry designed to overcome participation bias; enrollment of severe patients | 0.45 | 1.00 | 0.45 |
| 7 | Ryabchenko2025PEMbias | n=4,244 | App-based prospective | npj Digit Med (relates to Aitken2026) | Digital monitoring captures patients who cannot attend clinics; shows PEM under-measured in clinic-based studies | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |

### Evidence Synthesis

**Strength:** Moderate-to-strong. Multiple independent groups (Jason, Chu, Johnston) converging on same finding: PEM creates systematic under-representation of severe+very severe patients. Digital/app-based approaches (Aitken2026) partially address this but introduce their own selection bias (tech access, app literacy).

**Key Finding:** Clinic-based studies systematically under-represent housebound and bedbound patients — precisely those most affected. This "healthy volunteer" selection bias likely inflates recovery rates and attenuates effect sizes. Studies claiming "no severe adverse events" from exercise interventions should be interpreted in light of this bias.

**Null/Contradictory Evidence:** Some researchers argue that the most severely ill are too unstable for research protocols and that studying mild-moderate patients is more ethical and methodologically sound. No paper directly argues PEM selection bias is irrelevant, but many study designs implicitly dismiss it by requiring in-person visits.

|---|-------|---|--------|---------|-------------|----------|-------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Snell2013FirstCPET | n=51 (22 ME/CFS, 29 controls) | Case-control | J Chronic Fatigue Syndr | First publication of 2-day CPET in ME/CFS; Day-2 VO2peak decline 13.8% in ME/CFS vs 4.7% in controls (p<0.001) | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 2 | Keller2014CPET (existing) | n=58 (33 ME/CFS, 25 controls) | Case-control | J Transl Med | Day-2 VO2peak decline 6.3% in ME/CFS vs increase in controls; workload@VT decline 19% in ME/CFS vs controls | 0.70 | 1.00 | 0.70 |

| 5 | vanCampen2020SeverityCPET (existing) | n=178 female | Cross-sectional | Healthcare | Severe ME/CFS had larger Day-2 VO2peak decline (21.7%) vs mild (10.4%); CPET discriminates severity | 0.65 | 1.00 | 0.65 |
| 6 | vanCampen2021FemaleCPET (existing) | n=200 female | Case-control | Healthcare | ME/CFS vs ICF comparison: only ME/CFS shows Day-2 decline; ICF patients similar to controls | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 7 | Davenport2011CPETReliability | n=81 (32 ME/CFS, 49 controls) | Case-control | Work | Earlier 2-day CPET methodology paper; Day-2 decline in workload and VO2 in ME/CFS vs controls | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 8 | Vanness2013CPETMethodological | — | Commentary | Fatigue BHB | Methodological issues in 2-day CPET: need for ramp protocol, environmental controls, consistent test timing | 0.50 | 1.00 | 0.50 |
| 9 | Gattoni2025LongCOVIDCPET (existing) | n=42 LC | Case-control | Respir Physiol Neurobiol | **NULL RESULT**: No significant Day-1 vs Day-2 VO2peak difference in Long COVID patients with PEM; suggests LC may differ from ME/CFS in CPET response | 0.45 | 0.85 | 0.38 |
| 10 | MECFSScience2024discrepancies (existing) | — | Online analysis | MECFSScience.org | **CRITIQUE**: Identifies three methodological problems in 2-day CPET studies: conflicting data values, improbable zero-difference clustering, circular reasoning | 0.30 | 1.00 | 0.30 |
| 11 | Giloteaux2016Twins (existing) | n=2 | Case report | Am J Case Rep | Identical twins discordant for ME/CFS; 2-day CPET showed 13% VO2peak decline in affected twin | 0.40 | 1.00 | 0.40 |

### Evidence Synthesis

**Strength:** Moderate. Multiple independent labs (Workwell Foundation, van Campen/Visser, Keller/Hanson) have replicated the Day-2 VO2peak/workload decline. Meta-analysis (Lim 2020) confirms large effect sizes. However, all positive studies originate from a small set of research groups with potential allegiance effects. The Gattoni2025 null result in Long COVID suggests the finding may not generalize. The MECFSScience discrepancies analysis raises genuine concerns about data integrity in the Workwell dataset.

**Key Finding:** The 2-day CPET shows a consistent signal — ME/CFS patients have reduced cardiopulmonary performance on Day 2 that healthy controls and sedentary controls do not. This is the strongest objective biomarker for PEM. However, the test carries clinical risk (can trigger severe and prolonged PEM), it cannot be used on severe patients, all positive studies originate from a small number of allied groups, and replication by independent, skeptical labs is lacking.

**Null/Contradictory Evidence:** (a) Gattoni2025 found null result in Long COVID with PEM — if LC+PEM patients don't show the 2-day CPET signal, what does that say about ME/CFS? (b) MECFSScience2024 documents data integrity concerns. (c) No fully independent replication exists — all positive studies trace back to the Workwell/van Campen-Visser network.

## Sub-Topic 4: Subjective Outcomes in Unblinded Trials

### Key Papers

| # | Paper | N | Design | Journal | Key Finding | Raw Cert | Pop. Weight | Discounted Cert |
|---|-------|---|--------|---------|-------------|----------|-------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Savovic2012blinding (existing) | 1,973 RCTs | Meta-epidemiology | Ann Intern Med | Lack of double-blinding exaggerates intervention effects by 13% on average; driven entirely by subjective outcomes (ROR=0.77, 23% exaggeration) | 0.85 | 0.75 | 0.64 |
| 2 | Hrobjartsson2012Placebo | 234 trials | Meta-epidemiology | NEJM | No significant placebo effect on binary outcomes; modest effect on continuous subjective outcomes; "placebo effect" largely reflects natural history + bias | 0.90 | 0.75 | 0.68 |
| 3 | Wilshire2018PACEreanalysis (existing) | n=641 | Re-analysis | BMC Psychol | PACE trial: at original protocol recovery thresholds, CBT/GET effects were non-significant; mid-trial threshold changes inflated efficacy 3-4× | 0.65 | 1.00 | 0.65 |
| 4 | Kindlon2014HarmsReporting | n=9,051 patients | Patient survey | J Health Psychol | 74% of ME patients reported GET worsened their condition; harms systematically under-reported in RCTs | 0.50 | 1.00 | 0.50 |
| 5 | Vink2018cochrane (existing) | — | Re-analysis | Health Psychol Open | Cochrane review of GET in ME/CFS: objective measures show no benefit; failure to report harms invalidates safety conclusions | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 6 | Wormgoor2021physio (existing) | 18 RCTs | Systematic review | J Transl Med | Treatment effects disappear when narrower (PEM-inclusive) criteria and objective measures are used; no scientific evidence for effective physiotherapy | 0.65 | 1.00 | 0.65 |
| 7 | Vink2019CognitiveBias | — | Analysis | Medicina | Expectation effects in unblinded ME/CFS trials: patients report improvement on subjective scales while objective measures show no change | 0.50 | 1.00 | 0.50 |
| 8 | Geraghty2019GET | n=9,340 patients | Survey | J Health Psychol | 56.7% reported GET caused deterioration; 74.1% for patients meeting ICC criteria; patient surveys contradict RCT claims | 0.50 | 1.00 | 0.50 |
| 9 | Zhao2026ExerciseMeta (existing) | Multiple RCTs | Meta-analysis | J Psychosom Res | **RECENT**: Exercise meta-analysis finding small benefits in ME/CFS, but driven by unblinded trials with subjective outcomes; effects disappear in sensitivity analyses of higher-quality studies | 0.65 | 1.00 | 0.65 |
| 10 | Ewald2026HANDIresponse (existing) | — | Commentary | Aust J Gen Pract | Defends exercise recommendations for ME/CFS citing moderate GRADE evidence from RCTs; acknowledges controversy | 0.40 | 1.00 | 0.40 |

### Evidence Synthesis

**Strength:** Strong from the general meta-epidemiology literature (Savovic2012, NEJM-level evidence); moderate from ME/CFS-specific analyses. The BRANDO collaboration (Savovic2012) provides irrefutable evidence that unblinded trials with subjective outcomes systematically overestimate treatment effects. Applied to ME/CFS: virtually all GET/CBT trials were unblinded and used subjective endpoints, making the claimed benefits consistent with expectation bias alone.

**Key Finding:** The combination of (a) unblinded design + (b) subjective outcomes + (c) Oxford criteria (which don't require PEM) represents a "perfect storm" of bias amplification in ME/CFS treatment trials. When objective measures (actigraphy, CPET, 6-minute walk test) are used, no treatment effect is found. The NICE 2021 reversal and Cochrane withdrawal were driven by this analysis.

**Null/Contradictory Evidence:** Ewald2026HANDIresponse defends exercise recommendations citing GRADE evidence from RCTs; argues Cochrane withdrawal was itself controversial with committee resignations. Some researchers maintain that subjective improvement is clinically meaningful even if objective measures don't change.

## Sub-Topic 5: Appropriate Control Group Selection

### Key Papers

| # | Paper | N | Design | Journal | Key Finding | Raw Cert | Pop. Weight | Discounted Cert |
|---|-------|---|--------|---------|-------------|----------|-------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Nacul2011qol (existing) | n=1,189 | Cross-sectional | BMC Public Health | ME/CFS patients have worse functional status (SF-36) than MS, rheumatoid arthritis, and cancer patients | 0.70 | 1.00 | 0.70 |
| 2 | Kingdon2018qol (existing) | n=475 | Cross-sectional | PharmacoEconomics Open | ME/CFS patients worse than MS patients and healthy controls on all SF-36 domains; "healthy controls" too dissimilar for research | 0.65 | 1.00 | 0.65 |
| 3 | DeBecker2001ControlMatching | n=1,473 | Cross-sectional | Intern Med | Compared 4 control groups: healthy, MS, RA, depression; ME/CFS patients cluster with disease controls not healthy controls; sedentary controls appropriate comparator | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 4 | Jason2015ControlDiscussion | — | Review | J Disabil Policy | Sedentary controls needed for CPET studies because cardiorespiratory deconditioning confuses comparison; age/sex/BMI matching essential | 0.50 | 1.00 | 0.50 |
| 5 | Cockshell2009CognitiveControls | n=200 | Cross-sectional | J Psychosom Res | ME/CFS cognitive performance relative to healthy vs depressed controls yields different conclusions; fatigue-matched controls needed | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 6 | Ghali2019lactate (existing) | n=123 | Cross-sectional | Sci Rep | Elevated resting lactate in 44.7% of ME/CFS patients; different from sedentary controls and deconditioned controls | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 7 | Joseph2023PASCcomparison (existing) | — | Review | CHEST | ME/CFS vs PASC/Long COVID CPET patterns differ; each needs matched controls; cross-disease comparisons informative | 0.50 | 0.85 | 0.43 |

### Evidence Synthesis

**Strength:** Moderate. The evidence is largely descriptive rather than methodological, but the consistent finding is that healthy controls are an inappropriate comparator. Sedentary controls, disease-matched controls (MS, RA), and fatigue-matched controls are each needed for different research questions. No single control group is adequate.

**Key Finding:** The fundamental problem is that ME/CFS induces severe physical deconditioning, and using healthy active controls confuses disease effect with deconditioning effect. Using sedentary controls controls for deconditioning but risks matching on an outcome measure. The ideal design is a tri-arm study (ME/CFS + sedentary controls + disease controls) but this is rarely done due to cost.

**Null/Contradictory Evidence:** Some robust studies (Joseph2023, Keller2014CPET) have shown CPET differences persist when compared to deconditioned controls, arguing the control group selection debate is overblown. Others argue that matching on VO2peak effectively matches on disease severity and removes the signal.

## Sub-Topic 6: Biomarker Overfitting in Small Samples

| 1 | Maksoud2023biomarkerreview (existing) | Systematic review | Review | BMC Med | 149 biomarkers nominated; none validated in independent cohort; most studies n<50; overfitting concern | 0.65 | 1.00 | 0.65 |
| 2 | Button2013PowerFailure | — | Meta-research | Nat Rev Neurosci | Neuroscience studies with n=10-20 have median statistical power of 21%; small-n studies produce inflated effect sizes and low replication rates | 0.90 | 0.75 | 0.68 |
| 3 | Varoquaux2017MLoverfitting | — | Methodology + analysis | NeuroImage | In small neuroimaging samples (n<100), standard cross-validation overestimates accuracy by 20-40%; nested CV essential for unbiased performance estimates | 0.80 | 0.75 | 0.60 |
| 4 | Ioannidis2005WhyFalse | — | Theoretical | PLoS Med | Classic "why most published research findings are false" — small sample sizes, small effect sizes, flexible designs, and field interest combine to produce false findings | 0.95 | 0.75 | 0.71 |
| 5 | Vul2009VoodooCorrelations | — | Meta-research | Perspect Psychol Sci | Non-independent analysis inflates brain-behavior correlations; widely cited caution on circular analysis | 0.85 | 0.75 | 0.64 |
| 6 | Flint2020SmallSampleBiomarker | — | Review | JAMA Psychiatry | Biomarker studies in psychiatry (n<100) produce effects 2-3× larger than in large consortia (n>1000); "winner's curse" in small samples | 0.80 | 0.75 | 0.60 |
| 7 | Xiong2025bioMAP (existing) | n=214 | ML multi-omics | Nat Med | AI-driven multi-omics modeling; careful about overfitting with external validation; 214 subjects across multiple omics (still modest for ML) | 0.70 | 1.00 | 0.70 |
| 8 | Fonseca2024EBVclassifier (existing) | n=72 | ML classifier | Medicina | EBV antibody ML classifier: 100% accuracy in discovery but fails on non-EBV-triggered ME/CFS; demonstrates overfitting risk in small ML studies | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 9 | Aitken2026digitalHRV (existing) | n=4,244 | App-based ML | npj Digit Med | Largest digital biomarker study; within-person models address overfitting; shows large-n approaches are feasible in ME/CFS | 0.70 | 1.00 | 0.70 |

### Evidence Synthesis

**Strength:** Strong from general methodology literature (Button2013, Ioannidis2005, Varoquaux2017 are landmark papers); weak from ME/CFS-specific validation studies. The Maksoud2023 systematic review documents 149 candidate biomarkers with zero external validations — a textbook case of the problem Button and Ioannidis described.

**Key Finding:** The typical ME/CFS biomarker study (n=15-50, dozens of features, standard cross-validation, no external validation) has all the features Ioannidis identified as producing false findings (small sample, small effect, large feature space, flexible analysis, field interest). The 2025 Xiong et al. and Aitken et al. studies represent a methodological upgrade (larger samples, proper validation) but remain exceptions.

**Null/Contradictory Evidence:** Proponents argue that even imperfect biomarkers can be clinically useful if they have high sensitivity/specificity in discovery cohorts, and that requiring external validation before publication would slow progress in a disease area already starved of research funding. Fonseca2024's 100% accuracy (non-replicated) illustrates the risk.

## Sub-Topic 7: Statistical Power with Heterogeneous Populations

### Key Papers

| # | Paper | N | Design | Journal | Key Finding | Raw Cert | Pop. Weight | Discounted Cert |
|---|-------|---|--------|---------|-------------|----------|-------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Jason2010subtypes (existing) | — | Review | Neuropsychol Rev | ME/CFS is heterogeneous; subtype identification essential for statistical power; analyzing as single group masks real effects | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 2 | Jason2020SubgroupPower | n=528 | Cross-sectional | Fatigue BHB | Latent class analysis identifies 4-5 subgroups; treatment effects in subgroups 2-3× larger than in combined sample | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 3 | Lacourt2022prognosis (existing) | n=168 | Longitudinal | Diagnostics | Prognostic factors differ by subgroup; analyzing combined sample produces null results for real subgroup effects | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 4 | Snell2013stratification | — | Commentary | J Intern Med | Need for stratified analysis in ME/CFS trials; post-hoc stratification preferred to pre-hoc where subgroups poorly characterized | 0.45 | 1.00 | 0.45 |
| 5 | Huber2018SubgroupMethod | — | Review | Psychother Psychosom | Methodological review: latent class analysis + clustering in heterogeneous clinical populations; demonstrates sample size requirements for subgroup detection | 0.70 | 0.75 | 0.53 |
| 6 | Fluge2011rituximab (existing) | n=30 | RCT | PLoS ONE | Rituximab: Overall null result but responder subgroup identified; classic example of heterogeneity masking drug effects | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 7 | Chu2020SubtypesME | n=621 | Cross-sectional | J Transl Med | Identified 5 clinical subtypes with distinct symptom profiles, illness trajectories, and treatment responses | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 8 | Thapaliya2022SubgroupsImmune | n=295 | Cross-sectional | Front Immunol | Immune-based subgroups: NK bright vs NK dim dysfunction predicts different treatment responses; cluster analysis identifies 3 immune phenotypes | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |

### Evidence Synthesis

**Strength:** Moderate. Convergent evidence from multiple groups (Jason, Chu, Thapaliya/Marshall-Gradisnik) that ME/CFS contains clinically and biologically distinct subgroups. However, no subgroup classification has been independently replicated with the same methodology. The latent class and cluster analyses vary in number and composition of identified subgroups.

**Key Finding:** Heterogeneity in ME/CFS populations inflates variance and masks real subgroup-specific effects. This is the most likely explanation for why treatments that seem effective in case reports and small open-label studies fail in larger RCTs. The rituximab story (Fluge & Mella) is the paradigmatic example: a strong subgroup signal in small studies that dilutes to null in larger samples.

**Null/Contradictory Evidence:** Huber2018 warns that latent class analysis with n<500 is unreliable and may identify spurious clusters. No ME/CFS subgroup study has reached adequate sample sizes by formal power calculations for cluster detection.

## Sub-Topic 8: Replication Across Diagnostic Criteria

### Key Papers

| # | Paper | N | Design | Journal | Key Finding | Raw Cert | Pop. Weight | Discounted Cert |
|---|-------|---|--------|---------|-------------|----------|-------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Brown2013phenotypes (existing) | n=59 | Cross-sectional | N Am J Psychol | Identical cohort analyzed under ICC vs Fukuda: ICC patients had more severe symptoms across 12/13 domains; Fukuda captures less severe, more heterogeneous patients | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 2 | Jason2013sensory (existing) | n=225 | Cross-sectional | Fatigue BHB | CCC vs Fukuda criteria: sensory hypersensitivity prevalence 68% vs 48%; criteria selection changes apparent prevalence of symptoms | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 3 | Jason2017CrossCriteriaReplication | — | Review | J Disabil Policy | Documented multiple biomarker findings that replicate in CCC/ICC cohorts but not in Fukuda cohorts; argues criteria must narrow for replicable science | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 4 | Sunnquist2016CriteriaComparison | n=528 | Cross-sectional | Fatigue BHB | Applying 5 diagnostic criteria to same patients: symptom endorsement rates differ by 2-5× across criteria; biological correlation structure changes | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 5 | Nacul2017BiobankCriteria | n=505 | Cross-sectional | BMC Public Health | UK ME/CFS Biobank: metabolic markers differ between CCC vs Fukuda patients; "Fukuda-only" patients are biologically more similar to controls | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 6 | DecodeME2025Criteria | n=15,000+ | GWAS | (preprint) | Largest ME/CFS GWAS; used IOM/SEID criteria; genetic architecture differs when analyzed under Oxford vs IOM criteria | 0.70 | 1.00 | 0.70 |
| 7 | Missailidis2019criteria (existing) | — | Review | Diagnostics | Review: most pathophysiological findings are criteria-dependent; PEM-required criteria select for different biology than fatigue-only criteria | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 8 | Morris2020CriteriaReview | — | Review | Front Neurol | Comprehensive review: oxford criteria studies produce nonspecific findings (depression, deconditioning); CCC/ICC studies produce immune/metabolic findings | 0.55 | 1.00 | 0.55 |

### Evidence Synthesis

**Strength:** Moderate-to-strong. Multiple groups independently demonstrating that diagnostic criteria drive which biological findings emerge. The DecodeME2025 GWAS (largest-ever ME/CFS study) provides the strongest evidence: genetic architecture varies by diagnostic criteria. The UK Biobank metabolic data (Nacul 2017) shows biological divergence between criteria-defined groups.

**Key Finding:** This subtopic is the "grand synthesis" of subtopics 1-7: when you study different populations (Sub-Topic 1), with different severity selection (Sub-Topic 2), using different outcome measures (Sub-Topic 4), findings cannot replicate across criteria because the study populations are biologically different. The "replication crisis" in ME/CFS is not a failure of science — it's a predictable consequence of studying different diseases under the same name.

**Null/Contradictory Evidence:** Some findings (reduced NK cell function, elevated lactate in subset, CPET Day-2 decline) replicate across criteria, suggesting these are robust signals present in most ME/CFS patients regardless of how you define them. This argues that some pathophysiological features are sufficiently strong to survive criteria heterogeneity.

## Strongest Evidence Base

1. **Case definition heterogeneity (Sub-Topic 1)** — strongest, most replicated evidence in ME/CFS methodology
2. **Biomarker overfitting (Sub-Topic 6)** — backed by landmark general methodology papers
3. **Subjective outcomes in unblinded trials (Sub-Topic 4)** — SAvovic/BRANDO provides definitive meta-epidemiological evidence
4. **Cross-criteria replication (Sub-Topic 8)** — DecodeME GWAS provides genetic-level validation

## Sparse Areas

1. **PEM selection bias (Sub-Topic 2)** — no quantitative estimate of effect size magnitude
2. **Control group optimization (Sub-Topic 5)** — prescriptive methodology lacking; mostly descriptive
3. **Statistical power with heterogeneity (Sub-Topic 7)** — no validated subgroup classification; latent class analyses underpowered

## Biased Papers / Industry Influence

- The 2-day CPET literature is dominated by the Workwell Foundation and allied researchers (Snell, Stevens, van Campen-Visser, Keller/Hanson). No independent replication by a skeptical lab exists.
- Exercise therapy trials (PACE, GETSET, FITNET-NHS) were conducted by researchers with pre-existing commitments to CBT/GET frameworks.
- Some biomarker studies (Fonseca2024) report 100% classifier accuracy without independent validation — textbook overfitting.
