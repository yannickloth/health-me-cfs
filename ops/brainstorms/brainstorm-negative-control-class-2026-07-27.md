#### 1.1 The Inverse Negative-Control: When a Positive Response Falsifies the Hypothesis

**Category:** 1 — Novel Hypotheses About the Negative-Control Class
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The existing negative-control definition in subsec-13 covers drugs where a *null* response falsifies a hypothesis: "the hypothesis predicts the drug must work; if it doesn't, the hypothesis is falsified." But this is only one direction of the logical structure. The dual — the *inverse negative-control* — is drugs where a *positive* response falsifies a hypothesis. If hypothesis H contains the premise "node N is broken" and drug D's mechanism operates downstream of node N, then H predicts D *cannot* work — because the broken node N blocks the signal D needs to produce clinical effect. If D does work, the premise "N is broken" is falsified. This is the inverse of the negative-control: the hypothesis predicts a null, and a positive response is the falsifying observation.

The inverse negative-control is mechanismally narrower than the standard negative-control because it requires a specific broken-node premise within the hypothesis — not just "H predicts D must work" but "H predicts D *cannot* work because H asserts that X, which D depends on, is non-functional." This is a stronger logical structure: in a standard negative-control, a null could have many alternative explanations (drug didn't reach target, dose too low, duration too short). But in an inverse negative-control, a positive response cannot be explained by drug non-engagement — if the drug worked, it *reached its target and produced a biological effect*. The falsification is cleaner.

Candidate inverse negative-controls for ME/CFS: (a) If the TRPM3 Ca²⁺ channel is non-functional in mast cells (TRPM3 hypothesis), then mast cell stabilizers that act via Ca²⁺-dependent pathways (cromolyn sodium, ketotifen — both depend on blocking Ca²⁺ flux to prevent degranulation) should be ineffective in TRPM3-mutant ME/CFS patients because Ca²⁺ entry is already impaired. If ketotifen works in a TRPM3-mutant patient, either TRPM3 is not non-functional, or ketotifen works through a non-Ca²⁺ pathway — in either case, the premise "TRPM3 loss-of-function is the rate-limiting Ca²⁺ entry defect" is constrained. (b) If the PDH/ETC bottleneck is a fixed enzymatic block (PDH phosphorylation by PDK, irreversible ETC damage), then substrates that enter upstream of the block (glucose, pyruvate) should not produce energy — they would accumulate as lactate without entering the TCA cycle. If oral glucose or pyruvate produces sustained symptomatic improvement, the "fixed PDH block" premise is falsified — energy substrate flowed past the theoretical block. (c) If the central norepinephrine deficiency hypothesis (NE synthesis failure due to DBH insufficiency or locus coeruleus damage) is correct, then drugs that depend on NE release for their effect (e.g., amphetamine-class stimulants that release NE from presynaptic vesicles) should be ineffective because there is no NE to release. If methylphenidate or atomoxetine produces sustained benefit, the "NE depletion" premise is constrained — there was NE to release, or the drug's effect bypasses NE entirely.

**Evidence link:** No existing literature formalizes inverse negative-controls. The closest precedent is the *contradiction test* in clinical neurology: if a patient with suspected myasthenia gravis (anti-AChR antibody, postsynaptic AChR destruction) responds to pyridostigmine (acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that increases synaptic ACh), the diagnosis is *consistent*. If they respond to IVIG but NOT pyridostigmine, the diagnosis is *problematic* — postsynaptic AChR destruction should not be bypassed by pyridostigmine, so either the AChR loss is incomplete, the antibody is non-pathogenic, or there's an additional presynaptic lesion. This is inverse negative-control reasoning in clinical practice, unformalized. Fluge2019RituxMENegativePhase3 establishes the standard negative-control precedent; the inverse negative-control is its logical dual. Cabanas 2019 TRPM3 mutation data and the PDH/ETC cascade sections (ch07, ch33) provide the mechanistic premises for specific inverse negative-control candidate identification.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.25

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) For each candidate inverse negative-control drug—hypothesis pair, test in a cohort of patients with confirmed (or highly probable) mechanism. If the hypothesis is confirmed and the inverse negative-control drug produces clinical improvement in >30% of cases, the inverse negative-control reasoning identifies a genuine constraint on the hypothesis's premises. (b) Inverse negative-control drugs show higher specificity than standard negative-control drugs when tested against multi-hypothesis diagnostic panels — fewer patients with non-target hypotheses show positive response to an inverse negative-control than to a standard negative-control. (c) Falsified if: no candidate inverse negative-control drug produces a clinical response that is both (i) positive in the target-hypothesis population, and (ii) diagnostic of mechanism constraint (i.e., cannot be explained by an alternative pathway not captured by the hypothesis).

*Consequence:* If ketotifen works for your MCAS symptoms and you have a confirmed TRPM3 mutation that should make mast cell Ca²⁺ entry impossible, one of two things is true: your TRPM3 mutation doesn't actually impair mast cell Ca²⁺ entry (the hypothesis is wrong about what TRPM3 does), or ketotifen works through a mechanism nobody understands. Either way, the TRPM3-as-bottleneck model for your disease is wrong — and you learned that from a drug that *worked*, not from one that failed.

#### 1.2 Prospective Negative-Control Registry: Pre-Register Falsification Predictions Before Trialing the Drug

**Category:** 2 — Research Directions
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The most damaging gap in negative-control reasoning is post-hoc interpretation. After a drug trial produces a null result, researchers can choose whether to interpret it as a negative-control (falsification of mechanism) or an underdetermined failure (drug didn't work for other reasons). There is currently no pre-registration infrastructure that locks in the negative-control interpretation *before* the trial result is known. This enables motivated reasoning: if the researcher wanted the drug to work, null results are explained away ("dose too low," "wrong patient subgroup"); if the researcher wanted the mechanism to fail, null results are interpreted as negative-control evidence ("this falsifies the AAb hypothesis"). The identical data can support either conclusion, and the interpretive choice is post-hoc and unaccountable.

The solution: a prospective negative-control registry. Before initiating a drug trial that could serve as a negative-control, researchers register: (a) the hypothesis being tested, (b) the specific cascade node the drug intercepts, (c) the negative-control class assignment (I or II), (d) the pre-specified dose, duration, and outcome measures required for the trial to be "adequate" (condition 3 of subsec-13's decision heuristic), (e) explicit thresholds: "if the trial is adequately powered and dosed and the primary endpoint is null at p > 0.05, we will interpret this as Class I negative-control evidence against hypothesis H, with the following surviving alternatives [list]." The registry entry is time-stamped and immutable. Post-hoc re-interpretation is explicitly flagged as a deviation from pre-registered intent. This converts negative-control interpretation from a rhetorical choice to a contract-like commitment.

The registry also enables meta-analysis of negative-controls across the field: how many pre-registered negative-controls have been attempted? How many produced null vs positive results? Which hypotheses have accumulated the most negative-control evidence? This is the infrastructure for the Bayesian FDR approach (Idea 1.9) and addresses the publication bias concern (1.19 counts nulls, not just positives).

**Evidence link:** Clinical trial pre-registration (clinicaltrials.gov, ICMJE policy 2005) eliminated the most egregious forms of post-hoc endpoint switching and selective reporting. The negative-control registry is an analogous pre-commitment device applied to mechanism-level inference rather than treatment efficacy. Fluge2019RituxMENegativePhase3 was properly pre-registered (NCT02229942) — the trial design was locked before results were known. But the *interpretation* of the null as negative-control evidence against the B-cell AAb hypothesis was NOT pre-registered — it's post-hoc, however widely accepted. A negative-control registry would have required Fluge et al. to specify, pre-trial: "if rituximab fails to separate from placebo on the primary fatigue endpoint at week 24, we will interpret this as negative-control evidence against the CD20+ B-cell-dependent GPCR-AAb hypothesis." That statement was never pre-registered.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.50

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Implement the registry with a 24-month pilot. Count how many pre-registered negative-control interpretations are maintained vs. walked back after null results are obtained. If >30% of pre-registered negative-control nulls are re-interpreted post-hoc as "underdetermined failures," the registry exposed the motivated reasoning problem it was designed to detect — the field's negative-control claims are systematically inflated. (b) Compare the proportion of published null trials interpreted as negative-control evidence in the pre-registry era vs. post-registry era. If the post-registry era shows a lower proportion of null-to-negative-control conversions, the registry has reduced overclaimed falsifications. (c) Falsified if: no researcher registers a negative-control prediction in 24 months — the infrastructure exists but the field won't use it, implying that negative-control reasoning is a post-hoc rhetorical device, not a genuine pre-trial commitment.

*Consequence:* Right now, when a drug fails, researchers decide *after the fact* whether this means the hypothesis was wrong or the trial was inadequate. A registry forces them to decide *beforehand* what a negative result would mean — and holds them to it. This prevents the most common intellectual sin in pharmacodiagnostics: calling every null a negative-control when it suits the narrative, and every null an underdetermined failure when it doesn't.

#### 1.3 Computational High-Throughput Negative-Control Identification Pipeline

**Category:** 2 — Research Directions
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The current negative-control class (subsec-13) identifies negative-controls manually — a researcher examines a hypothesis cascade, identifies a drug that intercepts a rate-limiting node with high specificity, and declares it a negative-control. This manual process is slow, non-exhaustive, and vulnerable to confirmation bias (the researcher identifies drugs that support their preferred interpretation of the hypothesis). A computational pipeline could systematically screen the entire pharmacopeia against every cascade node in every hypothesis to identify candidate negative-control drugs that no researcher would think to consider.

The pipeline architecture: (a) For each cascade node N in each hypothesis H (ch30 sec-01 through sec-08), extract the node's molecular identity (protein, ion channel, receptor, enzyme) and its position in the cascade (upstream, rate-limiting, convergent). (b) For each FDA-approved medication (DrugBank, ChEMBL, or similar), extract the drug's primary molecular target(s) and known off-target interactions. (c) Match drugs to nodes: a drug is a candidate negative-control for hypothesis H if its primary target is N (the node is the drug's therapeutic target) AND N is rate-limiting (not redundant) in H's cascade AND no alternative pathway bypasses N. (d) Score each drug—hypothesis pair on: target specificity (1 if single target, ≤0.5 if multi-target), node position (1 if node is the bottleneck, ≤0.5 if node has redundant bypass), compartment coverage (1 if drug reaches the node's compartment, ≤0.5 if partial), and evidence depth (inference-chain length from drug binding to clinical effect — shorter is better). (e) Output: a ranked list of candidate negative-controls for each hypothesis, with scores. Drugs scoring >0.80 are hyper-specific negative-controls; drugs scoring 0.50–0.80 are moderate negative-controls. The pipeline would identify negative-control drug candidates for mechanisms currently lacking any negative-control — e.g., the connective tissue hypothesis (no drug is currently identified as a negative-control for CT pathology in ME/CFS), the Gerlier hypothesis (shoe lift is a negative-control, but are there pharmacological negative-controls for sympathetic chain compression?), the PIP2 convergence hypothesis (no source-level or pathway-level negative-control identified).

**Evidence link:** DrugBank and ChEMBL provide the molecular target databases. The cascade tracings in ch30 sec-01 through sec-08 provide the node-to-mechanism mapping. High-throughput computational drug repurposing pipelines exist (Pushpakom 2019, Nature Reviews Drug Discovery — systematic review of computational drug repurposing methods), but none have been applied to negative-control identification specifically. The closest analogue is computational drug target deconvolution — identifying a drug's mechanism from its response pattern — used in oncology (Iorio 2016, Cell — pharmacogenomic profiling of 1,001 cancer cell lines). The negative-control pipeline inverts this: from known mechanism, identify which drugs SHOULD produce a null response if the mechanism is false. Ravichandran2024ActiveLearning's labeling bias framework provides the calibration method — the pipeline's candidate rankings would require clinician adjudication to avoid embedding known biases into the computational scores.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.30

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) The pipeline identifies ≥3 candidate negative-control drugs for ≥4 of the 8 hypotheses in ch30 where no negative-control drug is currently identified. (b) ≥2 of the pipeline-identified candidates are drugs no ME/CFS researcher has previously proposed as negative-controls for that hypothesis — i.e., the pipeline produces novel candidates beyond human intuition. (c) When pipeline-identified candidates are tested in a small prospective cohort, ≥50% show concordance between predicted negative-control class and observed clinical null rates — confirming that computational identification has adequate PPV. (d) Falsified if: the pipeline identifies zero hyper-specific negative-controls (score >0.80) for any hypothesis — the pharmacopeia lacks sufficiently specific drugs for negative-control inference, and the negative-control class concept cannot scale beyond the few manually identified examples (rituximab, daratumumab).

*Consequence:* Instead of one researcher spending six months thinking about which drug would falsify a specific mechanism, a computer scans 2,000 approved drugs in an afternoon and ranks the candidates. It will find candidates no human would think of — maybe a blood pressure drug that would serve as the perfect negative-control for autonomic dysfunction if you just look at its mechanism from the right angle. The negative-control class becomes a searchable database, not a handful of researcher intuitions.

#### 1.4 Daratumumab as Class I Source-Level Negative-Control for the Plasma-Cell AAb Hypothesis

**Category:** 3 — Drug/Medication Ideas Relevant to Negative-Control Inference
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Subsec-13 already lists daratumumab as the next logical negative-control probe after rituximab null — but only in passing. Daratumumab (anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody) depletes CD38+ plasma cells and plasmablasts. If the GPCR-AAb hypothesis survives the rituximab null because the pathogenic AAb is produced by CD20− long-lived plasma cells (not CD20+ B cells), then daratumumab directly interrogates that surviving alternative. The cascade logic: GPCR AAb hypothesis → AAb must come from somewhere → if not from CD20+ B cells (rituximab null) → the most likely remaining source is CD38+ plasma cells → daratumumab depletes CD38+ plasma cells → if daratumumab produces a null result in an adequately powered trial, the plasma-cell source is excluded, leaving non-IgG classes (non-CD38-dependent production), tissue-sanctuary plasma cells, or intracellular AAb as surviving sources.

Daratumumab is a Class I negative-control (source-level) because it directly depletes the cells proposed to produce the pathogenic factor. Unlike rituximab, which only depletes CD20+ B-cell precursors to plasma cells, daratumumab eliminates the terminally differentiated antibody-factory cells. If BOTH rituximab AND daratumumab produce null results, the cascade constraints are: (a) the pathogenic factor is not produced by B cells (CD20+) or plasma cells (CD38+), i.e., the antibody-producing compartment hypothesis is essentially falsified, OR (b) daratumumab cannot access long-lived plasma cells in sanctuary sites (bone marrow niches, CNS), i.e., the drug's compartment coverage is incomplete (this is testable — measure plasma cell depletion in bone marrow aspirates).

**Evidence link:** Daratumumab is FDA-approved for multiple myeloma (CD38+ plasma cell malignancy) since 2015. Its plasma-cell-depleting mechanism is well-characterized. It depletes both malignant and normal plasma cells (Frerichs 2020 — daratumumab impairs humoral immunity by depleting normal CD38+ plasma cells, not just malignant ones). No daratumumab trial has been conducted in ME/CFS or any autoimmune disease driven by autoantibodies from long-lived plasma cells — it's an oncology drug, not an autoimmune drug. The rituximab→daratumumab sequential logic is identical to the strategy in refractory autoimmune disease: rituximab fails → suspect plasma-cell-driven autoimmunity → try bortezomib (proteasome inhibitor, plasma-cell-targeted) or daratumumab. This sequential strategy is used in refractory SLE (bortezomib for plasma-cell-driven anti-dsDNA — Alexander 2015) and refractory myasthenia gravis (bortezomib for plasma-cell-driven anti-AChR — Gomez 2020). The ME/CFS application follows the same logic.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.35

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a cohort of GPCR-AAb-positive ME/CFS patients who did NOT respond to rituximab, daratumumab produces clinical improvement (≥30% reduction on fatigue scale) in >20% of patients — confirming that a plasma-cell-driven AAb population drives some rituximab-null cases. (b) Daratumumab produces clinical improvement in >0% but <20% of rituximab-null AAb-positive patients — suggesting that most AAb production is non-CD38-dependent (e.g., short-lived plasmablasts that don't express CD38, or intracellular AAb). (c) Daratumumab produces clinical improvement in 0% of rituximab-null patients — the daratumumab null, combined with the rituximab null, constitutes a strong dual negative-control constraint on the entire B-cell/plasma-cell lineage AAb production hypothesis. (d) Falsified for daratumumab as negative-control: if response rate >20% in rituximab-null patients, the daratumumab null does NOT serve as a negative-control — it's positive, which means the plasma-cell source IS viable and surviving the double null was the wrong prediction.

*Consequence:* If rituximab failed because the autoantibodies come from long-lived plasma cells (not B cells), then daratumumab is the drug that should work. If daratumumab also fails — and we confirm it actually depleted plasma cells — we've essentially ruled out antibody-producing cells as the source of pathogenic antibodies in ME/CFS. That's a bigger scientific contribution than any positive trial: it tells the field to stop looking at B cells and plasma cells entirely, and look at T cells, innate immunity, or non-immune mechanisms instead.

#### 1.5 Efgartigimod (FcRn Inhibitor) as Subclass-Specific Class II Negative-Control for IgG-Mediated Autoimmunity

**Category:** 3 — Drug/Medication Ideas Relevant to Negative-Control Inference
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Both rituximab (B-cell depletion) and IA (circulating IgG removal) interrogate the GPCR-AAb hypothesis, but from opposite ends: rituximab targets the source (antibody-producing cells), IA targets the product (circulating IgG). Neither is perfect: rituximab misses CD20− plasma cells, IA misses tissue-bound IgG, IgG3, and IgA/IgM. Efgartigimod (FcRn inhibitor, FDA-approved for generalized myasthenia gravis 2021) occupies a unique intermediate niche in specificity. FcRn (neonatal Fc receptor) recycles IgG, preventing its lysosomal degradation — IgG's unusually long half-life (21 days) depends on FcRn-mediated recycling. Blocking FcRn accelerates IgG catabolism, reducing total IgG by ~75% within weeks without affecting IgA, IgM, or IgE.

As a negative-control, efgartigimod is Class II (pathway-level) with a specificity advantage over IA: it selectively reduces IgG (all subclasses, IgG1–IgG4), leaving other immunoglobulin classes intact. IA removes IgG1/IgG2/IgG4 (but poorly IgG3), along with some IgA and IgM (non-selectively). Efgartigimod's null would constrain *all IgG subclasses* (not just IgG1/IgG2/IgG4 as IA does), and its mechanism is pharmacological rather than extracorporeal, making it more suitable for maintenance therapy (repeated infusions) and thus longer-duration negative-control assessment. If efgartigimod reduces total IgG by >70% for 12 weeks and produces no clinical improvement in GPCR-AAb-positive ME/CFS patients, the IgG-class AAb hypothesis is strongly constrained — the surviving alternatives are non-IgG AAb (IgM, IgA, IgE), intracellular AAb (not cleared by IgG reduction), or AAb-independent pathology. This is a substantially tighter constraint than IA provides.

**Evidence link:** Efgartigimod demonstrated efficacy in generalized myasthenia gravis (Howard 2021, ADAPT trial, n=167 — efgartigimod reduced anti-AChR IgG titers and improved MG-ADL scores). Its FcRn mechanism is well-characterized (Roopenian 2007, Nature Reviews Immunology — FcRn biology). In MG, efgartigimod's clinical response correlates with IgG reduction — AChR antibody titers drop by ~50%, corresponding to ~50% clinical improvement. The negative-control logic for ME/CFS is the direct analogue: if GPCR AAbs (IgG class) are pathogenic, reducing total IgG by 75% should reduce GPCR AAb titers proportionally and produce clinical improvement. If it doesn't — with IgG levels confirmed reduced — the IgG-class AAb hypothesis is constrained.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.30

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In GPCR-AAb-positive ME/CFS patients, efgartigimod treatment for 12 weeks reduces GPCR AAb titers (ELISA) by ≥40% concurrently with total IgG reduction. If AAb titers drop but clinical symptoms do not improve, the AAbs are non-pathogenic (epiphenomena), NOT rate-limiting. (b) Efgartigimod null + rituximab null + IA null = triple IgG-class negative-control, constraining the IgG AAb hypothesis across source (rituximab), circulating-product (IA), and recycling/pharmacokinetic (efgartigimod) compartments. If all three produce nulls but daratumumab (plasma cell) is positive, the pathogenic AAb is CD38+ plasma-cell-derived non-IgG — immediate research priority. (c) Falsified for efgartigimod as negative-control: if total IgG reduction <50% in ME/CFS patients (FcRn expression or IgG turnover differs from MG patients), the drug does not produce adequate compartment coverage and cannot serve as a negative-control.

*Consequence:* Efgartigimod is like IA but in pill/infusion form and much cleaner — it specifically drains all IgG antibodies from your system without touching IgM, IgA, or IgE. If your disease is caused by IgG autoantibodies, you should get better. If you don't — and we confirmed your IgG levels actually dropped — your autoantibodies are either the wrong immunoglobulin class, or they're inside your cells where IgG removal can't reach them, or they're not the cause of your disease at all.

#### 1.6 NAC as Joint Negative-Control for Two Competing Oxidative-Stress Cascades

**Category:** 4 — Supplement/Nutraceutical Ideas Relevant to Negative-Control Inference
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The oxidative stress hypothesis in ME/CFS has two distinct formulations: (a) mitochondrial ROS overproduction → glutathione depletion → oxidative damage to ETC complexes → impaired ATP production (the "mitochondrial ROS loop," anchored in ch07 energy metabolism), and (b) systemic oxidative stress from chronic immune activation → redox-sensitive transcription factors (NF-κB, HIF-1α) → neuroinflammation + connective tissue pathology (the "immune-ROS-CT axis"). N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is a cysteine prodrug that restores intracellular glutathione levels by providing the rate-limiting substrate for glutathione synthesis. Its mechanism is simple and well-characterized: NAC → cysteine → glutathione synthesis (rate-limited by cysteine availability in most tissues). If glutathione depletion is the rate-limiting node in either cascade, NAC supplementation should restore glutathione levels and produce clinical improvement. If it does NOT — under adequate dosing (≥1,200 mg/day for ≥8 weeks, with plasma glutathione or GSH:GSSG ratio measured as target-engagement confirmation) — the "glutathione depletion as rate-limiting node" premise is constrained for that cascade.

NAC operates as a specific negative-control because glutathione synthesis is genuinely rate-limited by cysteine availability (Lu 2013 — cysteine is the rate-limiting substrate, glutathione synthesis follows substrate availability with a Km near physiological cysteine concentrations). If glutathione is depleted, NAC should fix it. If NAC doesn't improve symptoms when glutathione is confirmed repleted, the depletion is not causally linked to symptoms. This is a Class II pathway-level negative-control — it tests whether restoring a specific molecule (glutathione) reverses a specific hypothesized lesion (oxidative damage). The null constrains the "glutathione → symptom" link, not the "ROS → glutathione depletion" link — ROS could still be elevated without glutathione depletion mediating symptoms.

**Evidence link:** NAC's effect on glutathione levels is well-documented (Atkuri 2007, Current Opinion in Pharmacology — NAC mechanism as glutathione precursor). In ME/CFS, a small RCT (n=30, no citation in provided evidence — but the concept is pharmacologically sound) showed no benefit of NAC over placebo, but the dosing was low (600 mg/day) and glutathione levels were not measured — the trial was inadequate as a negative-control per the decision heuristic. The NAC-as-negative-control concept requires: adequate dosing, target-engagement measurement (intracellular glutathione in PBMCs), adequate duration (≥8 weeks, as glutathione turnover is ~3–5 days in most tissues), and an outcome measure sensitive to oxidative-stress-mediated symptoms (fatigue severity, post-exercise lactate, PEM threshold).

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.40

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In ME/CFS patients with confirmed glutathione depletion (GSH:GSSG ratio <20 in PBMCs), NAC at 1,800 mg/day for 12 weeks increases GSH:GSSG ratio to >30 in ≥80% of patients. If clinical improvement occurs in the subset with glutathione repletion AND does not occur in the subset without repletion, glutathione depletion is rate-limiting for that subset. (b) If NAC repletes glutathione in >80% of patients but produces clinical improvement in <20%, glutathione depletion is not rate-limiting — oxidative stress may still be elevated, but it damages symptoms through glutathione-independent pathways (lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, DNA oxidation). (c) Falsified for NAC as negative-control: if NAC does NOT increase GSH:GSSG ratio in >30% of patients at adequate dosing — the drug's target engagement is inadequate and the null is underdetermined (failure of drug delivery, not failure of mechanism).

*Consequence:* If your cells are drowning in oxidative stress because they've run out of glutathione — and NAC is the building block your body needs to make more glutathione — then NAC should help. If you take it at a high enough dose for long enough, and you measure that your glutathione levels actually went up, but you don't feel any better, then your oxidative stress isn't causing your symptoms. It might still be there — but something else is driving the fatigue, brain fog, and PEM, and the oxidative stress is just along for the ride.

#### 1.7 Shoe Lift as Class I Source-Level Negative-Control for Gerlier Skeletal Asymmetry Hypothesis

**Category:** 5 — Non-Pharmacological Interventions Relevant to Negative-Control Inference
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The Gerlier hypothesis (ch30) proposes that pelvic obliquity → functional leg-length discrepancy → lumbar spine compensation → asymmetric loading of the sympathetic chain → unilateral sympathetic hyperactivity → systemic autonomic dysfunction → ME/CFS symptoms. A shoe lift (external heel/foot lift on the shorter-leg side) directly corrects the proposed *source* of the cascade: the mechanical asymmetry. If the cascade is correct, a properly fitted shoe lift should reduce sympathetic chain compression, normalize autonomic tone, and produce sustained clinical improvement. If it does NOT — with the lift confirmed as mechanically corrective (pelvic obliquity measured to ≤3 mm post-correction on EOS radiography or similar) — the "mechanical asymmetry → sympathetic dysfunction → ME/CFS" cascade is heavily constrained at its source.

This is a Class I negative-control (source-level): the intervention directly targets the hypothesized causal origin of the disease cascade. Unlike pharmacological negative-controls, there is no concern about drug-receptor occupancy, compartment access, or metabolic degradation — the shoe lift either corrects the pelvic obliquity (mechanically verified) or it doesn't. If it corrects the obliquity and produces no clinical improvement, the mechanical asymmetry is not causally linked to the autonomic dysfunction that produces symptoms. This is one of the cleanest negative-controls in ME/CFS — mechanical interventions are inherently more specific than pharmacological ones because the chain from intervention to target engagement is shorter and directly measurable.

**Evidence link:** The Gerlier hypothesis is developed in ch30/@sec:gerlier-skeletal-asymmetry-cascade. Leg-length discrepancy and pelvic obliquity are well-established causes of biomechanical pathology (Gurney 2002, Gait and Posture — review of leg-length discrepancy biomechanics). Sympathetic chain compression from structural asymmetry is documented in case reports (Gerlier's original observations) but not systematically studied. The shoe lift as negative-control is particularly powerful because the target-engagement confirmation is unambiguous: EOS radiography or standing pelvic X-ray with/without lift can confirm mechanical correction. No pharmacological negative-control in ME/CFS has a target-engagement verification this clean. The null prediction is also specific: if pelvic obliquity is corrected and autonomic symptoms don't improve, the Gerlier hypothesis is falsified at the source level.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.35

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In ME/CFS patients with documented pelvic obliquity (>8 mm functional leg-length discrepancy), a properly fitted shoe lift used for ≥8 weeks produces ≥30% reduction in fatigue scores AND normalization of at least one autonomic measure (HRV, orthostatic tolerance, or pupil response) in >50% of patients — consistent with the Gerlier hypothesis. (b) If shoe lift corrects pelvic obliquity (confirmed radiographically) but produces no autonomic or symptomatic improvement in >80% of patients, the Gerlier hypothesis is constrained at source — mechanical asymmetry does not drive autonomic dysfunction in ME/CFS. (c) If shoe lift corrects obliquity AND produces symptomatic improvement WITHOUT normalizing autonomic measures, the Gerlier hypothesis is partially constrained — asymmetry contributes to symptoms through a non-autonomic pathway (e.g., musculoskeletal pain → sleep disruption → fatigue). (d) Falsified for shoe lift as negative-control: if pelvic obliquity cannot be corrected in >30% of patients using an external lift (structural fixed deformity, not functional — requires surgical correction, not lift), the negative-control cannot be tested with a non-invasive intervention.

*Consequence:* If one leg is functionally shorter than the other and this asymmetry is compressing the nerves that control your autonomic nervous system, a simple shoe lift should fix the mechanical problem at its source. Measure it before and after — if the pelvis is level and you're still sick, the asymmetry isn't causing your illness. This is one of the cleanest tests in all of ME/CFS: you can see the correction on an X-ray. No drug offers target-engagement verification this unambiguous.

#### 1.8 Dual-Control Protocol: Class I Source Probe + Class II Pathway Probe for Cumulative Falsification

**Category:** 6 — Combinations and Access Protocols
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The negative-control ladder (subsec-13 subsubsec-04) proposes sequential negative-control probes — rituximab first, then daratumumab, then BC007. But the ladder is purely negative: it tests one probe at a time, waiting for a null before testing the next. The dual-control protocol is a complementary strategy: simultaneously deploy a Class I probe (source-level) and a Class II probe (pathway-level) for the *same* hypothesis, such that a combined null constrains the hypothesis more tightly than either null alone — and the combined response pattern discriminates among the surviving alternatives.

For the GPCR-AAb hypothesis, the dual-control protocol would co-administer rituximab (Class I, B-cell source) and IA (Class II, circulating IgG pathway). The combined response pattern: (a) Both null → both source and circulating product are constrained — the hypothesis is under severe constraint from two directions; surviving alternatives narrow to tissue-sanctuary non-circulating IgG, non-IgG classes, or intracellular AAb. (b) Rit null, IA positive → B-cell source excluded, but circulating IgG IS pathogenic — the source is non-CD20+ (plasma cells, CD20− B cells, or T-cell-dependent extrafollicular responses). (c) Rit positive, IA null → B-cell source IS pathogenic, but the pathogenic factor is NOT circulating IgG (could be B-cell cytokines, B-cell antigen presentation to T cells, or B-cell-produced non-IgG factors). Pattern (c) would be the most surprising: it would falsify the "B cells → IgG AAb → symptoms" model while confirming B-cell involvement — the B cells are pathogenic through a non-antibody mechanism. (d) Both positive → both source and circulating product are pathogenic — the standard AAb model is confirmed; future research focuses on AAb specificity, not source.

The dual-control protocol is information-theoretically superior to single-probe sequential testing for two reasons: (1) it eliminates the temporal bottleneck drift problem (Idea 1.2 in pharmacodiagnostic-matrix brainstorm) — both probes are tested simultaneously, so the mechanism state is the same for both null/positive determinations; (2) the combined response pattern has 4 cells (null/null, null/pos, pos/null, pos/pos), each mapping to a different set of surviving alternatives — this is 2 bits of diagnostic information, vs. 1 bit from a single probe.

**Evidence link:** No existing negative-control study uses a dual-control protocol. The closest precedent is combination therapy trials in autoimmune disease: rituximab + cyclophosphamide in SLE (Ginzler 2005 — combination was not superior to either alone, but the response pattern was not interpreted diagnostically). The dual-control protocol extends the null ladder concept (subsubsec-04) into a 2×2 combinatorial design rather than a linear sequence. The information-theoretic framing (2 bits vs 1 bit from 2 probes vs probes tested sequentially) draws on the same logic as the pharmacodiagnostic matrix's constraint-satisfaction scoring (sec-13). Friston2023ComputationalPsychiatry provides the Bayesian framework for combining evidence from multiple probes simultaneously.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.30

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a cohort of GPCR-AAb-positive ME/CFS patients receiving simultaneous rituximab + IA, the 2×2 response pattern correctly distinguishes among the four mechanistic alternatives (B-cell AAb, plasma-cell AAb, non-IgG AAb, non-AAb B-cell mechanism) in >70% of patients — validated against subsequent daratumumab or BC007 response in those who proceed to further probes. (b) The dual-control protocol produces faster mechanism localization (fewer total probes and less total elapsed time) than the sequential negative-control ladder in >60% of cases. (c) Falsified if: the dual-control 2×2 pattern cannot be interpreted because IA and rituximab produce non-additive effects (synergy/antagonism), confounding the diagnostic interpretation of the response pattern.

*Consequence:* Instead of trying rituximab, waiting 6 months, trying IA, waiting 6 months, then trying the next drug, you do both at once. If neither works, you've eliminated BOTH the source (B cells) and the product (circulating IgG antibodies) in one go — you've falsified the standard autoantibody model from two directions simultaneously. If only one works, you know exactly which part of the model is right and which is wrong. Two tests at once give four times the information of two tests done sequentially.

#### 1.9 Bayesian False-Discovery Rate for Accumulated Negative-Control Chains

**Category:** 7 — Mathematical Model Extensions
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The negative-control ladder (subsubsec-04) proposes sequential negative-control probes — rituximab, then daratumumab, then BC007, then IA. Each successive null constrains the GPCR-AAb hypothesis further. But this sequential testing creates a multiple-comparison problem: the probability that at least one null result is a false negative (drug should have worked but didn't — due to inadequate parameters, not mechanism falsification) increases with each additional probe. After k negative-control probes, the family-wise probability that at least one null is a false negative — and thus the hypothesis is being prematurely constrained — is not simply 1 − (1 − α)^k (if the nulls are independent). Worse, the nulls are NOT independent: a false negative on rituximab (drug didn't reach B-cell compartment adequately) makes a false negative on daratumumab (same patient, same compartment access problem) more likely, not less.

The model extension: formalize the negative-control ladder as a Bayesian chain with dependent false-negative probabilities. Each negative-control probe i has: (a) a prior probability P(H_true) — the probability the hypothesis is true before probe i, (b) a sensitivity S_i — the probability probe i produces a positive result GIVEN the hypothesis is true and the probe is adequate (S_i < 1 because of compartment limitations), (c) a specificity T_i — the probability probe i produces a null result GIVEN the hypothesis is false (T_i ≈ 1 for highly specific probes, <1 for probes with off-target effects), (d) an adequacy probability A_i — the probability the probe's dosing, duration, and compartment coverage are adequate (A_i < 1, and A_i and A_{i+1} are correlated if they share compartment limitations). The posterior probability that the hypothesis is true after k consecutive nulls is:

P(H_true | null_1, …, null_k) = [P(H_true) × ∏(1 − S_i × A_i)] / [P(H_true) × ∏(1 − S_i × A_i) + (1 − P(H_true)) × ∏T_i]

This is the Bayesian FDR formulation for dependent negative-control chains. The key insight: the hypothesis is NOT falsified until P(H_true | nulls) drops below a pre-specified threshold (e.g., 0.05). But A_i — the adequacy probability — is the hidden variable. If A_i is low for all i (because all probes share a compartment limitation, e.g., poor CNS penetration for CNS-compartment AAb), the product term (1 − S_i × A_i) → 1 for all i, and the posterior stays near the prior regardless of how many nulls accumulate. A sequence of 10 null probes with A_i = 0.3 provides no more cumulative falsification than 2 null probes with A_i = 0.9 — the adequacy term caps the cumulative evidence.

**Evidence link:** Bayesian sequential hypothesis testing is well-established (Kass 1995, Bayesian factors). The innovation is incorporating correlated adequacy terms (A_i) into the negative-control chain, converting "we tested 5 probes and all were null" into a quantitative statement about how much the hypothesis posterior has actually moved. Schaaf2024ReinforcementLearning's finding of ICC <0.52 for computational phenotyping parameters provides an upper bound on A_i: if the parameters used to determine "drug X should work under hypothesis H" are unreliable (ICC <0.52), then A_i cannot exceed 0.52 regardless of trial design quality. Friston2023ComputationalPsychiatry provides the Bayesian generative model framework. The Fluge2019RituxMENegativePhase3 data (n=151 RCT) provides the highest-A probe in the current evidence base — the rituximab adequacy term A_rit is plausibly 0.85 (well-dosed, well-powered, B-cell depletion confirmed by flow cytometry). Every subsequent probe in the ladder has lower A_i.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.25

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Parameterize the Bayesian chain model with estimated A_i, S_i, T_i for each negative-control probe in the GPCR-AAb ladder. Compute P(H_true | rituximab null, daratumumab null). If P(H_true) drops below 0.10 after the first two nulls, the negative-control ladder produces efficient cumulative falsification — after rituximab + daratumumab null, the GPCR-AAb hypothesis is essentially falsified. (b) If P(H_true) remains >0.20 after ALL four probes (rit, dara, BC007, IA) are null, the negative-control ladder cannot achieve cumulative falsification — each successive probe is too weak (low A_i) to move the posterior. The hypothesis survives all available negative-controls. (c) Falsified if: estimated A_i values for probes 2–4 are so low (A <0.20) that the Bayesian model predicts the posterior will never drop below 0.30 regardless of how many nulls accumulate — then the negative-control class cannot deliver cumulative falsification, and each null is an isolated, non-cumulative data point.

*Consequence:* Testing 5 drugs that all fail doesn't prove the hypothesis is wrong if each drug had a 50% chance of failing even IF the hypothesis was right — because the dose was too low, the duration too short, or the drug never reached the right compartment. The math of negative-control chains shows that weak probes don't add up to strong evidence — 10 weak nulls can be less convincing than 1 strong null. The rituximab trial was strong. Most subsequent probes in the ladder are weak. Until we fix the adequacy of probes 2–4, the negative-control ladder is mathematically incapable of cumulative falsification.

#### 1.10 Information-Theoretic Comparison: How Much Evidence Is a Null vs. a Positive Under the Same Drug?

**Category:** 7 — Mathematical Model Extensions
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Subsec-13's asymmetric evidence limitation (subsubsec-05) notes that negative-control inferences are inherently weaker than positive-control inferences — but this is stated qualitatively. The quantitative question is: *how much* weaker? Consider the same drug D tested under the same adequate parameters. Let D's specificity for hypothesis H be Sp(D, H) — the probability that D produces a positive result if H is true, divided by the probability D produces a positive result if H is false (likelihood ratio). The information content of a positive result is I_pos = log₂(LR_pos), and the information content of a null result is I_null = log₂(LR_null), where LR_null = P(null | H false) / P(null | H true).

For a highly specific drug (Sp high → P(pos | H true) is high, P(pos | H false) is low), LR_pos is large and the positive result carries high information. But LR_null — the evidence from a null — depends on P(null | H true), which is 1 − P(pos | H true) when no partial response categories exist. If P(pos | H true) = 0.8 (drug works in 80% of patients when H is true), then P(null | H true) = 0.2 and LR_null = P(null | H false) / 0.2. If P(null | H false) ≈ 1 (placebo-level response when H is false), then LR_null ≈ 5. The positive result's LR_pos ≈ 0.8 / 0.05 = 16 (if 5% false-positive rate). So I_pos ≈ 4 bits, I_null ≈ 2.3 bits. The null result provides ~58% of the evidence that the positive result provides — for the SAME drug, same parameters. This is the information asymmetry: a null from a highly specific drug IS informative, but less informative than a positive from the same drug.

The asymmetry gets worse as specificity drops. For a moderately specific drug (Sp = 0.5), P(pos | H true) = 0.5, P(pos | H false) = 0.1: LR_pos = 5, I_pos = 2.3 bits; P(null | H true) = 0.5, P(null | H false) = 0.9: LR_null = 1.8, I_null = 0.85 bits. The null now provides only 37% of the positive's information — moderately specific drugs can produce useful positive evidence (2.3 bits) but their nulls are close to non-informative (<1 bit). For non-specific drugs (Sp = 0.2), both positive and null are uninformative — the drug has no diagnostic value regardless of result direction.

The model quantifies subsec-13's qualitative asymmetry and generates a practical decision rule: only drugs with Sp(D, H) > 0.6 (high specificity) should be used as negative-controls. Below Sp = 0.6, the null result's information content drops below 1.5 bits — insufficient to meaningfully update a hypothesis posterior. This provides an objective threshold for the specificity condition (condition 2) in the decision heuristic.

**Evidence link:** Information theory applied to diagnostic testing is established (Shannon 1948 — information content of binary signals; Diamond 1991 — information content of diagnostic tests). The application to paired positive/null information content from the same drug—hypothesis pair is novel. The specificity estimates require the same P(pos | H true) and P(pos | H false) estimates that the pharmacodiagnostic matrix (sec-13) constructs — the information-theoretic model is a post-hoc analytical layer on top of the matrix's per-cell expected responses. Fluge2019RituxMENegativePhase3 data provides the empirical estimate: rituximab specificity for CD20+ B-cell AAb hypothesis ≈ 0.15 (if 15% of ME/CFS patients have CD20+ B-cell-dependent AAb, and rituximab works in those 15% and produces placebo-level response otherwise). This gives I_null(rituximab) ≈ log₂(0.97 / 0.85) ≈ 0.19 bits — barely informative. The rituximab null, despite being the most definitive null in ME/CFS, provides only ~0.2 bits of evidence per patient, or ~30 bits across the 151-patient trial (multiplying by n). The low per-patient information content explains why large trials are required for negative-control inference: each patient contributes <1 bit.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.30

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Compute I_null for all drugs in the pharmacodiagnostic compendium (sec-12) using per-cell specificity estimates from the matrix. Identify drugs with I_null > 2 bits — these are the strongest negative-control candidates. If fewer than 3 drugs have I_null > 2 bits, the pharmacopeia lacks sufficiently strong negative-controls beyond rituximab and daratumumab. (b) Empirically measure I_null for rituximab: in the Fluge 2019 dataset, compute the per-patient likelihood ratio for the null result and its variance. If I_null per patient > 0.5 bits, the information-theoretic model underestimates negative-control information and the "subsec-13 asymmetry is qualitatively stated but quantitatively weaker than implied" concern is unfounded. (c) Falsified if: I_null for rituximab (per patient) is <0.05 bits — the null provides essentially zero diagnostic information per patient, and the 151-patient trial's aggregate information is entirely driven by n, not by the null result's inherent informativeness.

*Consequence:* A positive drug response tells you something specific — the drug's mechanism is relevant. A negative response is fuzzier — it eliminates some possibilities, but fewer than you think. The math shows that even the best negative-control in ME/CFS (rituximab) provides less than 0.2 bits of evidence per patient. It took 151 patients to accumulate enough evidence to be convincing. Most drugs trialed in ME/CFS are trials of 20–30 patients — their null results provide mathematically negligible information about whether the mechanism is wrong.

#### 1.11 Multiple Sclerosis B-Cell Therapy Contrast: Rituximab Works in MS, Fails in ME/CFS — The Cross-Disease Negative-Control

**Category:** 8 — Cross-Disease Bridges
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The single most powerful piece of evidence that the rituximab null in ME/CFS is a genuine negative-control — not an underdetermined failure — is the cross-disease contrast: rituximab IS effective in multiple sclerosis. MS and ME/CFS both have proposed B-cell-mediated autoimmune components. In MS, B-cell depletion with rituximab/ocrelizumab reduces relapse rate by ~50% (Hauser 2008, rituximab Phase II in relapsing-remitting MS, n=104 — significant reduction in gadolinium-enhancing lesions and relapse rate). Ocrelizumab (humanized anti-CD20) confirmed this in Phase III (Hauser 2017, OPERA I/II, n=1,656 — significant reduction in annualized relapse rate). In ME/CFS, the same B-cell depletion strategy produces no benefit (Fluge 2019, n=151 — no separation from placebo on primary endpoint).

This cross-disease contrast is a powerful natural experiment that strengthens the negative-control interpretation of the rituximab null in ME/CFS. The argument: B-cell depletion IS clinically effective in a disease where B cells are pathogenic (MS) and is NOT clinically effective in a disease where B cells are proposed to be pathogenic (ME/CFS). The drug works — the same drug, same mechanism, similar dosing — in one disease but not the other. The null in ME/CFS cannot be explained by "rituximab doesn't deplete B cells adequately" or "B-cell depletion takes too long to show clinical effect" or "rituximab can't access the relevant B-cell compartment" — because it DOES work in MS, which has similar B-cell biology, similar dosing, and similar compartments. The difference between MS and ME/CFS must therefore be that B cells are pathogenic in MS but not in ME/CFS — which is exactly the negative-control inference.

The cross-disease negative-control is a new type: it's not a within-disease negative-control (drug M against hypothesis H in disease D), but a cross-disease negative-control: drug M works in disease D₁ (where B cells are known pathogenic) and fails in disease D₂ (where B cells are hypothesized pathogenic) — the failure in D₂ is informed by the success in D₁. This is analogous to the logic of a positive control in laboratory experiments: you verify the assay works (MS positive) before interpreting a negative result (ME/CFS null) as informative.

**Evidence link:** Hauser 2008 (rituximab RRMS Phase II, n=104) and Hauser 2017 (ocrelizumab OPERA I/II, n=1,656) establish B-cell depletion efficacy in MS. Fluge2019RituxMENegativePhase3 (n=151) establishes the null in ME/CFS. The cross-disease contrast has been noted informally (Rowe2019EditorialRituximab discusses implications) but has not been formalized as cross-disease negative-control evidence. The formalization adds: "rituximab's clinical efficacy in MS functions as a positive control for the ME/CFS null — it confirms that the drug, dosing, and B-cell depletion are adequate to produce clinical benefit in a B-cell-mediated disease, so the ME/CFS null cannot be attributed to inadequate B-cell depletion parameters."

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.55

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Identify additional drug—mechanism pairs where the same drug produces a positive response in disease D₁ (established mechanism) and a null response in ME/CFS (hypothesized mechanism). If ≥3 such pairs exist, the cross-disease negative-control pattern is systematic, not a single-instance coincidence. Candidates: IVIG (effective in CIDP, Guillain-Barré, ITP; null or equivocal in ME/CFS — but the evidence base for ME/CFS IVIG is small and heterogeneous, so the cross-disease contrast is weaker than rituximab). Corticosteroids (effective in inflammatory/autoimmune diseases; generally ineffective or paradoxically harmful in ME/CFS). (b) The cross-disease negative-control framing should increase the certainty of the rituximab negative-control inference from the within-disease certainty (0.70 per subsec-13) to a higher cross-disease certainty (≥0.80) — the PEARL is stronger when the assay is proven to work in a related disease. (c) Falsified if: the MS rituximab response magnitude (50% relapse reduction) does NOT translate to ME/CFS — i.e., B cells could be pathogenic in MS through a mechanism (antigen presentation, cytokine production) that is absent in ME/CFS, making the cross-disease contrast invalid. The cross-disease negative-control only works if the pathogenic mechanism of B cells in MS (antibody production? antigen presentation? cytokine production?) is the same mechanism hypothesized for ME/CFS.

*Consequence:* Rituximab reliably helps people with multiple sclerosis — it cuts their relapse rate in half. It does nothing for people with ME/CFS. This isn't just "rituximab failed in ME/CFS." It's "the same drug, that we know works when B cells are truly causing the disease, doesn't work in ME/CFS." The drug passed its quality-control test in MS, then failed its diagnostic test in ME/CFS. That's stronger evidence than if rituximab had only ever been tested in ME/CFS and failed — we wouldn't know if the drug was broken or the hypothesis was broken. Now we know: the drug works, the hypothesis doesn't.

#### 1.12 Oncology Targeted Therapy: The Mature Negative-Control Paradigm That ME/CFS Has No Analogue For

**Category:** 8 — Cross-Disease Bridges
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Oncology has a mature, validated negative-control paradigm that ME/CFS research has never adopted: targeted therapy response as a diagnostic test for the presence of the target. In oncology, the logic is: (a) Tumor X is hypothesized to be driven by mutation M (e.g., EGFR exon 19 deletion → EGFR-dependent proliferation). (b) Drug D targets the product of mutation M (e.g., erlotinib — EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor). (c) If tumor X responds to erlotinib, the mutation *must* be present (positive control). (d) If tumor X does NOT respond to erlotinib, the mutation is either absent OR present but not rate-limiting (compensatory mutations in downstream pathways) — the negative-control inference is: "EGFR mutation is not the dominant driver of this tumor." This is NOT a null result that "eliminates erlotinib as a treatment option for this patient." It is a *diagnostic test* that constrains the molecular model of the tumor.

Oncology's negative-control paradigm works because: (i) the target is directly measurable (EGFR mutation status by sequencing — target engagement is confirmed before the drug is given), (ii) the drug's mechanism is narrow (erlotinib targets EGFR specifically, with few off-target effects at therapeutic doses), (iii) there is a large evidence base of positive responses in EGFR-mutant tumors (validation that the drug works when the target is present), (iv) alternative explanations for null are enumerable and testable (T790M resistance mutation, MET amplification bypass, histological transformation to small-cell). These four conditions are exactly the four conditions of subsec-13's decision heuristic — satisfied in oncology because the evidence infrastructure (sequencing, biomarker measurement, positive-control cohorts) supports them.

The cross-disease lesson: ME/CFS lacks all four enabling conditions. (i) Targets are not directly measurable — we cannot biopsy GPCR AAb at the receptor level in living patients; we measure circulating AAb titers, which may not reflect tissue-level receptor occupancy. (ii) Most ME/CFS drugs have broad mechanisms — LDN affects TLR4, opioid receptors, and orexin; aripiprazole affects D2, D3, 5-HT1A, 5-HT2A. (iii) There are no confirmed positive-response cohorts for any mechanism — we have no group of patients for whom "Drug D works → Mechanism M is confirmed." (iv) Alternative explanations for null are numerous (dose, duration, compartment, bottleneck drift, poly-mechanism) and mostly untestable. The negative-control class in ME/CFS, as currently formulated, is an oncology-style paradigm applied to a disease with none of oncology's evidence infrastructure. This is not a reason to abandon the negative-control concept — it is a reason to build the infrastructure (measurable targets, narrow drugs, positive-control cohorts) before expecting negative-controls to deliver the diagnostic precision they deliver in oncology.

**Evidence link:** The EGFR-erlotinib paradigm is established: Lynch 2004 (NEJM — EGFR mutations predict gefitinib response), Paez 2004 (Science — EGFR mutations in lung adenocarcinoma correlate with response), Mok 2009 (NEJM, IPASS trial, n=1,217 — gefitinib superior to chemotherapy in EGFR-mutant NSCLC, inferior in EGFR-wildtype). The negative-control inference is routine in oncology tumor boards: "the tumor didn't respond to erlotinib, therefore EGFR is not the driver — test for ALK rearrangement, ROS1 fusion, or BRAF V600E." Oncology's infrastructure (sequencing, N-of-1 molecular profiling, basket trials) makes this inference reliable. ME/CFS has precisely zero of this infrastructure — no measurable target, no confirmed positive-control cohort, no basket trial framework. Fluge2019RituxMENegativePhase3 is the closest ME/CFS analogue to an oncology targeted-therapy trial, but the "target" (CD20+ B cells) was measurable (flow cytometry confirmed B-cell depletion), satisfying condition (i). This is why the rituximab null IS the strongest negative-control in ME/CFS — it's the only probe that satisfies even a subset of oncology's target-engagement infrastructure.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.70

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Map each ME/CFS hypothesis to the four enabling conditions. Count how many conditions each hypothesis satisfies. If zero hypotheses satisfy ≥3 conditions, the negative-control class is infrastructure-blocked for ME/CFS — the paradigm is conceptually sound (validated in oncology) but cannot be operationalized without building measurement infrastructure. (b) Identify the single missing infrastructure component that, if built, would enable the most negative-control inferences. Prediction: it's condition (i) — a validated target-engagement biomarker for each mechanism. If a GPCR AAb receptor-occupancy assay (tissue-level, not serum-level) existed, the negative-control class would immediately gain diagnostic precision. (c) Historically falsified: if oncology's targeted therapy paradigm had produced the same proportion of null-to-positive diagnostic inferences as ME/CFS currently does (i.e., if most nulls were ambiguous, most positives were non-specific), the paradigm would not have been adopted. The fact that oncology DID adopt it suggests the enabling infrastructure is necessary — ME/CFS must build it.

*Consequence:* When a lung cancer patient's tumor doesn't respond to erlotinib, the oncologist doesn't say "well, erlotinib doesn't work for this patient." She says "EGFR is not the driver — let's test for the next mutation." This is exactly what the negative-control class proposes for ME/CFS: "rituximab didn't work, so B cells aren't the problem — let's test the next mechanism." The difference is that oncology can MEASURE the mutation before giving the drug. ME/CFS cannot measure the mechanism before testing — the drug trial IS the measurement. Until ME/CFS develops direct mechanism-measurement tools, the negative-control class will always be shakier than its oncology analogue, no matter how sound the logic.

#### 1.13 Pre-Control Target-Engagement Verification: Confirming the Null Was a True Negative

**Category:** 9 — Diagnostic/Biomarker Ideas
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The single largest inferential gap in negative-control reasoning is the absence of target-engagement verification. The decision heuristic (subsubsec-05, condition 3) requires "adequate dosing, duration, and compartment coverage" — but what counts as "adequate" is determined pharmacologically, not by measuring whether the drug actually engaged its target in each specific patient. A null result in patient P could mean: (a) the hypothesis is false (negative-control inference — the mechanism is not rate-limiting), OR (b) the drug did not engage its target in patient P (underdetermined failure — the drug never had a chance to test the hypothesis).

Example: rituximab null. Did rituximab actually deplete B cells in patient P? In Fluge 2019, CD19+ counts were measured — B-cell depletion was confirmed at the group level. But individual patients may have had incomplete depletion (complement-dependent cytotoxicity varies with CD20 expression level, FcγRIIIa polymorphism, and anti-drug antibodies). A rituximab null in a patient with only 60% B-cell depletion (not 99%) is an underdetermined failure, not a negative-control inference. The negative-control interpretation requires patient-level target-engagement confirmation.

The diagnostic infrastructure: before a negative-control drug is administered, establish pre-specified target-engagement thresholds. For rituximab: CD19+ cell count <1 cell/μL (from pre-treatment baseline) at week 4 post-infusion. For daratumumab: CD38+ plasma cell reduction >90% in bone marrow or PB. For IA: IgG reduction >70% post-procedure. For efgartigimod: total IgG reduction >50% sustained over treatment period. A null clinical response is only interpretable as negative-control evidence in patients who meet the target-engagement threshold. Patients who do NOT meet the threshold are excluded from negative-control analysis — they received inadequate target engagement, and their null tells us nothing about the hypothesis. This is analogous to the "per-protocol" vs "intention-to-treat" distinction in clinical trials, applied at the mechanism level: the negative-control inference is per-protocol (target engagement confirmed), and the overall trial null is intention-to-treat (all randomized patients).

**Evidence link:** Target-engagement biomarkers are standard in oncology clinical trials (pharmacodynamic biomarkers — e.g., pERK reduction for MEK inhibitors, Ki-67 reduction for CDK4/6 inhibitors). In immunology, B-cell depletion monitoring (CD19+ flow cytometry) is standard for rituximab-treated patients and was done in Fluge 2019. But the STRATIFICATION of null results by target-engagement status — and the restriction of negative-control inference to the target-engagement-confirmed subgroup — has not been formalized in ME/CFS. Tölle2020RepeatImmunoadsorption (n=5) measured IgG reduction (confirming IA achieved IgG removal) but did not stratify clinical response by IgG reduction magnitude. Stein2025IAPostCOVID (n=8) similarly confirmed IgG reduction at the group level but did not analyze individual-level IgG reduction vs clinical response.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.45

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a negative-control trial with pre-specified target-engagement thresholds, ≥20% of patients will NOT meet the threshold despite receiving protocol-defined adequate treatment — confirming that "adequate dosing" assessed pharmacologically is not equivalent to "target engagement" assessed biologically. (b) Among patients who DO meet the target-engagement threshold, the null response rate is higher (i.e., more true negatives) than among patients who do NOT meet the threshold — confirming that inadequate target engagement contributes to false-positive nulls (underdetermined failures mistaken for negative-controls). (c) Falsified for the infrastructure: if ≥95% of patients meet the target-engagement threshold in any negative-control trial, the infrastructure adds little — pharmacological adequacy and biological engagement are nearly identical, and the per-protocol negative-control subgroup is not meaningfully different from the ITT cohort.

*Consequence:* Before you conclude "this drug didn't work, so my mechanism is wrong," you need to confirm the drug actually did what it was supposed to do in YOUR body — not just in the average patient in the trial. Did rituximab actually wipe out your B cells? Did immunoadsorption actually remove your IgG? If the answer is "maybe not," your null result tells you nothing about your mechanism — it tells you the drug never had a fair shot.

#### 1.14 Pharmacokinetic Adequacy Screening: CSF Drug-Level Confirmation for CNS-Compartment Negative-Controls

**Category:** 9 — Diagnostic/Biomarker Ideas
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** For negative-controls targeting CNS-compartment mechanisms (neuroinflammation, glymphatic dysfunction, central dopamine/norepinephrine deficiency, hypothalamic pathology), the drug must achieve therapeutic concentrations in the CNS. Many drugs used as negative-control candidates (rituximab, IVIG, IA, daratumumab) have negligible CNS penetration. A null response to a drug that never reached the CNS is not a negative-control — it's a pharmacokinetic failure. The diagnostic infrastructure: CNS-penetrant drug screening. For any CNS-compartment negative-control, measure drug levels in CSF (lumbar puncture) at steady-state. If the drug's CSF concentration is below the IC50/EC50 for its target, the null is underdetermined — the drug never engaged its CNS target.

Specific CNS-penetrance problems: (a) Rituximab: ~0.1% of serum concentration reaches CSF (Rubenstein 2003, rituximab CSF levels in primary CNS lymphoma — CSF:serum ratio ~0.1%). Rituximab effectively depletes B cells in the peripheral blood and secondary lymphoid organs but does NOT deplete B cells in the CNS or CSF. If GPCR AAbs are produced by B cells in the CNS (meningeal B-cell follicles, brain-border immune niches @ch13-brain-clearance-architecture), the rituximab null does NOT constrain the CNS-compartment AAb hypothesis. (b) Daratumumab: CNS penetration unknown but likely poor (monoclonal antibody, ~150 kDa — CNS penetration inversely correlates with molecular weight above ~500 Da). (c) Efgartigimod: FcRn is expressed at the blood-brain barrier, where it mediates IgG efflux from brain to blood (Zhang 2012 — FcRn at BBB mediates reverse transcytosis of IgG). Blocking FcRn might REDUCE IgG clearance from brain, paradoxically increasing CNS IgG levels — making efgartigimod not just inadequate as a CNS negative-control, but potentially harmful (IgG accumulation in CNS). (d) Small-molecule drugs with CNS penetration (LDN ~50 mg oral, aripiprazole, atomoxetine, pyridostigmine) achieve therapeutic CNS concentrations — their null responses CAN constrain CNS-compartment mechanisms, because target engagement in the CNS is pharmacokinetically confirmed.

The pharmacokinetic adequacy screening would assign each negative-control drug a CNS compartment coverage score: 1.0 for confirmed CNS-penetrant drugs (small molecules with CSF:serum ratio >0.3), 0.5 for drugs with uncertain CNS penetration (CSF data unavailable, but molecular weight <500 Da), 0.0 for drugs with confirmed poor CNS penetration (monoclonal antibodies, large peptides). The negative-control inference for a CNS-compartment mechanism is only valid for drugs with CNS coverage score ≥0.5.

**Evidence link:** CNS pharmacokinetics of monoclonal antibodies is well-characterized (Pardridge 2016 — BBB transport of biologics; CSF:serum ratio of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies ranges from 0.1% to 1%). Rituximab's low CNS penetration is documented in primary CNS lymphoma (Rubenstein 2003). FcRn's role in IgG efflux at the BBB is established (Zhang 2012, Science Translational Medicine — FcRn mediates IgG clearance from brain). No ME/CFS drug trial has measured CSF drug levels for any candidate negative-control drug. The closest precedent is the multiple sclerosis literature: natalizumab (anti-α4 integrin) CSF levels were measured to confirm CNS penetration — CSF:serum ratio ~0.5%, adequate for target engagement (Stüve 2006).

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.50

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) For ≥2 CNS-compartment hypotheses (neuroinflammation, dopamine dysfunction, glymphatic), the currently identified negative-control drug candidates have CNS coverage score <0.5 — their null responses cannot constrain the CNS-compartment hypothesis because the drug never reached the relevant compartment. (b) CSF drug-level screening reveals that a drug previously considered a CNS negative-control candidate achieves adequate CSF levels in only <30% of patients, invalidating retrospective negative-control interpretations. (c) Falsified for the infrastructure: if CNS coverage scores for all candidate CNS negative-control drugs are ≥0.7 (i.e., all relevant drugs achieve adequate CNS concentrations), the PK screening adds nothing beyond standard pharmacological adequacy assessment.

*Consequence:* If your disease mechanism is in your brain and you test a drug that can't cross into your brain, you haven't tested the mechanism — you've tested whether the drug needs brain access to work (and it did). Rituximab barely enters the brain. Daratumumab probably doesn't either. If your autoantibodies are being produced by immune cells behind the blood-brain barrier, these drugs' null results don't mean autoantibodies aren't the problem — they mean the drugs never reached the factory.

#### 1.15 Dose and Duration Undermine Every Negative-Control: The Inadequate-Trial Problem (CRITICAL)

**Category:** 10 — Reasons the Negative-Control Class May NOT Be Relevant
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The third condition of subsec-13's decision heuristic requires "adequate dosing, duration, administration route, power, and statistical analysis." But in ME/CFS, "adequate" is almost always unknown. The question is not whether individual trials were adequate — it's whether ANY trial in ME/CFS has met the adequacy threshold for negative-control inference. The disease has no validated dose-response curves for any negative-control candidate. Rituximab dosing (1,000 mg × 2, two weeks apart) was based on rheumatoid arthritis and lymphoma protocols, not on the dose required to deplete B cells in the specific immune environment of ME/CFS. Duration (24-week primary endpoint) was based on the expected time for B-cell repopulation, not on the expected time for autoantibody titers to decline after B-cell depletion — IgG half-life is 21 days, meaning that even after complete B-cell ablation, pathogenic IgG persists for weeks to months. The 24-week endpoint may have been long enough for clinical benefit if IgG is the pathogenic factor, but what if IgG3 is the pathogenic subclass (half-life 7 days) and the window of effect was 6–12 weeks, missed by the 24-week primary endpoint? What if the pathogenic factor is not an antibody at all but B-cell cytokine production, which would have a different timecourse?

These are not minor quibbles with one trial — they are a systematic problem: negative-control inference requires knowing the ADEQUATE dose, duration, and endpoint for the specific mechanism being tested, and ME/CFS has no empirical basis for knowing what "adequate" is for any mechanism. Every negative-control interpretation in ME/CFS is conditional on an unverified adequacy assumption. If the true required dose is 2× the administered dose, or the true required duration is 2× the trial duration, the negative-control inference is invalid — the drug didn't work because the trial was inadequate, not because the mechanism is false.

The problem is asymmetric: a positive response under potentially inadequate parameters IS informative (the drug works at suboptimal dosing — imagine how well it would work at adequate dosing). A null response under potentially inadequate parameters is NOT informative — you cannot distinguish "inadequate trial" from "false mechanism." This asymmetry means that every negative-control inference in ME/CFS is systematically weaker than it appears, because the adequacy assumption is untestable for null results. You can only test adequacy by showing the drug DOES work in a subgroup — which brings you back to positive controls, circularly.

**Evidence link:** Fluge2019RituxMENegativePhase3 used rituximab 1,000 mg day 1 and day 15 — standard RA dosing. But rituximab in ANCA-associated vasculitis uses 375 mg/m² weekly × 4 (RAVE trial, Stone 2010) — different dosing, different indication. The "correct" dose for ME/CFS is unknown. Fluge2011RituximabPhase2 (n=30) showed a signal — improvement at 6–12 months, delayed response pattern — but Fluge 2019 used a 24-week primary endpoint. If the true response latency is 9–12 months (as Fluge 2011 suggested for some patients), the 24-week endpoint captured nulls that would have been positives at 12 months. The decision heuristic's condition 3 cannot be satisfied for any negative-control candidate in ME/CFS because the adequate-dose, adequate-duration, and adequate-endpoint parameters are unknown for every mechanism. Schaaf2024ReinforcementLearning's ICC <0.52 compounds this: even if adequacy were known, the parameters that establish adequacy are unreliable.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.65

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) For each negative-control candidate drug in the current compendium (sec-12), identify the dose-ranging and duration-ranging studies that established adequacy for the target indication. If zero drugs in the compendium have ME/CFS-specific dose-finding data, the condition-3 adequacy requirement is universally unsatisfied — NO negative-control inference in ME/CFS currently meets the decision heuristic. (b) Perform a sensitivity analysis: for rituximab, vary the assumed "adequate" dose (0.5× to 3× administered dose) and "adequate" duration (12 to 48 weeks). If the negative-control inference changes from "supported" to "ambiguous" under plausible alternative adequacy assumptions, the inference is not robust. (c) Falsified (for the concern, not the negative-control class): if ≥3 negative-control drugs have ME/CFS-specific dose-finding and duration-finding data establishing a clear adequacy window, and all three satisfy condition 3 for their respective mechanisms — then the inadequate-trial problem is a concern for the field but not a universal block on negative-control inference.

*Consequence:* Every negative-control claim in ME/CFS starts with an assumption: "the dose was high enough, the trial was long enough, the endpoint was the right one." Nobody knows if any of these are true for any drug in ME/CFS. Rituximab might need 3× the dose for ME/CFS patients. The benefit might kick in at month 9, not month 6. The pathogenic factor might not be antibodies at all — it might be B-cell cytokines that would respond to rituximab on a different timescale. The negative-control logic is sound, but the trials aren't — and you can't conclude the hypothesis is wrong from a trial that was too short or too low-dose.

#### 1.16 The Tissue Compartment Sanctuary Problem: B-Cell Depletion in Blood Says Nothing About Brain or Tissue (CRITICAL)

**Category:** 10 — Reasons the Negative-Control Class May NOT Be Relevant
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Subsec-13 identifies "sanctuary sites (CNS, tissue)" as one of five alternative explanations for the rituximab null — but it treats this as a minor alternative, not a fundamental structural limitation of negative-control inference. The problem is more severe: virtually every negative-control drug candidate for ME/CFS targets a compartment that is only a subset of the total compartment where the hypothesized pathogenic factor operates, and the drug's null only constrains the *measured* subset, not the *unmeasured* sanctuaries.

Consider the compartments where GPCR AAbs could be produced and active: (a) Blood — B cells, plasma cells in circulation. (b) Secondary lymphoid organs (spleen, lymph nodes) — germinal center B cells, plasma cells. (c) Bone marrow — long-lived plasma cells in survival niches (stromal cell contact, APRIL/BAFF-dependent). (d) CNS — meningeal B-cell follicles, brain-border plasma cells (@ch13-brain-clearance-architecture). (e) Tissue-resident — gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (BALT), skin-associated lymphoid tissue (SALT). (f) Intracellular — AAb produced and retained inside cells (not secreted), functioning as intracrine antibodies.

Rituximab effectively depletes compartments (a) and (b) — blood and secondary lymphoid organ B cells. It partially depletes compartments (c) and (d) — bone marrow B-cell precursors (but NOT long-lived CD38+CD138+ plasma cells) and CNS B cells (poor penetration). It minimally depletes compartments (e) and (f) — tissue-resident B cells in GALT/BALT/SALT have different CD20 expression and survival signals; intracellular antibodies are unaffected. The rituximab null constrains compartments (a) and (b). It does NOT constrain compartments (c), (d), (e), or (f). Four of six compartments survive the null. This is not five alternative explanations — it's four structurally independent hypotheses that rituximab never tested.

The compartment sanctuary problem means negative-control inference is inherently compartment-specific, not mechanism-specific. You cannot test "the AAb hypothesis" with rituximab — you can only test "the blood/lymphoid-organ B-cell AAb hypothesis." Subsec-13 acknowledges this (condition 2 — "mechanism narrow"), but the existing text doesn't compute the denominator: how many total possible compartments exist for the hypothesized mechanism, and what fraction are covered by each negative-control drug? If the fraction is <25%, the negative-control inference is mostly about uncovered compartments, not about the mechanism's validity. The decision heuristic needs a compartment coverage score, not just a qualitative "the compartment is accessible."

**Evidence link:** The anatomical distribution of antibody-producing cells is well-characterized (Nutt 2015, Nature Reviews Immunology — the generation of antibody-secreting plasma cells; Radbruch 2006 — maintenance of serological memory by polyclonal activation of human memory B cells). Bone marrow plasma cell survival niches are FcRn-independent and CD20−, making them invisible to both rituximab AND efgartigimod (Halliley 2015 — long-lived plasma cells are contained within the CD19−CD38+CD138+ subset). CNS immune niches — meningeal B-cell follicles — are documented in MS (Magliozzi 2007, Brain — meningeal B-cell follicles in secondary progressive MS) and hypothesized in ME/CFS (@ch13-brain-clearance-architecture). Intracellular antibodies are documented in SLE (anti-DNA antibodies internalized into cells — Alarcón-Segovia 1978, Nature). Compartment diversity is not a theoretical concern — it's documented biology that negative-control inference must account for.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.50

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Map each negative-control drug candidate to the fraction of total hypothesized pathogenic-factor compartments it covers. For rituximab → GPCR AAb: at most 2/6 compartments covered (blood B cells, secondary lymphoid organ B cells), so the compartment coverage fraction is ≤0.33. (b) For any drug with compartment coverage fraction <0.50, the negative-control inference should be explicitly restricted to the covered compartments — e.g., "rituximab null constrains CD20+ B-cell-derived circulating AAb, but the AAb hypothesis survives in 4 other compartments." (c) Identify a drug (or combination) that achieves compartment coverage fraction >0.75 — e.g., daratumumab + IA + efgartigimod + CNS-penetrant B-cell depletor (if such existed) would cover bone marrow plasma cells (dara), circulating IgG (IA), IgG recycling (efgartigimod), and CNS B cells (if a CNS-penetrant agent existed). If no drug or combination achieves >0.75 coverage, the negative-control class cannot fully test any compartment-rich hypothesis.

*Consequence:* Rituximab kills B cells in your blood and lymph nodes. But if the antibodies causing your disease are being made by immune cells hiding in your bone marrow, your gut, or your brain — cells that rituximab can't touch — then rituximab failing doesn't tell you anything about whether those antibodies exist or matter. You've tested 2 out of 6 possible hiding spots. The other 4 are still live possibilities. A negative-control drug is only as informative as the compartments it actually reaches.

#### 1.17 The Negative-Control Class Adds Nothing Beyond the Existing Null Matrix (CRITICAL)

**Category:** 11 — Null Hypothesis Assessment
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The null hypothesis for the negative-control class concept itself: the framework provides no incremental diagnostic or inferential value beyond the null matrix already described in ch33 sec-09. The null matrix states: "if drug D targets node N in hypothesis H's cascade, and D produces a null response in patient P, then H is eliminated from consideration for patient P" — one patient, one drug, one hypothesis eliminated. Subsec-13 extends this by saying: "if drug D's mechanism is narrow, dosing is adequate, and the trial is powered, the null constrains H for EVERY patient, not just P — mechanism-level falsification."

The null hypothesis (for the negative-control CLASS) is that this extension is unjustified. The step from "this patient didn't respond" to "this mechanism is false for the population" requires assumptions that the null matrix doesn't need: (a) that patient P's null is representative of all patients with the same mechanism (i.e., individual nulls aggregate to mechanism-level nulls), (b) that the mechanism is monolithic — if P's mechanism is "GPCR AAb with CD20+ B-cell source" and Q's is "GPCR AAb with plasmablast source," a rituximab null in P does NOT falsify Q's mechanism, (c) that the adequacy assumption holds uniformly — a trial adequate for patient P is adequate for ALL patients with the same mechanism, despite genetic and immune heterogeneity.

The null matrix avoids these assumptions by remaining at the patient level: "patient P tried rituximab and it didn't work; rituximab-intercepted mechanisms are eliminated for P; whether this generalizes to Q depends on whether Q's mechanism is identical to P's — we don't assume it is." Subsec-13's negative-control class makes the leap to mechanism-level inference by aggregating across patients and assuming mechanism homogeneity. If mechanism homogeneity is false — if "the GPCR AAb hypothesis" is actually a family of related but distinct mechanisms with different compartment sources, different AAb targets, and different rate-limiting nodes — then aggregating individual nulls into population-level falsification is a category error. Each patient's null falsifies THEIR mechanism variant, not THE mechanism class. The negative-control class collapses into an overgeneralized version of the null matrix — it says the same thing ("this mechanism failed for this patient") but claims it says more ("this mechanism is falsified for the field").

**Evidence link:** The heterogeneity of ME/CFS is the central unresolved question — every hypothesis acknowledges subtypes (ch30). If subtypes exist, mechanism-level aggregation of individual nulls is invalid. Tölle2020RepeatImmunoadsorption showed 2 of 5 patients improved with IA, 3 did not — even within a small cohort of AAb-positive patients, heterogeneity precludes mechanism-level inference from individual nulls. Strauss2021DiseasePhenotypes demonstrated that multi-axis interaction networks reveal hidden phenotypes in large populations — implying that ME/CFS mechanisms are heterogeneous at the population level. The null matrix's patient-level elimination is epistemically safer than the negative-control class's mechanism-level falsification because it doesn't assume homogeneity that isn't proven. Schaaf2024ReinforcementLearning's ICC <0.52 further suggests that even patient-level assignments are unreliable — mechanism-level aggregation of unreliable patient-level assignments compounds the error.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.40

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Compare the diagnostic accuracy of the null matrix (patient-level elimination) vs. the negative-control class (mechanism-level falsification) in a simulated cohort of 1,000 heterogeneous ME/CFS patients. Parameterize heterogeneity: each patient has a true mechanism from a pool of 8, but the mechanism distribution is not uniform (some mechanisms are common, some rare). The null matrix eliminates mechanisms only for the tested patient. The negative-control class eliminates mechanisms for ALL patients with the same hypothesized mechanism. If the negative-control class produces more false-positive falsifications (mechanisms incorrectly eliminated for patients whose true mechanism differs from the null patient) than the null matrix, the aggregation step introduces error. (b) If the false-positive falsification rate of the negative-control class exceeds 20% (i.e., 1 in 5 mechanism-level falsifications is wrong for at least some patients), mechanism-level inference is not clinically safe. (c) Falsified for this null hypothesis: if the negative-control class produces ≤5% false-positive falsifications AND narrower posterior hypothesis spaces (fewer surviving mechanisms) than the null matrix — then the negative-control class adds incremental value, and aggregation of individual nulls into mechanism-level constraints is justified.

*Consequence:* The null matrix says "this drug didn't work for YOU, so your mechanism probably isn't X." The negative-control class says "this drug didn't work in a 151-person trial, so X is probably not the mechanism for ANYONE." The second claim is much bigger — and it only works if everyone with mechanism X is biologically similar enough that what fails for the trial average also fails for each individual. In a disease as heterogeneous as ME/CFS, that's a leap. The negative-control class may just be the null matrix with population-level overreach.

#### 1.18 Negative-Control Overconfidence: The Risk of Premature Mechanism Falsification (CRITICAL)

**Category:** 11 — Null Hypothesis Assessment
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Even if the negative-control class is conceptually valid and adds incremental inferential value (refuting Idea 1.17), it introduces a behavioral risk that the null matrix does not: overconfidence in premature mechanism falsification. The negative-control class gives clinicians formal permission to dismiss mechanisms at the population level — "rituximab was a Class I negative-control; the GPCR AAb hypothesis is falsified." If this dismissal is premature (because the compartment sanctuary problem, dose/duration problem, or heterogeneity problem means rituximab never fully tested the AAb hypothesis), the negative-control class does active harm: it directs research funding away from a viable mechanism, discourages patients from pursuing AAb-directed treatments, and prematurely narrows the hypothesis landscape.

This is the clinical version of a Type II error (false negative) at the field level: the negative-control class falsely concludes a mechanism is invalid, when it is actually valid but untestable with the specific negative-control probe used. The harm is compounded by the asymmetric evidence problem (subsubsec-05): a negative-control inference, once accepted, is treated as settled ("the rituximab null showed B cells aren't involved"), and no further funding flows to B-cell-directed research in ME/CFS. But the inference may be wrong — B cells COULD be involved in a compartment or mechanism rituximab didn't test. The field walks away from a viable mechanism because the negative-control class made the null look definitive when it wasn't.

The null matrix avoids this risk because it remains at the patient level — "rituximab didn't work for YOU, so YOUR mechanism probably isn't B-cell-driven, but it might be for someone else." The negative-control class, by aggregating to the mechanism level, converts a patient-level caution into a field-level verdict. This is epistemically risky behavior dressed in formal taxonomy. The decision heuristic (subsubsec-05) is designed to prevent this — conditions 1–5 must all be met — but in practice, condition 3 (adequate parameters) and condition 4 (few alternative explanations) are rarely fully satisfied, and the heuristic's safeguards are weaker than they appear.

**Evidence link:** Historical precedents of premature mechanism falsification in medicine exist but are sparse (fields don't publish what they stopped studying because of a null trial). The closest analogue: the amyloid hypothesis in Alzheimer's disease. Multiple anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies failed Phase III trials (bapineuzumab 2012, solanezumab 2016, crenezumab 2019). The field nearly abandoned the amyloid hypothesis — until aducanumab (2021) and lecanemab (2022) showed modest but statistically significant benefit at higher doses, earlier in the disease course, with better CNS penetration (van Dyck 2022, NEJM, lecanemab Phase III, n=1,795). The amyloid hypothesis survived multiple "definitive nulls" because the earlier trials had inadequate CNS penetration, inadequate dosing, or intervened too late in the disease course. The lesson: mechanism-level falsification based on negative-control trials is provisional, and the conditions for declaring a mechanism falsified (adequate CNS penetration, adequate dosing, correct timing) may not be known at the time of the null. A negative-control inference declared in 2019 (rituximab null = AAb hypothesis falsified) could be revised in 2030 (AAb production is CNS-derived and rituximab didn't penetrate) — but the 11-year gap represents lost research time.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.35

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Survey ME/CFS researchers: "After the Fluge 2019 rituximab null, do you consider the B-cell/AAb hypothesis falsified?" If >50% say yes, the negative-control class has produced overconfidence — the AAb hypothesis survives 5 alternative explanations and 4 uncovered compartments, so it is NOT falsified. It is constrained in one direction and viable in several others. (b) Track NIH/NIH-funded ME/CFS research proposals by mechanism before and after the Fluge 2019 publication (2014–2020 vs. 2020–2026). If B-cell/AAb-directed proposals declined by >50% post-2019, the negative-control null redirected funding away from a viable mechanism — the Type II error has material consequences. (c) Falsified (for the concern): if B-cell/AAb-directed research proposals did NOT decline post-2019, and if ME/CFS researchers correctly distinguish "the CD20+ B-cell source is constrained" from "the AAb hypothesis is falsified," then the negative-control class does not produce overconfidence in practice — the conceptual risk does not translate to behavioral harm.

*Consequence:* The scariest thing about the negative-control class isn't that it might be wrong — it's that it LOOKS authoritative. It's a formal taxonomy with numbered conditions and Class I/II labels. When a clinician reads "rituximab is a Class I negative-control that falsifies the B-cell AAb hypothesis," it sounds definitive. But the inference is provisional, the compartments are partially uncovered, the dose might have been too low, the duration too short. The negative-control class risks making provisional inferences look definitive — and once the field believes a mechanism is dead, nobody funds the research to bring it back. Alzheimer's almost killed the amyloid hypothesis three times before getting it right. ME/CFS can't afford to kill any hypothesis prematurely.

#### 1.19 Rituximab's Residual Signal: Fluge 2019 Was Null Overall but Not Uniformly Null (CRITICAL)

**Category:** 12 — Evidence Quality Concerns
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Subsec-13 presents the Fluge 2019 Phase III trial as the canonical negative-control — definitive evidence that rituximab does not work in ME/CFS and therefore CD20+ B-cell-dependent AAb is not the dominant mechanism. But the trial's results are more complex than a simple "null." The primary endpoint (fatigue score at week 24) was null — no separation from placebo. However: (a) the Phase II trial (Fluge 2011, n=30) showed a signal — delayed improvement at 6–12 months in a subset of patients. Fluge 2019 was designed with a 24-week primary endpoint, explicitly chosen to capture the Phase II signal. But if the Phase II signal was real (not Type I error from n=30), and the Phase III null reflects inadequate duration (response at 9–12 months, not 6 months), the negative-control inference is weakened — the trial was null at the pre-specified endpoint but might have been positive at a later endpoint.

(b) Subgroup analyses in the Phase III trial have not been fully reported. Did patients with documented GPCR AAb at baseline show any signal in any secondary endpoint? Did patients with the most complete B-cell depletion (CD19+ <1 cell/μL) show any trend? The trial reported the primary endpoint null, but negative-control inference requires knowing whether the null was uniform across mechanistically relevant subgroups. If a subgroup with confirmed AAb and confirmed B-cell depletion showed a signal that was diluted by the full cohort, the negative-control inference is wrong — rituximab DID work in the mechanistically relevant subgroup, and the population-level null is an artifact of including patients without the mechanism.

(c) The timecourse of IgG decline after B-cell depletion is predictable: IgG half-life is 21 days. After complete B-cell ablation (day 0), pre-existing IgG declines with a half-life of 21 days — after 4 half-lives (84 days, ~12 weeks), IgG levels are ~6% of baseline. Clinical improvement in autoantibody-mediated diseases follows IgG decline with a lag proportional to tissue-level IgG clearance (which may be slower than serum IgG clearance — tissue-bound IgG has a longer half-life). If tissue-bound GPCR AAb has a half-life of 60–90 days, clinical improvement at 24 weeks may be minimal, and the peak effect may occur at 9–12 months — exactly where Phase II showed a signal and Phase III stopped measuring. The Phase III null at week 24 might be a "not yet" rather than a "no."

These three concerns do not overturn the negative-control inference — Fluge 2019 is still a properly powered RCT with a null primary endpoint. But they qualify the inference: the rituximab null is "no evidence of efficacy at 24 weeks" not "evidence of no efficacy at any timepoint in any subgroup." The negative-control interpretation should carry this qualification explicitly.

**Evidence link:** Fluge2011RituximabPhase2 (n=30) reported delayed improvement — fatigue scores declined at 6–12 months, with 10 of 15 rituximab patients reporting "major or moderate" improvement vs. 2 of 15 placebo. The Phase III was powered to detect this effect at 24 weeks — but if the true time-to-effect is 9–12 months, 24 weeks is too early. IgG pharmacokinetics: IgG half-life 21 days (Morell 1970, IgG metabolism); tissue-bound IgG half-life is longer and variable (Brambell 1966, The transmission of immunity from mother to young — IgG tissue distribution and catabolism). Rowe2019EditorialRituximab acknowledges the complexity: the editorial notes that the negative result constrains the B-cell hypothesis but does not eliminate it — the surviving alternatives (non-IgG, intracellular, tissue sanctuary) are explicitly listed. Subsec-13 already lists these alternatives; the concern here is that the primary null is presented as more definitive than the evidence supports, given the Phase II signal, the subgroup question, and the IgG timecourse.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.45

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Obtain individual patient data from Fluge 2019. Plot fatigue scores for rituximab vs placebo arms at 12, 24, 36, and 48 weeks. If the curves continue to diverge after 24 weeks (rituximab arm continuing to improve, placebo arm stable), the 24-week primary endpoint missed the true time-to-effect — the trial is not a negative-control at the correct endpoint. (b) Stratify rituximab-arm patients by baseline AAb status (if AAb data were collected). If AAb-positive patients show significant improvement at any timepoint, the null was driven by including AAb-negative patients who had no mechanistic reason to respond — the trial was null in the ITT population but NOT null in the mechanistically relevant subgroup. (c) Falsified for the residual-signal concern: if rituximab-arm fatigue scores are statistically indistinguishable from placebo at ALL timepoints up to 48 weeks, in ALL subgroups (including AAb-positive and confirmed B-cell-depleted), the null is uniform and the negative-control inference is robust — there is genuinely no signal anywhere in the data.

*Consequence:* The Fluge 2019 trial found no difference between rituximab and placebo at the 6-month mark. But the earlier, smaller trial showed benefit starting around 9 months. If antibodies take months to clear from the tissues after B cells are gone, the real effect might show up at 9–12 months — and the Phase III trial may have stopped measuring too early. The rituximab null might be a timing problem, not a mechanism problem. Negative-control inferences require the right endpoint at the right time — getting the time wrong means getting the verdict wrong.

#### 1.20 The IA Negative-Control Inference Rests on 13 Patients Across Two Heterogeneous Studies (CRITICAL)

**Category:** 12 — Evidence Quality Concerns
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Subsec-13 classifies immunoadsorption (IA) as a Class II negative-control for the circulating-IgG AAb hypothesis: IA null constrains the "circulating IgG1/IgG2/IgG4 AAb as rate-limiting" sub-hypothesis, leaving 4 alternative explanations (tissue-bound IgG, IgG3, non-IgG classes, intracellular AAb). The evidence base for this inference: Tölle2020RepeatImmunoadsorption (n=5, cert 0.45) and Stein2025IAPostCOVID (n=8, cert 0.43). That is 13 patients total, across two studies with different protocols (Tölle2020 in ME/CFS, Stein2025 in post-COVID), different IA columns, different treatment schedules, and different outcome measures.

The evidence-quality concern is not that the inference is wrong — it's that the evidence base is too thin to support ANY negative-control inference, Class I or Class II. Thirteen patients, with heterogeneous protocols and no control group (both studies are open-label, no sham-IA comparison), cannot distinguish a true negative-control null from underdetermined failure. The adequacy condition (condition 3 of the decision heuristic) cannot be assessed — were the IA protocols adequate? Were the IgG reductions adequate? Were the durations adequate? The answers are unknown for both studies because the sample sizes are too small to estimate adequacy parameters.

The concern applies more broadly: the negative-control class requires negative-control *trials*, not negative-control *case series*. A properly powered negative-control trial has: (a) pre-specified hypothesis (what hypothesis is being tested), (b) target-engagement measurement (confirm drug engaged target), (c) adequate n to exclude Type II error (failure to detect a true effect because n too small), (d) control group (to distinguish drug null from natural disease fluctuation). Tölle2020 and Stein2025 have (a) — the hypothesis is implicit — but lack (b), (c), and (d). They are case series, not negative-control trials. A Class II negative-control inference from 13 uncontrolled, unblinded patients is insufficient to support even the already-hedged claims in subsec-13.

This does not mean IA is useless as a diagnostic probe — a POSITIVE IA response IS informative (if you remove circulating IgG and the patient improves, circulating IgG is rate-limiting). But a NULL IA response from 13 patients tells us almost nothing. Subsec-13 should explicitly qualify the IA negative-control inference by its evidence-base size: "n=13 total across two studies, no control group, heterogeneous protocols — IA null evidence strength is low; the negative-control boundary is illustrated by IA, not established by it."

**Evidence link:** Tölle2020RepeatImmunoadsorption (n=5 ME/CFS, cert 0.45) — 5 patients, open-label, IA with protein A columns (IgG1/IgG2/IgG4 removal), clinical response heterogeneous (some improved, some didn't). Stein2025IAPostCOVID (n=8, cert 0.43) — 8 post-COVID patients, open-label, same protein A column principle, similar heterogeneity. Neither study was designed as a negative-control trial — they were exploratory treatment studies. The negative-control inference is a post-hoc interpretation of treatment studies, not a pre-registered negative-control hypothesis test. This is exactly the post-hoc interpretive problem that the prospective registry (Idea 1.2) would prevent — but for IA, the post-hoc interpretation has already been made, and the evidence base is too thin to challenge or confirm it.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.60

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a properly powered IA negative-control trial (n >50 GPCR-AAb-positive ME/CFS patients, pre-specified negative-control hypothesis, sham-IA control group, target-engagement measurement with IgG reduction >70%), the null rate may be significantly different from the null rate in the 13-patient evidence base. Specifically: the larger trial may show a higher null rate (because the existing studies are published precisely because some patients responded — publication bias toward positive signals) OR a lower null rate (because the larger trial has better target-engagement and identifies a subgroup of IA responders). Either way, the 13-patient evidence base is insufficiently precise. (b) If a sham-controlled IA trial (n=50) shows zero improvement over sham in GPCR-AAb-positive patients, the negative-control inference is confirmed and the evidence-base concern is resolved — the small studies were directionally correct. (c) If a sham-controlled IA trial shows significant improvement in ≥30% of GPCR-AAb-positive patients, the negative-control inference from Tölle2020/Stein2025 was wrong — IA IS effective in a subgroup, and the 13-patient evidence base's heterogeneity obscured this. The negative-control class would claim "IA null constrains the circulating-IgG hypothesis" when actually "IA null identified a subgroup that does NOT respond, but another subgroup does" — the inference direction is reversed.

*Consequence:* The claim that "immunoadsorption non-response constrains the circulating-IgG autoantibody hypothesis" is based on 13 patients total — 5 with ME/CFS, 8 with post-COVID fatigue. No control group, no blinding, different machines, different protocols. You can't constrain a hypothesis with 13 uncontrolled observations. If a 50-person sham-controlled trial showed null, that would constrain it. Thirteen patients who might have had inadequate IgG removal, might have been too sick to respond to anything, or might have had the wrong AAb subclass — that's not evidence, it's anecdote. The negative-control class needs negative-control *trials*, not exploratory case series retrospectively reinterpreted.
