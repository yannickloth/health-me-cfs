### H1: The Trust-Erosion Cascade — Implementation Gap as Independent Disease Modifier

**Category:** 1 — Novel Policy Mechanism
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The chapter documents an implementation gap — NICE 2021 exists but is unmonitored, physicians continue to recommend GET despite guideline reversal. The gap is treated as an evidence problem (we don't know if guidelines change practice). But the gap itself may be a disease modifier through a trust-erosion mechanism: when a patient with a contested illness encounters a physician who violates established national guidelines (recommends GET after NICE 2021), the patient experiences cognitive dissonance — "either the national guideline body is wrong, or my doctor is wrong." This is not merely unpleasant; it is a specific form of epistemic trauma that erodes trust in the healthcare system. The Bontempo2025invalidation model documents the consequence chain: provider dismissal → patient self-doubt → healthcare system avoidance → diagnostic delay → worsened outcomes. The implementation gap recruits this chain by creating a systematic divergence between what patients are told by guidelines and what they experience in the consultation room. The mechanism operates even when the physician means well — an uninformed but well-intentioned GP produces the same trust erosion as a dismissive one, because the patient cannot distinguish ignorance from malice.

The hypothesis extends the Bontempo model: trust erosion is not just about invalidation (being told symptoms are psychological) but also about institutional betrayal (being told the opposite of what the national guideline says). This predicts that implementation-gap-related trust erosion is highest for patients who are aware of the guideline — creating a perverse dynamic where patient education (making patients aware of NICE 2021) may increase distress when the physician doesn't follow it.

**Evidence link:** Bontempo2025invalidation (four consequence classes, causal pathway model). Kingdon2022NICEprimary (describes what clinicians should do, does not report what they do). Bateman2021Mayo (91% undiagnosed — structural evidence of the gap). Patient surveys documenting GET recommendations persisting after NICE 2021 — though no systematic audit exists. Trust-in-physician literature: patients with contested illnesses have lower trust (Looper2004); trust predicts adherence and outcomes across chronic diseases. Implementation science literature: guideline non-adherence is the norm, not the exception, across medicine — but the trust effects of non-adherence may be larger for contested illnesses where the patient's epistemic standing is already fragile.

**Certainty:** 0.40 (trust-erosion mechanism is plausible and consistent with Bontempo model; implementation-gap-as-disease-modifier is novel and untested; perverse dynamic of patient-education-increasing-distress is a logical extension but unmeasured; pathway from trust erosion to measurable outcomes is documented for other chronic diseases but not ME/CFS specifically.)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) ME/CFS patients who report guideline-discordant treatment (GET recommendation after NICE 2021 publication date) show lower trust-in-physician scores, higher healthcare avoidance, and worse functional outcomes at 12 months compared to patients who report guideline-concordant treatment, controlling for baseline severity. (b) The effect of guideline discordance on outcomes is mediated by trust erosion (measured via Wake Forest Trust in Physician scale) — not solely by the direct harm of the inappropriate treatment. (c) Patients with high guideline awareness (have read or been told about NICE 2021) show larger trust-erosion effects from guideline-discordant encounters than patients with low awareness — confirming the perverse patient-education dynamic.

**Non-specialist consequence:** The gap between what the guideline says and what your doctor does is not just a paperwork problem — it's a harm mechanism that may drive patients out of the healthcare system entirely. If true, the most cost-effective intervention isn't educating patients about NICE 2021 but enforcing it among physicians.

### H2: The Diagnostic Desert Hypothesis — Geographic Access as a Self-Reinforcing Inequality Trap

**Category:** 1 — Novel Policy Mechanism
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** ME/CFS specialist clinicians exist in approximately 10–15 centers across the US and UK, concentrated in major cities. The chapter documents that specialist clinic access is untested — no evidence that specialist care improves outcomes. But the problem may be more fundamental: for a disease where travel triggers PEM, specialist access is governed by a radius constraint. A patient living >X miles from the nearest specialist (where X is determined by individual PEM threshold for travel duration + cognitive load of navigation + sensory stress of transport) effectively has zero specialist access — not because the specialist doesn't exist, but because reaching them would cause deterioration.

This creates a "diagnostic desert" with two self-reinforcing properties: (a) patients in deserts cannot be diagnosed by specialists → fewer diagnoses recorded in desert regions → the region appears low-prevalence to health planners → no justification for deploying specialists to the desert, (b) patients in deserts who do get diagnosed (e.g., via self-advocacy, remote consultation) are self-selected — they have the health literacy, financial resources, and milder severity needed to navigate the gap — so the "diagnosed-in-desert" cohort is systematically healthier than the undiagnosed desert population, skewing regional prevalence estimates further downward.

The diagnostic desert mechanism is distinct from general rural healthcare shortages: in ME/CFS specifically, the "desertification radius" is shorter than for other chronic diseases because PEM makes travel an active harm, not just a cost/inconvenience. A patient with MS can drive 200 miles to see a specialist; a patient with severe ME/CFS may PEM-crash from a 20-mile car ride.

**Evidence link:** samms2025unequalaccess: 10× variation in diagnosis rates across English ICBs (0.1%–1.0%), unexplained by known demographics. Dimmock2024disparities: US racial and geographic disparities. The geographic variation in both datasets is consistent with diagnostic desert dynamics but has not been analyzed through this lens. Travel distance to specialist as access proxy is standard in health-services research (time-to-care analyses in oncology, cardiology) but has not been applied to ME/CFS with PEM-adjusted distance (where travel cost includes PEM aftermath, not just travel time). The KCE Belgian report (Cornelis2026KCE420) documents recognition as the central unmet need — consistent with diagnostic desert framing.

**Certainty:** 0.45 (geographic variation in diagnosis is documented; PEM-as-travel-barrier is mechanistically plausible; the self-reinforcing trap mechanism is novel and untested; requires demonstrating that low-diagnosis ICBs are also high-distance-to-specialist ICBs AND that this relationship is stronger for ME/CFS than for non-PEM chronic conditions.)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In the UK, diagnosis rate (per ICB, from Samms2025 data) is inversely correlated with distance from the nearest ME/CFS specialist clinic, and this correlation is stronger than for comparably ambiguous chronic conditions (e.g., fibromyalgia, which also lacks biomarkers but doesn't have PEM as a travel barrier). (b) PEM-adjusted travel impedance (distance × PEM severity score) predicts diagnosis probability better than raw distance — the desertification radius is PEM-modulated. (c) Diagnosis rate in an ICB is a better predictor of future specialist-deployment decisions than true prevalence → ICBs with low diagnosis rates remain low-diagnosis → desertification is self-reinforcing. A natural experiment: if a new specialist clinic opens in a previously underserved area, ME/CFS diagnosis rates should increase faster than for comparison conditions.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If you have severe ME/CFS and live more than an hour from a specialist, the specialist might as well not exist. The healthcare system looks at low diagnosis rates in your area and concludes there's no demand — but the demand is there, just invisible because you can't reach the clinic without making yourself sicker.

### H3: The Guideline Cascade Duration Hypothesis — Contested Diseases Have Longer Implementation Gaps

**Category:** 1 — Novel Policy Mechanism
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** All clinical guidelines take time to reach practice — the "bench-to-bedside" literature estimates 17 years for evidence to reach routine care on average. But the cascade duration may be systematically longer for contested illnesses because: (a) physicians have preexisting beliefs (ME/CFS is psychosomatic, GET is helpful) that the guideline contradicts — belief revision requires more exposure than belief formation, (b) contested illnesses lack specialty champions — for a new cancer guideline, oncologists advocate for uptake at tumor boards and department meetings; ME/CFS has no equivalent specialty presence in most hospitals, (c) the symptom the guideline centers (PEM) is invisible — a guideline for a disease defined by an invisible symptom that patients report subjectively faces an additional barrier: physicians must trust patient reports to apply the guideline, and trust in patient reports is exactly what is compromised in contested illnesses, (d) the guideline's main recommendation is negative (don't recommend GET, don't say CBT is curative) — negative recommendations are harder to audit than positive ones (you can measure who got chemotherapy; you can't measure who didn't get GET).

These four mechanisms predict that the NICE 2021 cascade duration will exceed the 17-year average, and that uptake will be uneven: faster in academic centers and among younger physicians, slower in community practice and among physicians trained in the pre-2015 era. The hypothesis further predicts that countries without a statutory care directive (i.e., every country except Germany, where the G-BA LongCOV-RL has legal force) will show longer cascade durations because guidelines are advisory.

**Evidence link:** Standard implementation science: 17-year evidence-to-practice gap (Morris2001, Balas2001). Guideline adherence literature: negative recommendations have lower adherence than positive ones across medicine. Kingdon2022NICEprimary: acknowledges the gap without measuring it. DEGAM2022fatigue: German S3 guideline warns against activating measures — published 2022, cascade duration unknown. GBA2023LongCOVRL: Germany's statutory directive provides a natural experiment: binding guidelines should have shorter cascade duration than advisory guidelines. No ME/CFS-specific cascade duration study exists.

**Certainty:** 0.35 (general implementation science supports the prediction; contested-illness amplification is novel; cascade duration for NICE 2021 is currently unobserved — we are inside the cascade; the statutory-vs-advisory natural experiment is testable but requires German data collection.)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) At 5 years post-NICE 2021 (2026), the proportion of UK primary care physicians who correctly identify that GET is no longer recommended will be <50% — exceeding the typical 5-year guideline uptake rate for unambiguous diseases. (b) German physicians practicing under the G-BA statutory directive will show higher guideline knowledge of PEM contraindications than UK physicians under the advisory NICE 2021 — testing the statutory-vs-advisory cascade duration difference. (c) Younger physicians (graduated after 2015 IOM report) and physicians with ME/CFS-specialist colleagues will show faster guideline uptake — consistent with the belief-revision and specialty-champion mechanisms. (d) Guideline-consistent referral rates (referrals to specialist ME/CFS care rather than to physiotherapy for GET) will increase over time more slowly in community practices than academic centers.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Even if NICE 2021 is perfect evidence, most doctors are still giving the wrong advice — and we don't know how many, because nobody is counting. If it takes 17+ years for contested-illness guidelines to reach practice, every adult physician practicing today will retire before their training catches up.

### H4: Healthcare Invalidation × PEM Synergy — A Bootstrap Trap Preventing Care Re-Entry

**Category:** 1 — Novel Policy Mechanism
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The Bontempo2025invalidation model identifies healthcare system avoidance as a consequence — patients delay or forgo needed care after invalidating encounters. The chapter also establishes that PEM prevents study and clinic participation (travel, cognition, sensory load). These two barriers interact synergistically: a patient who has been invalidated is reluctant to seek care, and a patient with PEM cannot easily access care even when motivated. The interaction creates a bootstrap trap: the patient is too traumatized to trust doctors and too fatigued to shop for a new one. Even when good care becomes available (a new specialist opens, a local GP reads NICE 2021), the patient cannot re-enter the system because: (a) past invalidation produces anticipatory dread of the next medical encounter, (b) PEM makes the search process (calling offices, researching providers, traveling to appointments) physiologically prohibitive, and (c) each failed search attempt costs PEM energy that cannot be recovered.

The result: the most harmed patients — those with the longest history of invalidation and the most severe PEM — are least able to benefit from healthcare improvements. This trap means that healthcare reform may show perverse effects: investments in better care may primarily benefit patients who were less invalidated (milder disease, shorter disease duration) while the patients who need reform most remain outside the system. The trap predicts that healthcare avoidance rates should be higher for ME/CFS than for comparably disabling diseases without the invalidation+PEM synergy.

**Evidence link:** Bontempo2025invalidation: health system avoidance documented as consequence class 3; 151 qualitative reports provide granular detail on avoidance mechanisms. Hussein2024HealthcareBarriers: Canadian scoping review documents inadequate/inconsistent provider education as core barrier. Podell2020: 75% unable to work — functional access barrier quantified. Fennell2021Suffering: theoretical framework for suffering in severe ME/CFS, healthcare-engendered trauma. The synergy (invalidation × PEM) is not directly documented — it requires combining two literatures that have not been integrated.

**Certainty:** 0.45 (both mechanisms individually documented; the synergy is logically implied by the conjunction of Bontempo's avoidance model and the PEM physiology documented in the paper's treatment chapters; the trap prediction — that healthcare reform benefits the least-harmed — is untested but falsifiable; the "perverse effect" claim is the riskiest and least-supported component.)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a cohort of ME/CFS patients, healthcare avoidance (months since last primary care or specialist visit for ME/CFS symptoms) is predicted by the interaction of invalidation history (Bontempo consequence classes experienced) × PEM severity, with a significant interaction term above and beyond either main effect. (b) When a new specialist clinic opens in a region, the first 50 patients to access it will have lower baseline invalidation scores and milder PEM than the regional ME/CFS population — the trap selectively filters out the most harmed patients. (c) ME/CFS patients who screen positive for medical trauma (healthcare-engendered PTSD-type symptoms) show longer gaps between symptom onset and first specialist visit than patients without medical trauma, even when controlling for disease severity and geographic proximity — the invalidation × PEM trap is measurable.

**Non-specialist consequence:** The patients who need a good doctor most — the ones with the worst history of being dismissed and the most severe energy limitations — are the least likely to find one, even if one is available. Building better clinics may not be enough; we may need to actively reach out to the patients who have been burned too many times to seek care on their own.

### R1: The NICE 2021 Natural Experiment — Before/After Audit of UK Primary Care Practice

**Category:** 2 — Policy Experiment Design
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Objective:** Test whether national guideline publication changes clinical practice in the absence of enforcement. NICE NG206 (October 2021) was a binding evidence reversal: GET explicitly not recommended, PEM recognized as central clinical feature, diagnosis permitted at 3 months. Five years post-publication, no audit exists.

**Rationale:** This is the single most actionable health-services question in the domain: did NICE 2021 change anything? The UK's centralized healthcare data infrastructure (CPRD, QResearch, OpenSAFELY) makes this question answerable with existing administrative data. If the answer is "no detectable change in practice," the implementation-gap mechanisms (H1, H3) are confirmed and the field's energy should shift from guideline writing to implementation. If "yes, practice changed measurably," NICE 2021 becomes an implementation template for other countries.

**Methods:** (a) Define the pre-NICE window as April 2018–September 2021 (NICE draft published November 2020, final October 2021 — include the draft consultation period as a sensitivity analysis), (b) post-NICE window as January 2022–December 2024, (c) primary outcomes: rate of new ME/CFS diagnoses per 100,000 patient-years; proportion of ME/CFS-coded patients referred to physiotherapy for fatigue (proxy for GET); proportion referred to graded exercise or exercise therapy specifically; proportion prescribed antidepressants as first-line treatment, (d) secondary outcomes: diagnostic delay (time from first fatigue/PEM presentation code to ME/CFS diagnosis code), referral to specialist services (neurology, ME/CFS clinic, CFS/ME service), (e) interrupted time series analysis with the October 2021 publication date as the structural break point, (f) control conditions: fibromyalgia (also subjective, also contested, no guideline change in 2021), rheumatoid arthritis (objective biomarker, no guideline change) — if referral/exercise patterns change for ME/CFS but not fibromyalgia or RA, the change is guideline-attributable.

**Evidence link:** Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD Aurum) covers ~20 million UK patients with longitudinal primary care records, including Read codes for ME/CFS diagnoses, referrals, and prescriptions. Samms2025unequalaccess used CPRD for the n=100,055 diagnosis-rate analysis — establishing feasibility. Kingdon2022NICEprimary describes the implementation gap qualitatively. OpenSAFELY allows federated analysis of primary care data across England. No interrupted time series of NICE 2021 impact exists.

**Certainty of feasibility:** 0.75 (data exists, method is standard pharmacoepidemiology, Samms2025 precedent demonstrates data access and coding validity; primary barrier is researcher initiative, not data access or methodology).

**Falsifiable prediction (for the guideline-optimist position):** If NICE 2021 changed practice, the interrupted time series will show a structural break in October 2021: (a) GET/physiotherapy referral rates for ME/CFS decrease by ≥20% relative to pre-NICE trend, (b) diagnostic delay decreases (more diagnoses at 3–6 months post-symptom-onset rather than 2+ years), (c) no structural break for fibromyalgia or RA control conditions. If no structural break is detected, the chapter's implementation-gap concern is confirmed: a guideline revision without enforcement is a publication, not a practice change.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Five years after the UK's national health authority said "don't recommend GET for ME/CFS," we still don't know whether doctors stopped recommending it. This study is cheap, fast, and could be done with existing data — the fact it hasn't been done is itself evidence of how little health-services research attention ME/CFS receives.

### R2: The Geographic Quasi-Experiment — Specialist Clinic Access as Instrumental Variable

**Category:** 2 — Policy Experiment Design
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Objective:** Estimate the causal effect of specialist clinic access on ME/CFS outcomes without randomization. The chapter identifies zero comparative effectiveness studies of specialist vs primary care — and an RCT is decades away (funding unlikely, ethical equipoise uncertain, severe patients cannot be randomized to travel for clinic or clinic cannot be randomized to not-travel). The quasi-experimental approach exploits geographic variation: patients who happen to live near a specialist clinic vs those who don't.

**Rationale:** Geographic proximity to healthcare is a standard instrumental variable in health-services research because it satisfies the exclusion restriction (distance to clinic should affect outcomes only through clinic use, not directly) and is strongly associated with the exposure (closer patients use clinics more). In ME/CFS, the PEM-adjusted distance (what is the travel cost in PEM terms?) adds a disease-specific refinement: the instrument is not raw miles but PEM-weighted impedance.

**Methods:** (a) Identify all specialist ME/CFS clinical services in the UK (NHS CFS/ME services mapped by ICS, plus private clinics where data exists) and in the US (Bateman Horne Center, Stanford, OMI, others). (b) Using CPRD (UK) or claims data (US), identify all patients with an ME/CFS diagnosis code. (c) Compute distance from patient residential postcode/ZIP to nearest specialist clinic. (d) Primary analysis: two-stage least squares (2SLS) — first stage: distance predicts specialist clinic use; second stage: specialist clinic use predicts outcomes (diagnostic delay, functional status via SF-36 if available or employment status/proxy, disability benefit receipt, PEM-related emergency department visits). (e) Sensitivity analysis: PEM-adjusted distance where the impedance weight is inversely proportional to the patient's functional capacity score. (f) Control for area-level deprivation, rurality, local GP density, and age/sex. (g) Compare near-clinic outcomes for ME/CFS to near-clinic outcomes for MS (where specialist access is known to improve outcomes) to calibrate the instrument.

**Evidence link:** Samms2025unequalaccess provides the ICB-level diagnosis variation map — the geographic signal is large. Geographic instrumental variable methods are standard in health economics (McClellan1994 distance-to-hospital for AMI; Doyle2005 distance-to-ICU). Fadlon2019 geographic quasi-experiment in oncology. The ME/CFS application is novel. Requires specialist clinic location data (publicly mappable) and patient-level outcome data (CPRD for UK, Medicare/Medicaid claims for US — though US claims have sparse functional outcome data).

**Certainty of feasibility:** 0.55 (method is standard; UK data exists; US data is richer on claims but poorer on functional outcomes — may require limiting to disability determination outcomes; the PEM-adjusted distance refinement is novel and requires PEM-severity data not routinely collected; the "near-clinic" threshold must be calibrated — too wide and the instrument is weak, too narrow and the sample is too small).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Patients living within 30-minute travel of a specialist ME/CFS clinic have shorter diagnostic delay, lower rate of ED visits for ME/CFS-related complaints, and higher rate of diagnosed treatable comorbidities (POTS, MCAS) compared to matched patients living >60 minutes away. (b) The specialist access effect is larger for ME/CFS than for MS (because MS has more standardized primary care management — specialist access adds more marginal value for ME/CFS). (c) PEM-adjusted distance predicts outcomes better than raw distance. (d) If no specialist access effect is detected, the null hypothesis (O2) is supported — specialist clinics do not improve outcomes over informed primary care, or the effect is too small to detect against selection bias.

**Non-specialist consequence:** We don't know whether building more specialist ME/CFS clinics actually helps patients. This study would tell us whether patients who live near a clinic do better than those who don't — without needing to randomize anyone.

### R3: The Medical Education Intervention Pilot — A Cluster-Randomized Trial with Diagnostic Rate as Primary Outcome

**Category:** 2 — Policy Experiment Design
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Objective:** Test the chapter's most upstream hypothesis: that medical education deficits cause diagnostic failure, and that structured education interventions can increase diagnostic rates. Muirhead2021 documents that 41% of UK medical schools don't teach ME/CFS; Bateman2021 estimates 91% undiagnosed. The causal chain — education → knowledge → diagnosis → appropriate management → better outcomes — is untested.

**Rationale:** The chapter devotes its first substantive section to medical education as the upstream failure. But "everyone agrees medical education is the problem" is not evidence that fixing education fixes outcomes. A cluster-randomized pilot in primary care practices tests the most proximal link: education → diagnosis. If education doesn't increase diagnosis rates, the upstream-failure framing may be incomplete — other barriers (stigma, time pressure, referral-pathway absence) may dominate even after education. If education does increase diagnosis rates, the pilot provides an effect-size estimate to power a full trial with patient outcomes.

**Methods:** (a) Recruit n=20–30 primary care practices (UK general practices or US family medicine clinics), cluster-randomize 1:1 to intervention vs control. (b) Intervention: structured 2-hour ME/CFS education module covering PEM recognition (orthostatic tachycardia + post-exertional symptom exacerbation as red flags), diagnostic criteria (IOM 2015, NICE NG206), guideline awareness (GET not recommended, pacing energy management), referral pathways, and — critically — a point-of-care EHR decision support tool that flags patients with fatigue+PEM symptom patterns for ME/CFS consideration. (c) Control: usual care (no additional ME/CFS training beyond standard CME). (d) Primary outcome: new ME/CFS diagnoses per 10,000 registered patients over 18 months post-intervention, comparing intervention vs control arms. (e) Secondary outcomes: diagnostic delay (time from first documented fatigue/PEM presentation to ME/CFS diagnosis), appropriateness of referrals (proportion of ME/CFS referrals sent to appropriate specialist service vs physiotherapy/exercise service), patient-reported experience (brief PREM adapted from Thornton2025 co-production preferences), clinician confidence in ME/CFS management (pre-post questionnaire). (f) Stratify by practice size, area-level deprivation, baseline diagnostic rate.

**Evidence link:** Muirhead2021 (education deficit documented). Samms2025unequalaccess (diagnosis rates vary 10× — suggests some practices diagnose more, but doesn't establish whether education predicts diagnosis). Hussein2024HealthcareBarriers (education identified as core barrier). Wormgoor2023 (PEM-focused care reduces dissatisfaction — but tested in specialist context, not primary care). The EHR decision-support component is adapted from successful interventions in diabetes and hypertension screening where point-of-care prompts increase screening rates. No ME/CFS education intervention trial exists.

**Certainty of feasibility:** 0.50 (methodologically straightforward; primary outcome is extractable from EHR without additional patient burden; the education module must balance brevity (2 hours is maximum feasible for practicing GPs) against content depth; the 18-month follow-up may be too short — diagnostic delay is 5+ years, so detecting a change in mean delay requires longer follow-up or a larger sample; the pilot is powered to detect a doubling of diagnosis rate, not smaller effect sizes).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Intervention practices show ≥50% increase in ME/CFS diagnosis rate compared to control practices (primary hypothesis). (b) Intervention practices show lower rate of referral to physiotherapy/exercise services for fatigue patients (secondary — guideline awareness). (c) Baseline diagnosis rate correlates with clinician ME/CFS knowledge at baseline (validation that education-deficit causes diagnostic deficit within practices). (d) If intervention practices show no increase in diagnosis rate despite improved clinician knowledge (tested by questionnaire), the barrier is not knowledge — it's structural (time pressure, lack of referral options, stigma). The education-as-upstream-failure narrative would need revision.

**Non-specialist consequence:** We spend decades arguing that doctors need to be taught about ME/CFS — but has anyone actually tested whether teaching them changes what they do? This pilot is the smallest, cheapest experiment that could give us an answer. If it fails, the problem isn't education — it's something else in the system.

### R4: The PEM-Proof Healthcare Delivery Trial — Telemedicine + Remote Symptom Tracking vs Standard Clinic-Based Care

**Category:** 2 — Policy Experiment Design
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Objective:** Test whether a care delivery model designed to avoid PEM (telemedicine, asynchronous communication, remote physiologic monitoring) produces better outcomes than standard clinic-based care for ME/CFS patients — addressing the "healthcare triggers PEM" paradox directly.

**Rationale:** The chapter identifies that standard healthcare delivery is PEM-generating: travel to clinic, waiting room sensory overload, cognitive load of explaining a complex illness history, physical exam. The co-production study (Thornton2025, n=8) identifies patient preferences for PEM-aware care delivery, but no study tests whether PEM-avoiding care delivery improves outcomes. This trial — the "PEM-Proof" model — tests whether the delivery channel matters independently of the clinical content.

**Methods:** (a) Randomized trial (n=200 newly diagnosed ME/CFS patients from primary care referrals), two arms: PEM-Proof Care (all visits via video telehealth, asynchronous secure messaging for non-urgent communication, patient-reported outcomes via smartphone app with actigraphy, clinic coordinator who manages referrals so patients don't have to navigate multiple specialist offices, pre-visit energy-conservation instructions) vs Standard Care (in-person clinic visits at the same ME/CFS specialist service, phone follow-up, same clinical protocols — pacing education, comorbidity screening, pharmacological treatment as indicated). (b) Clinical content is identical between arms — same physicians, same treatment algorithms, same referral patterns. Only the delivery channel differs. (c) Primary outcome: SF-36 physical function at 12 months. (d) Secondary: PEM episode frequency (patient diary + actigraphy-validated rest days), healthcare utilization (number of visits, ED visits, hospitalizations), patient satisfaction (PREM), cost (payer perspective — telehealth may be cheaper but requires more frequent touch-points; clinic visits are expensive but less frequent). (e) Subgroup: severe patients (housebound at enrollment) — hypothesized to benefit most from PEM-Proof delivery.

**Evidence link:** Thornton2025energy: co-production identified telehealth, mHealth, and energy-respecting scheduling as patient priorities. Chu2020: remote data collection feasibility demonstrated in ME/CFS. Pendergrast2024SelectionBias: 71% PEM prevents study participation — same mechanism should prevent clinic visit participation. Telemedicine trial literature: generally non-inferior to in-person for chronic disease management (diabetes, hypertension, depression) but no ME/CFS-specific trial exists. The risk: telehealth may reduce PEM from travel but increase cognitive load from screen-based communication — the net effect on PEM burden is unknown and must be tested.

**Certainty of feasibility:** 0.55 (telemedicine infrastructure exists; ME/CFS specialist clinics already offer telehealth — this trial would standardize and compare it to in-person; the PEM-diary + actigraphy outcomes package is validated; the non-inferiority design acknowledges that PEM-Proof may be equivalent rather than superior; the main barrier is that patients cannot be blinded to delivery channel, and patient expectations about telehealth vs clinic may bias subjective endpoints — actigraphy outcomes address this partially.)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) PEM-Proof Care is non-inferior to Standard Clinic Care on SF-36 physical function at 12 months (the null — delivery channel doesn't matter — is plausible). (b) PEM-Proof Care is superior on PEM episode frequency (fewer PEM episodes because travel and in-person visit stress are eliminated). (c) Severe/housebound patients in PEM-Proof arm show higher study completion rates and larger SF-36 benefit (because in-person travel is the dominant barrier for this subgroup). (d) Cost analysis favors PEM-Proof (fewer ED visits, fewer missed appointments, lower patient-borne transport costs). (e) If PEM-Proof is inferior on functional outcomes despite reducing PEM episodes, the clinical content delivered via telehealth (physical exam limitations, rapport-building difficulty) may matter more than PEM avoidance — an important negative result constraining the co-production narrative.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If we design healthcare visits around what the disease does to patients — instead of around what's convenient for the clinic schedule — do patients actually get better? The answer determines whether the push for "PEM-aware care" is a patient preference issue or a clinical effectiveness issue.

### O1: The Medical Education Null — Knowledge Does Not Produce Behavior Change

**Category:** 10 — Reasons This Approach May NOT Be Relevant
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Rationale:** The chapter's opening argument — medical education deficits as the "most upstream failure" — implies that fixing education would cascade downstream to better diagnosis, management, and outcomes. But the evidence for knowledge-behavior linkage in rare/complex chronic diseases is weak. Physicians who receive education on any given rare disease rarely encounter a case; knowledge decays rapidly; and in contested illnesses, knowledge may produce "informed dismissal" — the physician knows enough to suspect ME/CFS but has no referral pathway, no treatment to offer, and no time in a 10-minute consultation to diagnose a multi-system disease. The frustration of knowing-but-not-helping may be worse for the patient than ignorance — the educated physician says "this sounds like ME/CFS, but there's nothing I can do" which is both validating and devastating, vs the uneducated physician who orders irrelevant tests and makes a psychiatric referral, which is invalidating but may trigger earlier specialist referral (as the patient rejects the psychiatric frame and seeks a second opinion).

The medical-education narrative is intuitively compelling but mechanistically fragile. If the dominant barrier to diagnosis is not physician knowledge but structural constraints (10-minute consultations, absence of specialist referral pathways, absence of treatments that would justify the diagnostic effort), then education interventions will fail. Physicians do not diagnose conditions they cannot treat — this is a well-documented clinical behavior pattern (diagnostic nihilism). If ME/CFS has no evidence-based pharmacological treatment, a physician who knows how to diagnose it faces a "so what?" problem that may suppress diagnostic effort.

**Evidence link:** Muirhead2021 (education gaps documented). Bateman2021Mayo (91% undiagnosed). The knowledge-action gap is documented in clinical practice across multiple diseases (Kennedy2004, Cabana1999). Diagnostic nihilism in untreatable conditions is documented in dementia, ALS, and other neurodegenerative diseases where physicians delay or avoid diagnosis because the diagnostic label does not lead to a treatment plan. ME/CFS has treatable components (POTS, MCAS, sleep, pain) but no disease-modifying treatment — the education-to-action pathway may bottleneck at "diagnose, then what?"

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a survey of physicians who received ME/CFS education (at medical school or CME), knowledge scores on ME/CFS diagnostic criteria and management predict self-reported diagnostic confidence but do NOT predict actual ME/CFS diagnosis rates in their patient panels — the knowledge-behavior gap is large. (b) Physicians who practice in systems with a clear ME/CFS referral pathway (specialist clinic, care coordinator) show higher diagnosis rates than equally knowledgeable physicians without referral pathways — confirming the structural bottleneck. (c) If the R3 education pilot, above, finds no increase in diagnosis rates despite improved knowledge, the medical-education framing needs to be downgraded from "upstream failure" to "necessary but insufficient."

**Non-specialist consequence:** Teaching every doctor about ME/CFS might achieve nothing if doctors still have no specialist to refer to, no treatment to offer, and no time to diagnose. The education argument sounds right, but sometimes the thing that "everyone agrees is the problem" isn't actually the bottleneck.

### O2: The Specialist Clinic Paradox — No Evidence That Specialist Care Is Better, and It May Be Worse

**Category:** 10 — Reasons This Approach May NOT Be Relevant
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Rationale:** The chapter correctly identifies zero comparative effectiveness studies of specialist clinics. But the null hypothesis that specialist clinics do not improve outcomes deserves explicit articulation — not as a nihilist position, but as a constraint on the advocacy default ("every patient should have access to a specialist clinic").

Arguments for the null: (a) Self-selection: patients who reach a specialist clinic are systematically different from those who don't — they are wealthier (affording travel, specialist co-pays), more health-literate (navigating referral systems), less severely affected (able to travel), and more health-engaged (motivated to seek specialist care). Any observed benefit of specialist care may reflect the selection, not the care. (b) Over-investigation risk: specialist clinics may order extensive diagnostic panels (full autoimmune workups, tilt-table testing, skin biopsies for SFN, genetic panels) that generate incidental findings, borderline results, and false positives. Each borderline result triggers a cascade of follow-up testing — more visits, more travel, more PEM — without improving outcomes. The specialist may be doing more medicine but not better medicine. (c) Fragmented care: a specialist clinic model that refers to 6+ subspecialists (POTS → cardiology, MCAS → allergy/immunology, SFN → neurology, GI symptoms → gastroenterology, sleep → sleep medicine, pain → pain clinic) creates fragmented care with unmanaged interactions. The patient now has 6 physicians who don't communicate. A good primary care GP who manages the whole patient may produce better outcomes than 6 uncoordinated specialists. (d) The treatment ceiling: if the ceiling on treatment benefit is low (pharmacological treatment for ME/CFS has small effect sizes; the main evidence-based interventions are pacing education and comorbidity management), then the marginal benefit of specialist over primary care is bounded — a GP who knows pacing and comorbidity screening may achieve near-ceiling outcomes, leaving little room for specialist superiority.

**Evidence link:** Bateman2021Mayo: expert consensus on what specialists recommend — not evidence that specialist care is superior. The AHRQ review (Smith2014AHRQ): treatment evidence rated low-to-moderate — the treatment ceiling is low. KCE Belgian report (Cornelis2026KCE420): patients want recognition, not necessarily more specialists. The over-investigation cascade is documented across medicine (Moynihan2012, Welch2011) but not specifically in ME/CFS specialist clinics. The "6 subspecialists" scenario is clinical experience, not published evidence — but is consistent with the multi-system pathology documented in the paper's treatment chapters.

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) The geographic quasi-experiment (R2, above) will find no specialist clinic benefit on functional outcomes after controlling for demographic and socioeconomic selection factors — the specialist-advocacy position fails. (b) Specialist clinic patients with ≥3 subspecialist referrals show higher medical utilization but no better functional outcomes than matched patients with ≤1 subspecialist referral — more medicine does not produce better results. (c) In a matched comparison of specialist clinic patients vs primary-care-managed patients with similar severity and demographics, costs are 3–5× higher in the specialist group with no functional outcome difference.

**Non-specialist consequence:** The patient-advocacy position ("everyone deserves a specialist") is the right value — but we should check whether specialist care works before demanding it. There is a real possibility that building dozens of specialist clinics would cost hundreds of millions and produce no measurable improvement over well-informed primary care.

### O3: The Disability System Reform Null — Removing Assessment Barriers Reveals Idiopathic Severity

**Category:** 10 — Reasons This Approach May NOT Be Relevant
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Rationale:** The Podell2020 argument — that standard functional capacity assessments underestimate ME/CFS disability because they don't capture PEM — is mechanistically sound. The reform implication: disability assessment should use PEM-aware evaluation (2-day CPET, actigraphy, PEM diary). But the null hypothesis is that even PEM-aware assessment would not substantially change disability determination outcomes because: (a) disability systems are designed to screen out ambiguous conditions regardless of assessment method — the function of the standard assessment is to produce a "objective" score that justifies denial; changing the assessment instrument without changing the adjudicator's incentives may produce the same denial rate with different justification, (b) PEM-aware assessment may document that ME/CFS patients have severe disability — but also document that ME/CFS disability is highly variable (good days and bad days), which may make patients appear *less* disabled under a more granular assessment (the standard one-day snapshot captures the patient on a good or bad day; 14-day actigraphy captures the *average*, which may be above the disability threshold even though the *worst* days are below it), (c) disability adjudicators may discount PEM-aware evidence because PEM is "self-reported" or "subjective" even when measured objectively — the 2-day CPET decline is objective, but attributing the decline to PEM rather than disuse/deconditioning requires clinical judgment that adjudicators may be reluctant to exercise.

In other words, the disability system failure is not an assessment-instrument problem — it's a structural problem of a system designed around diseases with objective biomarkers, stable trajectories, and predictable response to treatment. Changing the assessment instrument doesn't change the system's design assumptions. A fairer assessment method applied by a system that structurally defaults to denial produces the same outcome.

**Evidence link:** Podell2020 (practice guide documenting the mismatch between assessment instruments and PEM). SSA disability determination data: overall approval rates for musculoskeletal and mental health conditions range 20–40%; no ME/CFS-specific data exists. Disability determination studies in fibromyalgia (a similarly contested, subjective, fluctuating condition): objective evidence of functional limitation (tender points, functional capacity evaluation) did not substantially improve claim approval rates — the limiting factor was adjudicator judgment, not evidence quality. This precedent from a closely analogous condition supports the null.

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a jurisdiction where PEM-aware assessment (actigraphy + PEM diary + 2-day CPET) is systematically offered to ME/CFS disability claimants, the claim approval rate increases by <10 percentage points relative to jurisdictions where standard assessments are used — the reform effect is small. (b) PEM documentation quality (objective vs self-reported) is not the primary predictor of claim outcome — adjudicator characteristics (experience with contested illnesses, geographic region) predict outcome more strongly. (c) If the disability reform null is refuted — PEM-aware assessment increases approval rates by >20 percentage points in a real-world implementation — the assessment-instrument argument is confirmed, and the reform lever is assessment modernization, not system redesign.

**Non-specialist consequence:** It sounds right that "if we measured PEM properly, disability systems would approve more claims." But fibromyalgia tried this — better evidence didn't change claim outcomes. The system may be built to deny ambiguous conditions regardless of evidence quality.

### N1: The Universal Access Null — Removing All Barriers Does Not Change the Disease Trajectory

**Category:** 11 — Null Hypothesis Assessment
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Null hypothesis statement:** If every patient were diagnosed within 3 months of onset, every physician were trained in ME/CFS management, and every disability claim were approved on PEM evidence, the core disease trajectory (onset → ratchet deterioration → severity progression) would remain unchanged. Healthcare system quality affects the *experience* of the disease — validation reduces suffering, disability benefits reduce poverty, appropriate management prevents iatrogenic harms — but the underlying biology (the inflammation-mitochondrial-epigenetic cascade, the energy ratchet, the attractor dynamics) is insensitive to healthcare system performance.

**Why this matters:** The chapter frames healthcare system failure as a contributor to worsened disease outcomes through pathways (diagnostic delay → accumulated PEM events → ratchet progression; healthcare invalidation → healthcare avoidance → missed comorbidity → worse outcomes; inappropriate treatments → iatrogenic worsening). If the null is true, these pathways affect quality-of-life outcomes (stigma, financial security, suffering) but not the biological trajectory (functional capacity, severity level, treatment response). The distinction between "disease severity" and "disease experience" is the key axis: healthcare reform may improve patient experience without touching the disease.

**Evidence for the null:** (a) The rituximab Phase III trial found no benefit — if the strongest disease-modifying drug candidate tested to date had null results, it's plausible that nothing in the healthcare delivery system (pacing education, comorbidity management, validation) has disease-modifying power. (b) The DecodeME GWAS found 8 genome-wide significant loci — substantial heritability component, implying that a significant portion of disease risk is genetic and not environmentally modifiable through healthcare. (c) The natural history of ME/CFS (Nacul2011, Jason2006, Brown2012) shows that a minority of patients recover or improve spontaneously, a majority remain stable or worsen, and the predictors of recovery are demographic (younger age, male sex, early diagnosis) and trigger-related (post-infectious onset) — not healthcare-system factors. If spontaneous recovery is predominantly biologically determined, healthcare may shift the recovery probability by percentage points, not by the multiples the chapter implies.

**Evidence against the null:** (a) Diagnostic delay is associated with worse outcomes — early-diagnosed patients have better functional trajectories (though this is correlational, not causal). (b) GET causes documented harms in a majority of patients — removing harmful treatment is an intervention that demonstrably prevents iatrogenic worsening. (c) Pacing reduces PEM frequency in clinical experience — if PEM events are ratchet steps, reducing PEM frequency through pacing should slow disease progression, even if it doesn't reverse it. The effect of pacing on long-term trajectory is untested but mechanistically plausible.

**Falsifiable prediction:** The natural experiment of a jurisdiction that implements comprehensive ME/CFS healthcare reform (early diagnosis, PEM-aware management, disability-system modernization, specialist clinic access) — if one exists in the future — would show: (a) improved quality-of-life outcomes (lower financial stress, lower healthcare invalidation, higher patient satisfaction), (b) no difference in disease severity outcomes (functional capacity, PEM severity, progression to severe/very severe) compared to matched patients in reform-absent jurisdictions. The universal access null would be refuted if reform-absent cohorts show faster functional decline than reform-present cohorts.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If healthcare reform makes patients feel better but doesn't make them physically better, the case for reform is humanitarian — it's about dignity and suffering — not therapeutic. Politically, this is a weaker argument for investment, which is why the chapter should distinguish between outcomes it expects reform to improve (patient experience) and outcomes it may not (disease biology).

### N2: The Co-Production Null — Patient-Designed Services May Not Improve Outcomes

**Category:** 11 — Null Hypothesis Assessment
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Null hypothesis statement:** Healthcare services co-designed with ME/CFS patients — incorporating patient preferences for multidisciplinary teams, mHealth tools, PEM-aware scheduling, and asynchronous communication — produce equivalent or worse clinical outcomes than services designed by clinical experts without patient co-design input. The null holds that: (a) patient preference is a value, not an evidence-based predictor of clinical effectiveness, (b) patients with a disease that impairs cognition (brain fog), reduces energy, and introduces systematic biases (pacing advocacy may lead to over-recommendation of pacing vs other modalities; diagnostic delay trauma may lead to over-prioritization of validation vs investigation) may design worse services than clinical experts, (c) co-production may add process complexity (consensus-building, iterative revision) that delays service deployment without improving content.

**Why this matters:** Thornton2025energy (n=8) represents the only co-production evidence in ME/CFS. The chapter currently treats co-production as a positive design principle (spec:coproduction — "co-produced service design may be the most efficient route to closing the gap") without acknowledging the null. Co-production is a democratic value — patients should have a voice in designing their care. But it is not yet an evidence-based method for improving outcomes in ME/CFS, and previous examples of patient-preferred services not improving outcomes exist in other diseases (patient-preferred diabetes self-management programs, patient-co-designed mental health crisis services).

**Evidence for the null:** (a) Thornton2025: n=8, not powered to detect anything — the study demonstrates co-production feasibility, not co-production effectiveness. (b) In other diseases, patient preference and clinical effectiveness sometimes diverge: cancer patients in the 1970s–80s preferred aggressive chemotherapy over palliative care, despite evidence favoring palliation for quality-adjusted survival; diabetes patients prefer less-frequent glucose monitoring, but continuous monitoring improves HbA1c; mental health patients prefer unstructured therapy, but structured protocols (CBT manualized) show larger effect sizes in trials. (c) The null's strongest form: ME/CFS patients are dealing with a disease that has taught them that healthcare is adversarial. Preferences shaped by a decade of invalidation may favor validation over investigation — "I want a doctor who believes me" over "I want a doctor who runs the right tests." A service optimized for validation may under-investigate treatable comorbidities and over-rely on pacing as a universal recommendation.

**Evidence against the null:** (a) ME/CFS may be a special case where patient preference is correct because clinical expertise has historically been wrong — the PACE trial's experts recommended GET, and patients (correctly) reported that GET harmed them. (b) Co-produced services in other chronic diseases (diabetes shared medical appointments, HIV patient navigation) have shown equivalent or better outcomes. (c) The null ignores that clinical experts without patient input may design services that patients cannot use (clinic appointments that trigger PEM, questionnaires too long for cognitively impaired patients) — co-production's value is in avoiding design errors that clinical experts wouldn't foresee.

**Falsifiable prediction:** A comparative trial of co-designed vs expert-designed ME/CFS care services (identical clinical content, different service design) would show: (a) equivalent clinical outcomes (SF-36 at 12 months), non-inferior, (b) higher patient satisfaction in the co-designed arm, (c) lower no-show rates in the co-designed arm. This pattern — equivalent clinical outcomes, better patient experience — would be a win for co-production but would disprove the stronger claim that co-production improves clinical outcomes. If co-designed services show *worse* clinical outcomes, the co-production null would be confirmed and co-production should be limited to patient-satisfaction-quality improvement rather than service design leadership.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Including patients in service design is the right thing to do — but we shouldn't assume it improves clinical outcomes just because it's the right thing to do. It might just make patients feel better about getting the same care.

### E1: The Qualitative Participation Filter — Systematic Exclusion of the Most Affected from the Evidence Base

**Category:** 12 — Evidence Quality Concern
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Concern:** Every qualitative study in the evidence base — Bontempo2025 (151 qualitative reports, n=11,307), Parslow2017children, Parslow2017paediatric, Hussein2024HealthcareBarriers, Melby2024, Fennell2021, Asbring2002 — samples patients who can participate in research: sit for an interview, complete a written questionnaire, attend a focus group, use a smartphone app. The most severely affected patients — bedbound, tube-fed, light-sensitive, cognitively unable to sustain conversation, too ill to open an email — are systematically excluded. This exclusion is not correctable through better recruitment methods because the exclusion is inherent to the disease: participation in any research imposes cognitive, physical, and logistical burdens that exceed the capacity of the most severely ill patients.

The result: the qualitative evidence base defines "the patient experience of healthcare" as the experience of the patient subset well enough to describe it. The patients who cannot describe their experience — who are most harmed by healthcare failure, most in need of policy reform, and most likely to have experiences that diverge from the verbalizable minority — are invisible. If the severely affected have qualitatively different experiences (more healthcare invalidation because severe disability is less believed; less invalidation because they are too sick to engage with the healthcare system at all; more financial harm because they cannot work; less access to research-derived knowledge because they cannot participate in studies), the evidence base is biased in an unknown direction.

**Evidence link:** Pendergrast2024SelectionBias: 71% PEM prevents study participation, 67% declined due to PEM risk, 0% of published studies include housebound patients despite 15% prevalence. This study establishes the quantitative participation gap for quantitative research. No equivalent study exists for qualitative research specifically, but the mechanisms are identical — qualitative studies require the same or greater cognitive load. Bontempo2025invalidation, despite being the highest-quality study in the domain, cannot correct for this — it can only meta-synthesize the reports that exist, and those reports exclude the most affected patients.

**Direction of bias:** Unknown. Three possibilities: (a) upward bias — participating patients are less severely affected and have less negative healthcare experiences, so the literature underestimates the true severity of healthcare failure (the most harmed patients are the ones who can't tell us), (b) downward bias — participating patients are more health-engaged and more likely to have sought specialist care, experienced validation, and recovered enough to tell their story, so the literature overestimates positive experiences (the patients who were too discouraged to participate are the ones with the worst experiences), (c) both biases operate in different domains — the participation filter selects for moderate severity (too sick to work, not too sick to speak), so both the mildest patients (who disengage because ME/CFS doesn't dominate their identity) and the severest (too sick to participate) are excluded. The literature describes the moderate-ME/CFS-experience, and we must acknowledge we don't know what the mild or severe experience looks like.

**Falsifiable prediction (for bounding):** A saturation study — repeated qualitative interviews with severe/bedbound patients using the minimal-burden methods possible (proxy reports from caregivers, written-asynchronous responses, short voice messages) — will produce themes that are absent from the existing literature: (a) more extreme invalidation experiences (bedbound patient told they are "giving up"), (b) complete healthcare disconnection (patients who have no contact with the healthcare system at all and are therefore absent from healthcare-experience research), (c) different priorities from the moderate patients in Thornton2025 (energy conservation for survival, not mHealth for improvement). If saturation study produces identical themes, the participation filter is unbiased and the concern is theoretical. If it produces new themes, the qualitative evidence base is incomplete.

**Non-specialist consequence:** The research on "patient experience of ME/CFS healthcare" is really research on "the experience of patients well enough to participate in research." The patients worst off can't tell us what's happening to them — and we don't know what we're missing.

### E2: Cross-Sectional Design Constraint — All Healthcare-Systems Evidence Is a Snapshot of a Dynamic Disease

**Category:** 12 — Evidence Quality Concern
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Concern:** All 13 papers in the evidence base are cross-sectional or qualitative — they describe the healthcare system at a single time point. But ME/CFS is fundamentally dynamic: symptoms fluctuate, PEM episodes accumulate, functional capacity ratchets down over years, and the patient's relationship with the healthcare system evolves. A patient at Year 1 post-onset (seeking diagnosis, hopeful, engaged) has a fundamentally different healthcare experience from the same patient at Year 10 (severely disabled, discouraged, avoiding healthcare). Cross-sectional studies sample patients at different disease stages but treat them as a single population, averaging across disease duration and producing point-estimates of "healthcare experience" that conceal the trajectory.

This creates two specific biases: (a) Survivorship bias in patient-experience research: a patient who reports "moderately satisfied with care" at a cross-sectional survey wave may be bedbound and disconnected from care entirely by the next wave — their voice survives in the data but their deterioration doesn't. If patients who deteriorate most rapidly are most likely to drop out of longitudinal cohorts (and cross-sectional surveys capture only those who can still respond), the survey overestimates satisfaction by excluding the patients for whom healthcare failed most completely. (b) The "engagement window" effect: patients engage with the healthcare system maximally during the diagnostic-odyssey phase (Years 0–5) — seeking diagnosis, trying specialists, applying for disability. After diagnosis (or after disability determination failure), engagement declines sharply. Cross-sectional surveys that preferentially capture engaged patients (because they are recruited through clinics, advocacy organizations, or research registries) over-sample the engaged phase and under-sample the disengaged phase. If disengagement is itself a consequence of healthcare failure (Bontempo2025 avoidance consequence), the cross-sectional design underestimates healthcare failure by missing the patients who gave up.

**Evidence link:** Bontempo2025invalidation: cross-sectional meta-synthesis — identified avoidance as a consequence but cannot measure its duration or trajectory. Samms2025unequalaccess: cross-sectional analysis of diagnosed patients — the undiagnosed (91%) are excluded by design, and among the diagnosed, recent diagnoses may have different characteristics from long-standing diagnoses (but this is not analyzed). No ME/CFS healthcare-experience cohort study exists. The ratchet model (ch42) provides the theoretical framework for disease dynamics that the cross-sectional evidence cannot capture.

**Falsifiable prediction (for bounding):** A longitudinal cohort (PsychOPC-style, n=500 newly diagnosed, 3-year follow-up, 6-month waves) measuring healthcare experience, functional status, PEM frequency, and healthcare utilization at each wave will show: (a) healthcare satisfaction declines over time — patients are more satisfied at diagnosis than at Year 3, (b) healthcare avoidance increases over time — more patients report forgoing care at Year 3 than at baseline, (c) the rate of decline in satisfaction is predicted by the rate of functional decline — patients who get worse faster get more dissatisfied, consistent with healthcare system failure causing or failing to prevent deterioration, (d) the most dissatisfied patients at Year 2 are disproportionately lost to follow-up at Year 3 — the cross-sectional Year 3 survey overestimates satisfaction relative to the full cohort.

**Non-specialist consequence:** All our evidence about "what patients think of healthcare for ME/CFS" comes from patients well enough to answer a survey, at the moment they answered it. We have no idea how that experience changes as the disease gets worse — and we may be systematically missing the patients for whom healthcare failed most completely.

### E3: The Guideline Evidence Paradox — Self-Referential Evidence in a Changing Diagnostic Landscape

**Category:** 12 — Evidence Quality Concern
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Concern:** NICE 2021 was informed by systematic reviews of ME/CFS treatment evidence (GET, CBT, pharmacological interventions). Those systematic reviews depended on RCTs (PACE, FINE, and earlier trials) that enrolled patients using the Oxford (Sharpe1991oxford) or Fukuda (1994) criteria — definitions that do not require post-exertional malaise and are the least specific for ME/CFS among current case definitions. The Smith2014AHRQ review explicitly identified Oxford criteria as inflating heterogeneity and potentially diluting or inflating treatment effects.

The paradox: NICE 2021, which now defines ME/CFS as a PEM-centered disease requiring energy management — rather than an exercise-responsive fatigue condition — was built on a systematic review of evidence from trials that studied patients who may not have had PEM-defined ME/CFS. The guideline's central reversal (no GET) was justified by patient survey evidence of GET harms (Kindlon2014, Geraghty2019) and qualitative reports — not by the RCT evidence that guideline hierarchies normally privilege. NICE 2021 represents a rare case where a guideline body prioritized patient-reported harm data over controlled trial efficacy data because the controlled trials were conducted on the wrong patient population.

This creates an epistemological asymmetry: NICE 2021 is, by patient-advocacy standards, the most evidence-based ME/CFS guideline (because it incorporates patient experience). But by standard evidence-based-medicine hierarchy standards, NICE 2021 is less evidence-based than its 2007 predecessor (because it deprioritized RCT evidence in favor of lower-tier evidence). The paradox is not a flaw in NICE 2021 — the 2007 guideline's RCT evidence was generated on the wrong patients. The paradox is a consequence of the diagnostic criteria problem: when the definition of the disease changes, the old evidence is measuring a different condition but there is no new evidence (because new trials haven't been done). Every evidence-based guideline for ME/CFS published between now and the completion of the first PEM-requiring RCTs will inherit this paradox.

**Evidence link:** Smith2014AHRQ: documented 8 different case definitions across 71 studies, Oxford criteria inflating heterogeneity. NICE 2021 methodology: systematic review of RCT evidence, supplemented by qualitative evidence review and patient testimony. PACE trial used Oxford criteria. Kindlon2014: survey of patient-reported GET harms (n=1,428, 74% reported worsening or no improvement from GET). Geraghty2019: re-analysis demonstrating that PACE's recovery criteria were weaker than the general population's normal range. The diagnostic-criteria-to-guideline-evidence loop has been analyzed in the PACE controversy (ch41) but not as a structural feature of all future guideline development.

**Falsifiable prediction:** The first PEM-requiring RCT (CCC or ICC criteria, blinding, objective primary outcome) of any active treatment for ME/CFS will produce effect sizes that differ directionally from the Oxford/Fukuda trial evidence for the same treatment class — because the Oxford/Fukuda trials included patients who didn't have PEM and therefore responded to treatments (GET, CBT) that patients with PEM-defining ME/CFS find harmful. If the PEM-requiring RCT produces results concordant with the older evidence for a treatment class that patients report as harmful, the guideline-evidence paradox is a theoretical concern with no practical consequence. If the results are discordant, the paradox is a structural problem that invalidates or inverts every guideline recommendation that depends on pre-PEM-era evidence.

**Non-specialist consequence:** The UK's official guideline for ME/CFS was built on trial evidence from studies that may not have studied ME/CFS. NICE 2021 got the right answer — listen to patients — but it arrived at it through a path that would be considered inadequate for any disease with a better evidence base. We're relying on patient testimony because we have nothing better, and we won't have anything better until the first properly diagnosed trials are completed.

### E4: Geographic Evidence Monoculture — UK/US/Canada/Europe Dominate; Global South Absent

**Category:** 12 — Evidence Quality Concern
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Concern:** Every paper in the evidence base originates from the UK, US, Canada, or Western Europe. The KCE Belgian report, Samms2025 UK primary care data, Dimmock2024 US CDC data, Hussein2024 Canadian scoping review, DEGAM German guideline, G-BA German statutory directive, EUROMENE European network, NICE UK guideline — all describe healthcare systems in high-income countries with universal or near-universal healthcare coverage. Zero studies exist from low- or middle-income countries, where ME/CFS prevalence is unknown but likely substantial (post-infectious triggers are universal), healthcare infrastructure is weaker, specialist access is nonexistent, disability systems don't exist, and the illness may be interpreted through entirely different cultural and medical frameworks.

The generalizability limit is severe: everything the chapter says about healthcare systems, policy, and disability applies to rich countries with functional healthcare infrastructure. For the 85% of the world's population living in LMICs, the chapter's evidence base describes a world they don't live in. The chapter's policy recommendations (medical education reform, specialist clinics, guideline implementation, disability system redesign) presuppose a functioning healthcare system with the capacity for reform. In an LMIC context where the nearest physician is hours away by foot, where diagnostic testing is unavailable, where disability benefits don't exist, and where ME/CFS may be interpreted as spirit possession or moral failure rather than biomedical disease — the chapter's recommendations are irrelevant. And we have no evidence to offer in their place.

**Evidence link:** Every paper cited is from a high-income country. The chapter acknowledges this limitation indirectly (the smith2014AHRQ systematic review included no LMIC studies; the Bontempo2025 meta-synthesis included no LMIC qualitative reports). ch43 (global perspectives) is an empty stub — the paper acknowledges the gap but has not yet filled it. The limitation is not a specific flaw in any single study; it's a structural feature of the domain. ME/CFS research requires diagnostic infrastructure (specialists who can apply CCC/ICC criteria, labs that can exclude alternative diagnoses) that doesn't exist in LMICs. Research can't happen where diagnosis can't happen.

**Direction of bias:** The chapter's evidence base describes the best-case scenario for ME/CFS healthcare — patients in rich countries who can access systems designed, however imperfectly, to care for them. The chapter's conclusion that health systems "fail" patients must be read against the counterfactual of what happens in systems that don't even attempt to serve them. From a global perspective, the UK patient who waits 5 years for a diagnosis and is invalidated by half their doctors is receiving care that the median global ME/CFS patient would consider unattainable.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Everything we know about ME/CFS healthcare comes from the richest countries on Earth. For most of the world's ME/CFS patients, we don't even know what the problems are, let alone the solutions. This is a bigger evidence gap than anything inside the chapter — and it's one the chapter cannot address with available data.
