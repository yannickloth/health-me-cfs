# Brainstorm: History of ME/CFS

**Date:** 2026-07-26
**Source papers:** 34 papers from Phase 1 literature search (14 new + 20 existing — Gilliam1938, Sigurdsson1950, RoyalFree1957, Acheson1959, McEvedy1970, Ramsay1986, Holmes1988, Sharpe1991oxford, Buchwald1992, Fukuda1994, Carruthers2003, Carruthers2011, Brurberg2014, White2011pace, Wilshire2018, Vink2015, Geraghty2019GET, IOM2015, Walitt2024NIH, DecodeME2025, NICE2021mecfs, plus additional outbreak and nomenclature sources)

**Key evidence anchors:**
- 14 outbreak clusters (1934–1985) with consistent phenotype, each recorded under local geographic names — pattern of institutional amnesia
- McEvedy's 1970 "mass hysteria" paper set 40-year psychosomatic trajectory; refuted by Ramsay 1986 but not institutionally reversed until IOM 2015
- PACE trial (2011) mid-trial recovery threshold changes inflated efficacy 3–4x; patient-led FOIA requests forced data release; NICE/Cochrane/CDC reversed recommendations by 2021
- Nine successive case definitions (Holmes→Fukuda→CCC→ICC→IOM→NICE) selecting populations varying by factor 2.5+
- IOM 2015: unambiguous repudiation of psychosomatic model; "ME/CFS is not a psychiatric disorder"
- NIH 2024: deep phenotyping validating biological abnormalities despite normal standard labs
- DecodeME 2025: 8 genome-wide significant loci, brain tissue enrichment, heritability 9.5%
- Nomenclature evolution: atypical poliomyelitis → ME → CFS → ME/CFS → SEID — each name reflecting dominant medical framework of era

### Category 1 — Novel Hypotheses

#### 1.1 The Psychosomatic-Lag Hypothesis: A General Law of Unexplained Illness Recognition

**Epistemic rationale:** When an illness cluster appears with (a) consistent clinical phenotype, (b) negative standard laboratory tests, (c) female predominance, and (d) absence of mortality — the default medical response is psychosomatic attribution. This is not a specific failure of ME/CFS medicine but an instance of a general epistemological law. The mechanism: medicine's evidentiary hierarchy privileges positive lab findings over clinical phenomenology; when no lab abnormality is found, the default null model is "patient psychology" rather than "test sensitivity." The 40-year gap between McEvedy (1970) and IOM (2015) is not an outlier but a predictable lag governed by the time required for technology (molecular biology, GWAS) to advance sufficiently to detect the invisible biology.

This hypothesis makes testable predictions about currently contested diagnoses: long COVID, Havana syndrome, chronic Lyme, and any future unexplained illness cluster will follow the same trajectory — initial psychosomatic framing → patient-led advocacy → institutional resistance → slow accumulation of biological evidence → eventual (partial) institutional reversal. The compression factor (time from psychosomatic framing to institutional reversal) should decrease over historical time because of improved research infrastructure, patient internet organization, and institutional learning from prior cases.

**Evidence link:** McEvedy 1970 → IOM 2015 = 45 years of psychosomatic dominance. Long COVID trajectory: initially dismissed as anxiety (2020) → recognized by WHO with ICD code (2021) → NIH RECOVER funded (2021) = ~18 month compression. Gulf War Illness: symptoms reported 1991 → IOM recognition 2010 = 19 years. Fibromyalgia: contested through 1990s → ACR criteria 2010 → still partially contested. The compression is empirically observable. Multiple sclerosis: Charcot described as distinct entity 1868 but psychosomatic framing persisted until MRI demonstrated lesions (1980s) — >100 year lag.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.65 (historical pattern robust; compression trend supported by multiple examples; generalizability to future clusters is inductive)

**Falsifiable prediction:** The next unexplained illness cluster appearing after 2025 will show: (a) initial psychosomatic framing within 2 years of first case recognition, (b) patient-organized advocacy within 3 years, (c) institutional recognition within 10 years (compressed from ME/CFS's 45 years), (d) biological evidence (molecular/genetic) within 15 years. Deviations from this timeline (faster or slower) would falsify the compression component. If a future cluster receives immediate biological recognition with no psychosomatic phase, the "general law" component is falsified.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If psychosomatic lag is a general law, then medical education should teach it explicitly — train clinicians that "normal labs + unexplained symptoms ≠ psychiatric" — rather than waiting for each disease to fight its own battle.

#### 1.2 The Nomenclature Contamination Hypothesis: Disease Names as Research Confounders

**Epistemic rationale:** The 1988 CDC decision to name the disorder "chronic fatigue syndrome" did not merely trivialize the illness — it systematically contaminated the research evidence base. A trivializing name selects for: (a) investigators who take the illness less seriously (selection of research personnel), (b) study designs that use subjective outcomes ("because fatigue is subjective"), (c) patient populations that are heterogeneous in the direction of milder/more psychiatric cases (because the name signals psychiatric rather than neurological disease), (d) lower funding allocations (because "fatigue" is a low-priority symptom compared to "encephalomyelitis"), and (e) lower journal standards for publication (because "chronic fatigue" stories are less scrutinized than "neurological disease" stories). The name is not merely a label — it is a causal agent in shaping the evidence base.

The testable prediction: compare research quality metrics (sample size, blinding, objective outcomes, journal impact factor, funding per study, replication rates) between ME/CFS studies and studies of comparably prevalent neurological diseases (multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's). If nomenclature is causal rather than merely correlational, ME/CFS research should show systematically lower quality on these metrics, and this gap should narrow after the IOM 2015 repudiation of the CFS name.

**Evidence link:** Jason 2002 showed "CFS" elicits more negative attributions than "ME" in vignette studies. Brurberg 2014 documented that case definitions selecting on "fatigue" (Oxford, Fukuda) capture populations 2.5x larger and more heterogeneous than those requiring PEM (CCC). PACE trial used Oxford criteria — the broadest, most fatigue-centric definition — and got inflated treatment effects that reversed when re-analyzed. NICE 2021 reversed GET recommendation after shifting to PEM-centric criteria. The evidence base's quality correlates with the specificity of its diagnostic entry criteria, and the name shapes which criteria investigators choose.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.60 (strong correlational evidence; causal direction supported by vignette experiments; magnitude of contamination effect unknown)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) A bibliometric analysis will show that ME/CFS studies using CCC or IOM criteria have significantly higher rates of objective outcome measures, larger effect sizes for biological endpoints, and higher replication rates than studies using Fukuda or Oxford criteria. (b) After the IOM 2015 report (which explicitly repudiated "CFS" naming), the proportion of funded studies using PEM-requiring criteria will increase year-over-year. (c) A controlled experiment randomizing physicians to an identical case vignette labeled "CFS" vs "ME" vs "SEID" will show significantly different treatment recommendations (GET recommended more for "CFS"), diagnostic confidence, and urgency of specialist referral.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If disease naming is causally important, then "SEID" or "ME" should replace "CFS" in every institutional context (CDC, NIH, journals, medical textbooks) as rapidly as possible — not as a patient preference but as an evidence-quality intervention.

#### 1.3 The Outbreak Amnesia Hypothesis: Geographic Naming as Knowledge Fragmentation

**Epistemic rationale:** The 14 epidemic clusters from 1934 to 1985 shared a consistent clinical phenotype but were each named after their location (Iceland disease, Royal Free disease, Akureyri disease), not after their shared pathophysiology. Geographic naming prevented pattern recognition because: (a) each cluster appeared in literature as a distinct entity with a distinct keyword — a researcher searching for "Iceland disease" would not find "Royal Free disease"; (b) local naming signals "this is a local phenomenon" rather than "this is the nth instance of a global phenomenon"; (c) the local investigators had no incentive to search the literature for similar clusters — their publication incentive was local novelty, not global synthesis.

This is a testable, generalizable hypothesis about medical knowledge accumulation: geographic naming of disease clusters → systematic under-recognition of global patterns → delayed disease discovery. Applied to current medicine: are there disease patterns currently scattered across the literature under local/geographic names that will be recognized as a single disease in the future?

**Evidence link:** Acheson 1959 was the first synthesis — it required an investigator (Acheson) who deliberately searched for and aggregated geographically named clusters. Before Acheson, no single paper connected Los Angeles 1934 to Akureyri 1948 to Adelaide 1949. The knowledge was present in the literature but fragmented across geographic keywords. Modern analog: Kawasaki disease was initially described only in Japan (1967) and took years to be recognized as a global disease — another case of geographic naming delaying global pattern recognition.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.55 (historical pattern documented; generalization to other diseases is inductive; modern search tools may mitigate this failure mode)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) A systematic review of pre-1959 medical literature will identify outbreak reports matching the Acheson phenotype that were not included in Acheson's synthesis, published under additional geographic/descriptive names. (b) A modern search for disease terms that appear in geographically restricted literature clusters (appearing only in one country/region's journals, not yet aggregated into a systematic review) will identify at least one currently unrecognized disease entity. (c) Geographic naming is a stronger predictor of delayed synthesis than any other naming convention (descriptive, eponymous, pathophysiological) — testable by comparing time-to-synthesis across disease naming types.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Medical journal editors and MeSH indexers should flag and cross-reference geographically named disease clusters as "unrecognized syndrome" categories, ensuring that future searches can aggregate them even when the authors did not.

#### 1.4 The Institutional Reversal Sequence Hypothesis: How a Trial's Methodology Reversal Propagates

**Epistemic rationale:** The PACE trial's trajectory from Lancet publication to institutional reversal followed a specific, predictable sequence: (1) publication of a methodologically vulnerable trial in a high-impact journal → (2) independent re-analysis identifies mid-trial methodology changes → (3) patient-led FOIA/data-access campaign forces raw data release → (4) re-analysis under original protocol shows null results → (5) systematic review (Cochrane) withdraws endorsement → (6) national guideline body (NICE) reverses recommendation → (7) public health agency (CDC) removes treatment from guidance.

This sequence is not specific to PACE — it describes a general institutional correction mechanism. Each step is a necessary precondition for the next: without independent re-analysis (step 2), data release (step 3) would not have been pursued; without data release, re-analysis (step 4) would be impossible; without re-analysis showing null results, Cochrane withdrawal (step 5) would lack evidence; without Cochrane withdrawal, NICE reversal (step 6) would contradict evidence synthesis; without NICE reversal, CDC would have no institutional precedent for changing its guidance. The hypothesis is that this sequence generalizes: any major medical reversal initiated by independent re-analysis will propagate through this institutional chain in order, and the time between steps is a function of institutional inertia.

**Evidence link:** PACE reversal sequence documented in chapter (steps 1–7 above). The HRT reversal (Women's Health Initiative 2002) followed a similar sequence: initial trials suggested benefit → WHI RCT showed harm → systematic reviews revised → guidelines reversed. The Vioxx reversal: VIGOR trial published 2000 → independent analyses showed cardiovascular risk → FDA review → Merck withdrawal 2004 → guideline changes. These share the same institutional sequence.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.55 (PACE sequence well-documented; generalization to other reversals is inductive; some reversals skip steps — depends on whether methodology change or safety finding triggered reversal)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In the next major medical reversal triggered by independent re-analysis of a published trial, the sequence will follow steps 1→2→3→4→5→6→7 in order, with each step preceding the next. (b) The time between step 2 (independent re-analysis) and step 7 (public health agency reversal) will be ≤10 years for all future cases. (c) Trials with mid-trial methodology changes will always show this sequence; trials without methodology changes but with genuinely negative results will skip steps 2–4 and proceed directly to guideline revision.

**Non-specialist consequence:** When a high-profile trial shows positive results using a changed methodology, patients should expect that independent re-analysis and institutional reversal will follow — it is not a matter of "if" but "when." The appropriate response is not outrage but systematic demand for data release.

#### 1.5 The Diagnostic Criteria as Successive Filters Hypothesis: Multiplicative Population Reduction

**Epistemic rationale:** Each successive case definition acts as a filter, and the filters compound multiplicatively rather than additively. Starting from a pool of patients with unexplained fatigue: Holmes retains ~80% (only excludes clear psychiatric/somatic cases); Fukuda retains ~60% of Holmes (adds 6-month minimum, 4/8 symptom requirement); CCC retains ~40% of Fukuda (adds mandatory PEM, neurological/autonomic/immune requirements); ICC retains ~30% of CCC (adds energy metabolism impairment, PENE terminology); IOM retains ~80% of CCC (similar structure but slightly relaxed neurological requirements, 2/3 optional). The multiplicative filtering means that if Fukuda captures 100 patients, CCC captures ~40, ICC captures ~12 — a factor of 8 difference between the broadest and most specific definitions used in research.

This is not merely a heterogeneity problem — it is a selection-bias propagation problem. A treatment effect found in a Fukuda-defined population cannot be assumed to apply to a CCC-defined population without explicit testing, because the two populations differ systematically on the cardinal disease feature (PEM). Studies that use broad definitions will systematically fail to replicate when replicated with narrow definitions — not because the treatment doesn't work but because the population changed. This explains the repeated "replication failure" pattern in ME/CFS research.

**Evidence link:** Brurberg 2014 documented Fukuda selects 2.5x more patients than CCC. Holmes 1988 required 8/11 or 6/11+physical criteria — the original 11-symptom list was established by expert consensus without validation. The PACE trial's Oxford criteria selected the broadest population and produced inflated treatment effects that disappeared when stricter recovery definitions (and presumably stricter populations) were applied. The ICC explicitly argued that "ME" and "CFS" are different diseases — if true, studies mixing them are studying two diseases at once.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.65 (empirically testable; Brurberg provides quantitative evidence; multiplicative model is a simple approximation — actual filtering will have interactions)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a single patient cohort assessed under multiple criteria simultaneously, the probability of meeting criteria N given criteria N-1 is met will be ≤0.80, ≤0.70, ≤0.60, ≤0.40 across successive criteria pairs (Holmes→Fukuda→CCC→ICC). (b) Treatment effect sizes in ME/CFS trials will monotonically increase as the criteria become more specific (CCC > Fukuda > Oxford) because more specific criteria select populations with a higher proportion of "true ME/CFS" and fewer "fatigue-dominant" patients. (c) A simulation model using published prevalence and criteria overlap data will predict the population size under any combination of criteria to within ±15% of empirically observed sizes.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Every ME/CFS study should report which case definitions its participants meet (not just the one used for inclusion), so readers can assess how the population would change under a different definition. A drug that "works for CFS" is meaningless without specifying which CFS.

### Category 2 — Research Directions

#### 2.1 Systematic Retrospective Mapping of Pre-Acheson Outbreaks

**Epistemic rationale:** Acheson's 1959 synthesis identified 14 epidemics matching the ME phenotype. But his search was manual and pre-digital. It is plausible that additional outbreak clusters predating 1959 exist in the medical literature under additional names and in journals not indexed by the resources available to Acheson. A systematic retrospective search using modern search tools (PubMed, historical archives, non-English journals) could identify additional clusters that expand the pre-1959 outbreak map and may alter the estimated historical prevalence or geographic distribution.

This is useful not for its direct clinical consequence but because additional outbreak data would constrain models of disease transmission, trigger identification, and natural history that inform current hypotheses about infectious triggers.

**Evidence link:** Acheson 1959 lists 14 outbreaks. The Gilliam 1938 USPHS bulletin was not a peer-reviewed journal article — there may be similar non-journal institutional reports not captured by Acheson. Sigurdsson 1950 was in Am J Hyg (now Am J Epidemiol) — a major journal, suggesting the literature is findable. Post-Acheson, the Incline Village cluster (1984-85) was a new addition not covered by the 1959 synthesis.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.45 (chance of finding new pre-1959 clusters is moderate; methodological challenge of retrospective diagnosis across languages and non-indexed sources is high)

**Falsifiable prediction:** A systematic search of medical literature 1900–1959 using the Acheson phenotype (cluster onset, female predominance, neurological involvement, prolonged fatigue, negative standard testing) will identify ≥3 additional clusters not included in Acheson's 14, with at least one from a non-English source.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If additional outbreaks are found, the historical prevalence and geographic range of ME/CFS expands, strengthening the case that this is a globally distributed, recurring phenomenon rather than a modern Western disease.

#### 2.2 Controlled Vignette Experiment for Nomenclature Effects on Clinical Reasoning

**Epistemic rationale:** The chapter asserts that the name "chronic fatigue syndrome" shaped clinical attitudes and research quality. This is a testable claim. A randomized controlled experiment presenting identical clinical vignettes to physicians with the diagnosis label manipulated (CFS vs ME vs SEID vs postviral fatigue syndrome vs fibromyalgia as comparator) can measure: (a) diagnostic confidence, (b) recommended treatment (GET vs pacing vs pharmacotherapy), (c) perceived patient credibility, (d) anticipated prognosis, (e) referral urgency (psychiatry vs neurology vs infectious disease), (f) interest in seeing such a patient in their practice.

If nomenclature is causal, the label will drive systematic differences in all these outcomes. If nomenclature is merely correlational (a symptom of preexisting attitudes), the label manipulation will not change outcomes.

**Evidence link:** Jason 2002 found "CFS" label elicited more negative attributions than "ME" in undergraduate student samples — but this was a student sample, not physicians. No physician-targeted randomized vignette experiment exists in the literature. The methodology is well-established in medical education research (vignette studies for diagnostic reasoning). Terman 2020 developed validated stigma scales that could serve as outcome measures.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.55 (methodology established; Jason 2002 provides preliminary evidence in non-physician samples; physician-specific effect size unknown)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) The "CFS" label (vs "ME" or "SEID") will produce: 40%+ higher rate of GET recommendation, 30%+ higher rate of psychiatric referral, 20%+ lower rating of "likely organic etiology," 25%+ lower rating of patient credibility. (b) Effect sizes will be largest in physicians who trained before 2015 (IOM report), smaller in physicians trained after 2015, consistent with the psychosomatic-lag hypothesis. (c) The "fibromyalgia" label will produce intermediate outcomes between "CFS" and "ME" — FM is less trivialized than CFS but more contested than a named neurological disease.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If label manipulation robustly changes clinical behavior, medical schools should teach the disease under its least biasing name, and journal editors should require neurologically accurate naming (ME or SEID, not CFS) as a condition of publication.

#### 2.3 Bibliometric Analysis of Case Definition Adoption and Persistence

**Epistemic rationale:** Each new case definition (Holmes 1988, Fukuda 1994, CCC 2003, ICC 2011, IOM 2015, NICE 2021) was intended to replace its predecessors. In practice, older definitions persist in the literature for years after a new definition is published, creating a "definition halo" where multiple definitions are simultaneously in use. This has two consequences: (a) contemporary studies select different populations and produce non-comparable results, and (b) a study's choice of case definition is a signal about the investigators' epistemological stance (fatigue-centric vs PEM-centric vs biological).

A systematic bibliometric analysis mapping: publication year × case definition used × journal impact factor × funding source × author disciplinary background — could quantify how quickly old definitions are abandoned, which definitions cluster in which journals, and whether definition choice predicts study outcomes independent of study design.

**Evidence link:** Brurberg 2014 documented this problem qualitatively — 20 definitions in use, only 2 with adequate content validity. No quantitative analysis of adoption curves or journal clustering exists. Bibliometric methodology for tracking definition/criterion adoption is well-established (e.g., DSM criteria adoption in psychiatry).

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.60 (data exists in published papers; analysis is straightforward; external validity high)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Fukuda 1994 persisted as the most common research definition at least 10 years after CCC 2003 was published, with a median abandonment half-life of >8 years. (b) Studies using Oxford criteria cluster in UK psychiatry/psychology journals; studies using CCC cluster in physiology/immunology journals; studies using Fukuda are evenly distributed. (c) Choice of case definition predicts study conclusion: studies using Oxford criteria are >3x more likely to report positive results for CBT/GET than studies using CCC/ICC.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Systematic reviewers of ME/CFS literature should stratify by case definition as a mandatory subgroup analysis, not pool across definitions. Journals should require authors to state the most specific definition their population meets, not just the one used for recruitment.

#### 2.4 Comparative Analysis of Medical Reversal Epistemology

**Epistemic rationale:** The PACE trial is the most dramatic medical reversal in ME/CFS history, but it is not unique in medicine. Other major reversals — hormone replacement therapy (WHI 2002), Vioxx/rofecoxib (2004), aprotinin (2007), tight glucose control in ICU (2009), stenting for stable coronary disease (COURAGE 2007) — share features with PACE: initial positive trials in high-impact journals, subsequent independent re-analysis or larger trials showing null/harmful effects, and eventual guideline reversal.

A systematic comparative analysis of ~20 major medical reversals could identify: (a) what methodological vulnerabilities are shared across reversal cases, (b) the institutional factors that accelerate or delay reversal, (c) whether patient-led advocacy (as in PACE) speeds reversal compared to physician-led or regulator-led reversal, and (d) whether the epistemological pattern of reversal (methodology change vs safety signal vs replication failure) predicts the speed of institutional response.

**Evidence link:** Prasad 2013 (Mayo Clin Proc) documented 146 medical reversals from 2001–2010, providing a dataset. Herrera-Perez 2019 (eLife) analyzed 396 reversals. PACE reversal is a documented case. Comparative analysis methodology is well-established.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.60 (existing reversal databases; PACE as case study; generalizability depends on how representative PACE is)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) PACE shares ≥4 of 6 common reversal-triggering features: unblinded design, subjective primary outcome, mid-trial methodology change, selective outcome reporting, failure to report harms, conflict of interest. (b) Patient advocacy-accelerated reversals (PACE type) are faster (median 5 years) than purely academic/regulatory reversals (median 10 years) because advocacy bypasses institutional gatekeeping. (c) Reversals triggered by evidence of harm (Vioxx, aprotinin) are faster than reversals triggered by evidence of ineffectiveness (PACE, tight glucose control) — safety trumps efficacy in institutional priority.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Medical education should teach "medical reversal" as a distinct epistemological phenomenon, with case studies (including PACE), so that clinicians can recognize the pattern when they encounter a future reversal and adjust practice faster than institutional guidelines can update.

#### 2.5 Temporal Analysis of ME/CFS Funding as a Function of Institutional Events

**Epistemic rationale:** NIH funding for ME/CFS has remained below 0.03% of the NIH budget, with documented decline since 2024, despite the biological evidence base strengthening (IOM 2015, NIH 2024, DecodeME 2025). This is a paradox: evidence of disease severity and biological basis is accumulating while funding is decreasing. The hypothesis: funding is driven not by evidence but by institutional events (congressional hearings, advocacy campaigns, high-profile publications) that create brief "funding windows" followed by reversion to baseline — a punctuated equilibrium model of research funding.

A temporal regression analysis: monthly NIH funding allocation for ME/CFS × timing of institutional events (IOM report, PACE data release, NIH study publication, DecodeME publication, congressional hearings, advocacy campaigns) — would test whether funding responds transiently to events or tracks the cumulative evidence base.

**Evidence link:** NIH funding data is publicly available (NIH RePORTER). Institutional event dates are documented in the chapter. Punctuated equilibrium models exist in political science (Baumgartner & Jones) and could be adapted to science funding dynamics. No such analysis has been published for ME/CFS.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.50 (data accessible; hypothesis plausible; confounding factors — overall NIH budget, competing disease priorities, political administration changes — complicate causal attribution)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) ME/CFS funding shows a punctuated pattern: brief spikes (6–12 months) following high-visibility events (IOM 2015, PACE data release 2016, NIH study 2024) with reversion to baseline within 24 months. (b) The cumulative published evidence score (quantified as number of Nature/Science/Lancet/NEJM papers per year) does not predict funding level — funding is event-driven, not evidence-driven. (c) Congressional hearing mentions of ME/CFS are the single strongest predictor of subsequent-year funding allocation (stronger than publication count, prevalence estimates, or economic cost estimates).

**Non-specialist consequence:** If funding is event-driven rather than evidence-driven, advocacy organizations should concentrate resources on generating visible institutional events rather than accumulating additional evidence — the bottleneck is attention, not proof.

### Category 7 — Mathematical Model Extensions

#### 7.1 Bayesian Outbreak Aggregation Model: Institutional Learning Under Geographic Fragmentation

**Epistemic rationale:** The outbreak amnesia hypothesis (1.3) can be formalized as a Bayesian learning model. Each outbreak investigator i observes clinical data D_i from a local cluster. If the investigator assigns the cluster a local name ("Iceland disease"), the prior probability of connecting it to other clusters is low because the keywords don't match. The institutional prior P(global disease | local cluster) is very low when clusters have geographic names.

The model: P(D_i | θ) × P(θ) where θ = {global disease present, no global disease}. The prior P(θ = global) starts high if clusters share a name, low if they don't. The posterior after observing N outbreaks: if names differ, the evidence accumulates additively but slowly because each outbreak is modeled independently; if names are shared, the evidence accumulates multiplicatively because each outbreak updates the same prior. The delay-to-recognition τ is a function of the naming convention: τ(geographic) >> τ(descriptive) > τ(pathophysiological).

This can be simulated: parameterize the model with historical outbreak data, estimate τ under geographic vs shared naming, and test whether the actual 25-year delay (1934 Los Angeles → 1959 Acheson synthesis) falls within the predicted distribution.

**Evidence link:** Acheson 1959 synthesis occurred 25 years after the first documented outbreak — the model should predict delays in this range under geographic naming. The model can be validated against other diseases that followed similar trajectories (Kawasaki: Japan-only description 1967, global recognition ~1980: 13-year delay). Bayesian cognitive models of concept learning (Tenenbaum 1999) provide the formal framework.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.40 (theoretical model; historical calibration data is limited; actual cognition of investigators is more complex than Bayesian model)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Simulated delay-to-synthesis under geographic naming is 20–30 years for parameters calibrated to 14-outbreak Acheson dataset — matching historical delay. (b) Simulated delay-to-synthesis under shared descriptive naming is 5–10 years (factor 2–6x shorter). (c) The model predicts that the minimum number of outbreaks needed for recognition is 8–12 under geographic naming vs 3–5 under shared naming.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If the model shows that naming is the bottleneck, then MeSH and ICD should assign provisional shared codes to unexplained disease clusters at the time of first publication, not wait for synthesis. The cost of synthesis delay is preventable.

#### 7.2 Multiplicative Filter Model of Case Definition Population Reduction

**Epistemic rationale:** The diagnostic criteria as successive filters hypothesis (1.5) can be formalized as a simple multiplicative probability model. Define P_i as the probability that a patient meeting criteria C_{i-1} also meets criteria C_i. The expected population under criteria N is: N_0 × Π_{i=1}^{N} P_i, where N_0 is the population with unexplained fatigue of sufficient duration.

Each P_i represents the proportion of patients retained by the additional specificity of criteria C_i relative to C_{i-1}. Using published data on criteria overlap (Brurberg 2014, Jason et al.), estimate P_i for each successive criteria pair. The model predicts population sizes under any combination of criteria and can identify which criteria components do most of the filtering (likely: mandatory PEM, which is the CCC's main addition to Fukuda).

Extension: the model can incorporate non-independence — some filters are redundant (e.g., the "neurological impairment" component of CCC and ICC may capture the same patients already captured by mandatory PEM), reducing the effective number of independent filters.

**Evidence link:** Brurberg 2014 provides the primary overlap estimate (Fukuda captures 2.5x CCC patients). Jason 2004 and Nacul 2011 provide additional criteria overlap data. The multiplicative model is a first-order approximation; a more sophisticated latent class model would capture correlations between criteria components.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.55 (model structure is straightforward; parameter estimates from literature exist but have wide confidence intervals; multiplicative assumption is simplistic but testable)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) A cohort assessed under Holmes, Fukuda, CCC, ICC, and IOM criteria simultaneously will show population sizes whose ratios are within ±20% of the multiplicative product prediction. (b) The "PEM filter" (CCC over Fukuda) will account for >50% of the total population reduction across all criteria pairs — it is the single largest filter. (c) Adding the ICC "energy production/transport impairment" criterion to CCC will filter an additional 30–50% of the CCC population, indicating that energy metabolism is a separable dimension from neurological impairment.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If the multiplicative model is validated, research review panels could estimate the effective sample size of a proposed study under stricter criteria and require justification when broad criteria are used without adjustment for population heterogeneity.

#### 7.3 Event-Driven Punctuated Equilibrium Model of Research Funding

**Epistemic rationale:** Research direction 2.5 hypothesizes that ME/CFS funding follows a punctuated equilibrium pattern driven by institutional events rather than cumulative evidence. This can be formalized as: F(t) = F_baseline + Σ_i A_i × exp(-λ(t - t_i)) for t > t_i, where F(t) is funding at time t, F_baseline is the steady-state funding level (sub-0.03% of NIH budget), A_i is the amplitude of funding spike after event i at time t_i, and λ is the decay rate (rate of reversion to baseline).

Fit this model to NIH RePORTER data for ME/CFS (and comparator diseases: MS, lupus, FM) using known event dates (IOM 2015 at t_1, PACE data release 2016 at t_2, NIH study 2024 at t_3, DecodeME 2025 at t_4). Estimate A_i and λ for each event type. Test whether the model fits ME/CFS better than comparator diseases (which may not show the same event-driven pattern because their funding is more institutionalized).

**Evidence link:** NIH RePORTER provides annual funding data. Event dates are documented. Punctuated equilibrium models from political science (Baumgartner & Jones 1993, "Agendas and Instability in American Politics") provide the theoretical framework. The exponential decay assumption can be relaxed — a more flexible decay kernel can be tested.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.40 (data accessible; model is simple; confounding by overall NIH budget trends and political cycles is significant; small number of events limits statistical power)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) The punctuated model fits ME/CFS funding data significantly better (ΔAIC > 4) than a linear trend model or a constant-funding null model. (b) λ estimates: decay half-life is 12–24 months for publication events, 24–36 months for congressional hearing events. (c) Comparator diseases (MS, lupus) show no significant event-driven dynamics — their funding follows smooth trends, consistent with institutionalized research programs.

### Category 8 — Cross-Disease Bridges

#### 8.1 Multiple Sclerosis: The MRI as Epistemological Watershed

**Epistemic rationale:** Multiple sclerosis followed a trajectory parallel to ME/CFS but with a different outcome. MS was described by Charcot in 1868 as a distinct clinical-pathological entity, but through the early 20th century, many MS presentations were dismissed as "hysteria" because neurological symptoms were transient (relapsing-remitting course), disproportionately affected young women, and standard tests were often normal between relapses. The introduction of MRI in the 1980s transformed MS from a clinically diagnosed, often-contested entity into a visually confirmed, incontestable one — the T2/FLAIR hyperintensity is visible, objective, and persuasive.

ME/CFS lacks an MRI-equivalent — a single test that demonstrates objective abnormality visible to both clinician and patient. This comparison isolates the variable that determines whether a disease gets psychosomatic or biological treatment: not evidence quality per se, but the existence of a test that produces an image or number that non-specialists can understand as "abnormal." GWAS (DecodeME 2025) provides genetic evidence but is not a diagnostic test — it cannot show that a given individual has ME/CFS. The holy grail is an imaging biomarker or blood test with the same "persuasive power" as the MS MRI.

**Evidence link:** MS history: Murray 2005 ("Multiple Sclerosis: The History of a Disease"). Charcot 1868 initial description; psychosomatic framing common through mid-20th century; MRI introduced 1981 (Young et al., Lancet). ME/CFS: NIH 2024 found abnormalities only on deep phenotyping, not standard imaging; DecodeME found genetic associations but not diagnostic markers.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.65 (historical parallel well-documented; MRI as transformative test is well-established; application to ME/CFS is inductive)

**Falsifiable prediction:** When/if ME/CFS acquires an objective diagnostic test with the persuasive power of MRI (visible brain/body abnormality), the rate of "psychosomatic" attribution by frontline clinicians will decline by >50% within 5 years — matched to the MS trajectory where psychosomatic framing declined within a decade of MRI introduction.

**Non-specialist consequence:** ME/CFS research should prioritize development of a visually compelling, clinically deployable diagnostic test (not just research-grade biomarkers) because the history of MS shows that test visibility, not just test accuracy, determines clinical acceptance.

#### 8.2 Fibromyalgia: The Compressed Psychosomatic-to-Biological Trajectory

**Epistemic rationale:** Fibromyalgia followed the same psychosomatic→biological trajectory as ME/CFS but on a compressed timescale: described as "fibrositis" in the early 20th century (implying inflammation, never demonstrated), reclassified as "fibromyalgia" (pain, not inflammation) and contested through the 1980s−1990s, received ACR diagnostic criteria in 1990 (revised 2010), and gained biological plausibility through central sensitization theory and fMRI studies showing amplified pain processing. Time from initial description to ACR criteria: ~80 years (vs ME/CFS's ~55 years to CCC 2003). Time from ACR criteria to biological evidence acceptance: ~15 years (vs ME/CFS's ongoing incomplete transition).

The comparison reveals that FM's faster trajectory may be attributable to: (a) FM has a simpler, more specific cardinal symptom (widespread pain is less ambiguous than "fatigue"), (b) FM's fMRI findings (amplified pain processing) are more intuitively compelling than ME/CFS's complex multi-system abnormalities, (c) FM's pharmaceutical industry interest (pregabalin/Lyrica, duloxetine/Cymbalta, milnacipran/Savella all gained FDA approval for FM) provided commercial pressure for legitimacy. ME/CFS lacks any FDA-approved drug, and thus lacks the pharmaceutical industry as an ally in legitimacy-building.

**Evidence link:** FM history: Inanici & Yunus 2004 (Curr Pain Headache Rep), Wolfe 1990 ACR criteria, Gracely 2002 fMRI study. FDA approvals: pregabalin 2007, duloxetine 2008, milnacipran 2009. ME/CFS has zero FDA-approved treatments.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.60 (parallel well-documented; compressed timeline vs ME/CFS is empirically supported; pharmaceutical industry as legitimacy vector is plausible but correlational)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) FDA approval of any ME/CFS drug will be followed within 5 years by measurable reduction in "psychosomatic" attributions by physicians, improvement in disability claim approval rates, and increase in NIH funding allocation — the FM trajectory shows that drug approval is a legitimacy ratchet. (b) Diseases with at least one FDA-approved drug will have shorter psychosomatic-to-biological transition times than diseases with zero FDA-approved drugs, controlling for prevalence and severity.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Pharmaceutical industry engagement, while often viewed skeptically by patient communities, may be an underappreciated accelerator of disease legitimacy. The absence of FDA-approved ME/CFS treatments is not just a treatment gap — it is a legitimacy gap that perpetuates the psychosomatic framing.

#### 8.3 Long COVID: The Compressed Trajectory as Natural Experiment

**Epistemic rationale:** Long COVID is undergoing the same psychosomatic→biological trajectory as ME/CFS, but with all time constants compressed by a factor of ~10–30x: initial dismissal as anxiety (2020, months after pandemic onset) → patient-organized advocacy and symptom documentation (2020) → WHO ICD code (2021, 1 year after recognition) → major NIH funding (RECOVER, $1.15B, 2021) → biological evidence accumulating (2021–2025). This compression creates a natural experiment: if the psychosomatic lag hypothesis (1.1) is correct, Long COVID should show the same trajectory but faster, and the institutional learning from ME/CFS should be visible in the speed of Long COVID's recognition.

Key differences: Long COVID has (a) a clear temporal anchor (SARS-CoV-2 infection) that ME/CFS lacks — post-infectious ME/CFS exists but the infection is often not identified or documented; (b) a massive patient cohort (millions globally within months) vs ME/CFS's gradual accumulation; (c) preexisting ME/CFS advocacy infrastructure that immediately recognized and articulated Long COVID's legitimacy. These differences predict the compression factor.

The comparison also reveals what happens when two patient populations with partially overlapping biology (estimated 40–50% of Long COVID patients meet ME/CFS criteria) are treated by radically different institutional responses: ME/CFS patients watch Long COVID receive in 3 years what they haven't received in 90 years — funding, clinical trials, institutional legitimacy. This asymmetry is itself an object of study.

**Evidence link:** Long COVID trajectory documented: WHO ICD-10 code U09.9 (2021), NIH RECOVER funded $1.15B (2021), Nature/Science/Lancet publications accumulating. ME/CFS trajectory: IOM 2015 (80 years after first outbreak), NIH funding <$15M/year vs RECOVER's $1.15B. Overlap: Davis 2021 (EClinicalMedicine) documented PEM in Long COVID; Komaroff 2023 (Front Med) reviewed overlapping mechanisms.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.70 (trajectories well-documented; compression factor quantifiable; causal inference about "what drove compression" is complex — pandemic urgency, political pressure, healthcare worker prevalence all contribute)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) The time from first case recognition to institutional recognition (ICD code + major funding) will be 2–3 years for Long COVID vs 80 years for ME/CFS. (b) If ME/CFS institutional learning contributed to this compression, then Long COVID advocacy infrastructure will show measurable inheritance from ME/CFS advocacy (shared organizations, shared leaders, shared advocacy tactics). (c) As Long COVID ages (5+, 10+ years post-pandemic), the psychosomatic framing will creep back in proportion to the proportion of patients whose standard labs remain normal — testing the hypothesis that "normal labs → psychosomatic" is a constant force, not a one-time historical event.

**Non-specialist consequence:** The Long COVID/ME/CFS asymmetry is an argument for equity: the institutional machinery that mobilized for Long COVID should be retroactively applied to ME/CFS, not just because it's fair, but because the biology overlaps and treating one without the other is scientifically inefficient.

#### 8.4 Gulf War Illness: The Government-Contested Diagnosis

**Epistemic rationale:** Gulf War Illness (GWI) represents a variant of the psychosomatic trajectory where the gatekeeper is not the medical establishment but the government/military establishment. Veterans returning from the 1990–91 Gulf War reported a consistent syndrome (fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, pain, gastrointestinal symptoms) that was initially attributed to stress/PTSD by the Department of Defense and VA. The trajectory: symptoms reported 1991 → contested through 1990s (DoD denied existence) → IOM committee reviews (1996, 2000, 2006, 2010) → IOM 2010 report: "Gulf War illness is a real, serious condition affecting at least 175,000 veterans" → limited VA disability recognition.

GWI shares with ME/CFS: (a) consistent phenotype across a defined exposed population, (b) initial attribution to psychology (stress/PTSD), (c) negative standard laboratory findings, (d) slow institutional recognition over decades, (e) patient-led advocacy forcing institutional action. GWI differs: (a) specific exposure (deployment to Gulf War) provided a temporal/geographic anchor, (b) Department of Defense as institutional adversary rather than medical journals, (c) political pressure from veteran organizations is more powerful than patient advocacy (veterans are a politically protected class; ME/CFS patients are not).

The comparison isolates the role of political power in disease recognition: GWI achieved IOM recognition in 19 years; ME/CFS took 81 years. The difference is not in the strength of evidence (arguably comparable) but in the political capital of the affected population.

**Evidence link:** GWI trajectory: IOM 2010 "Gulf War and Health, Volume 8"; Research Advisory Committee on Gulf War Veterans' Illnesses (RAC-GWVI) reports; Fukuda 1998 JAMA case definition. ME/CFS trajectory for comparison: chapter content. Political power differential: veterans' organizations (American Legion, VFW) have congressional testimony rights, dedicated VA budget line items, and electoral significance.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.65 (trajectories well-documented; political power hypothesis is plausible but correlational; alternative explanations — GWI's defined exposure window, higher prevalence — may account for some of the acceleration)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Diseases affecting politically powerful groups (veterans, children, first responders) will achieve institutional recognition faster than diseases affecting politically weak groups (ME/CFS, chronic Lyme, fibromyalgia) controlling for prevalence, severity, and evidence quality. (c) The "9/11 first responder illness" trajectory will show faster recognition than GWI (greater political sympathy), and ME/CFS will show the slowest of all four.

#### 8.5 POTS: The Tilt-Table Test as Epistemological Gatekeeper

**Epistemic rationale:** Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) — a condition frequently comorbid with ME/CFS — largely avoided the psychosomatic trajectory despite also affecting predominantly young women. The key: the tilt-table test produces an objective, visible, immediately abnormal finding — heart rate increases ≥30 bpm within 10 minutes of upright tilt. The physician sees the abnormality in real time. POTS has a "moment of truth" that ME/CFS lacks.

This comparison isolates the variable that determines psychosomatic vs biological treatment. It is not: disease severity (ME/CFS is arguably more disabling), prevalence (comparable), female predominance (both), or negative standard labs (both have normal resting labs). The variable is: does the disease have a test that produces a visible, unambiguous abnormality that cannot be attributed to patient effort or psychology? POTS's tilt-table test is such a test; ME/CFS's CPET (2-day cardiopulmonary exercise testing) shows abnormality (reduced VO₂ at VT on day 2) but is not widely used, not visually intuitive, and is effort-dependent (interpretable as deconditioning). The "persuasiveness" of a test, not its accuracy, determines clinical acceptance.

**Evidence link:** POTS: Schondorf & Low 1993 (Mayo Clin Proc) established tilt-table diagnostic criteria; Freeman 2011 (Heart Rhythm) consensus statement. ME/CFS CPET: Keller 2014 (J Transl Med), Snell 2013 (Fatigue Biomed Health Behav) — 2-day CPET shows reduced work at anaerobic threshold on day 2, but this test is not standard clinical practice and is interpreted by some as deconditioning. POTS prevalence in ME/CFS: 27–50% (Rowe 2016, Reynolds 2014).

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.55 (parallel well-documented; tilt-table vs CPET comparison is plausible but inductive; the "test persuasiveness" variable has not been formally studied)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Among patients with overlapping POTS+ME/CFS, the POTS diagnosis is accepted by clinicians faster and with less skepticism than the ME/CFS diagnosis — measurable as: time from symptom onset to POTS diagnosis < time to ME/CFS diagnosis in the same patient. (b) In a physician vignette experiment, a patient case described with "positive tilt-table test => POTS" vs "reduced VO₂ on day-2 CPET" vs "normal labs, clinical diagnosis of ME/CFS" will show: POTS case receives highest biological attribution, ME/CFS with CPET case receives intermediate attribution, ME/CFS without test receives lowest attribution — the objective test, not the disease, drives credibility. (c) When ME/CFS acquires a POTS-equivalent diagnostic test (visible, unambiguous, effort-independent), the "POTS gap" (faster diagnosis for POTS than ME/CFS in the same patient) will close.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Investment in developing a POTS-equivalent diagnostic test for ME/CFS — a bedside test that produces an unambiguous visible abnormality — is the highest-leverage research priority, because test persuasiveness is the gatekeeper variable for clinical acceptance based on the POTS comparison.

### Category 9 — Diagnostic/Biomarker Ideas

#### 9.1 The PEM Criterion as Taxonomic Lever: Retrospective Reclassification of the Evidence Base

**Epistemic rationale:** Making post-exertional malaise a universal mandatory criterion (as CCC, ICC, IOM, and NICE all require) would retroactively reclassify the entire ME/CFS treatment evidence base. Every trial that used Oxford or Fukuda criteria (which did not require PEM) included patients who may not have had PEM and therefore may not have had ME/CFS under current criteria. The PACE trial is the clearest example: 641 patients recruited under Oxford criteria; unknown what proportion would meet IOM or NICE criteria.

The research proposal: re-contact PACE trial participants (or any large Oxford/Fukuda cohort) and re-assess them against IOM/NICE criteria. Stratify the original trial results by: (a) patients who retrospectively meet current PEM-requiring criteria vs (b) patients who do not. If the positive CBT/GET effects are driven primarily by group (b) (non-PEM fatigue patients), then the conclusion that CBT/GET "works for ME/CFS" is false — it works for a mixed population that includes non-ME/CFS patients. This retrospective reclassification can be applied to the entire treatment literature for which patient-level data exists.

**Evidence link:** Brurberg 2014: Fukuda captures 2.5x more patients than CCC, so ~60% of Fukuda-defined cohorts may not meet CCC criteria. PACE data is publicly available (released 2016). NICE 2021: systematic review using GRADE methodology, but did not have access to patient-level reclassification data — could not stratify results by IOM/NICE criteria status within older trials.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.55 (conceptually straightforward; requires access to patient-level data from older trials; re-contact of PACE participants may be impossible due to time elapsed; retrospective PEM assessment from medical records may be unreliable)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In any large Oxford/Fukuda cohort, ≥40% of participants will NOT meet IOM/NICE criteria (missing mandatory PEM). (b) Treatment effect sizes (CBT/GET vs control) will be significantly larger in the subgroup that does NOT meet IOM/NICE criteria than in the subgroup that does. (c) The PACE trial's positive CBT/GET results will be entirely attributable to the subgroup without mandatory PEM — when re-analyzed on the PEM-positive subgroup alone, CBT/GET will show null or minimal effects.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If retrospective reclassification shows that CBT/GET effects are driven by non-PEM patients, all remaining institutional endorsements of GET (in countries that have not yet reversed) should be withdrawn. The PACE trial should be flagged in PubMed with a correction noting that the study population is not representative of ME/CFS as currently defined.

#### 9.2 The Diagnostic Criteria as a Rosetta Stone for Biomarker Discovery

**Epistemic rationale:** Different case definitions select different populations — a problem for research homogeneity, but potentially a tool for biomarker discovery. If Fukuda criteria capture a heterogeneous population, and CCC captures a more specific subset, then biological markers that differ between these populations (Fukuda-only vs CCC-overlap) can identify the biological dimension that PEM adds to disease definition.

Design: recruit a large cohort and assess under all major criteria simultaneously (Fukuda, CCC, ICC, IOM, NICE). Perform multi-omics profiling (transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics). Identify molecular signatures that differ between: (a) healthy controls vs Fukuda-positive patients (broad CFS signature), (b) Fukuda-positive/CCC-negative vs CCC-positive patients (the "PEM dimension" signature), (c) CCC-positive/ICC-negative vs ICC-positive patients (the "energy metabolism dimension" signature). These signatures would identify the molecular basis of each definitional criterion and validate whether the criteria are cutting at biological joints.

**Evidence link:** DecodeME 2025 provided genetic data on a broadly defined ME/CFS population (does not stratify by criteria). NIH 2024 studied 17 strictly defined post-infectious patients (rich phenotyping but n=17, no criteria comparison). No study has systematically compared multi-omics profiles across multiple case definitions in a single cohort.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.50 (design is straightforward; recruiting a cohort large enough for multi-omics across criteria subgroups is resource-intensive; existing data could be pooled but requires harmonization)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) The PEM dimension (CCC-positive vs CCC-negative/Fukuda-positive) will be associated with transcriptomic signatures of mitochondrial dysfunction, immune dysregulation, or neuroinflammation — not with signatures of depression or deconditioning. (b) The energy metabolism dimension (ICC-positive vs CCC-positive/ICC-negative) will be associated with metabolomic signatures of impaired glycolysis, β-oxidation, or oxidative phosphorylation. (c) At least one molecular signature will show a step-function relationship with criteria specificity (Fukuda < CCC < ICC) rather than a binary presence/absence — indicating a continuous biological severity dimension that the criteria progressively filter.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If criteria components map to distinct molecular signatures, the heterogeneity problem becomes a biomarker discovery tool — the criteria themselves contain information about which molecular pathways are disrupted in which patients, enabling personalized treatment selection.

#### 1.6 The Counterfactual Nomenclature Hypothesis: What If the CDC Had Chosen Differently in 1988?

**Epistemic rationale:** The 1988 Holmes criteria made two decisions: (1) define the disease operationally, (2) name it "chronic fatigue syndrome." These were separable. Contingency plays a causal role in medical history. What if the CDC had chosen "myalgic encephalomyelitis" in 1988 instead of CFS? The counterfactual can be modeled by comparing ME/CFS's history with diseases that retained their neurological names despite similar uncertainty at the time of naming: multiple sclerosis (uncertain etiology through the 20th century, retained a pathophysiological name), Parkinson's disease (named after James Parkinson's 1817 "An Essay on the Shaking Palsy" — a descriptive name, not trivializing), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (named by Charcot in 1874 based on pathological findings, retained despite uncertain etiology).

The hypothesis: diseases named with pathophysiological descriptors (encephalomyelitis, sclerosis, palsy) receive meaningfully different institutional treatment (funding, journal placement, clinical credibility) than diseases named with trivializing or symptom-generic descriptors (fatigue, irritable, chronic). If true, the 1988 naming decision was a contingent event that causally shaped 40 years of history — and the lesson is that disease naming is a high-stakes institutional decision, not a cosmetic preference.

This is formally untestable (the counterfactual cannot be run) but can be approximated through comparative historical analysis.

**Evidence link:** The MS/ME comparison (8.1). The trajectory of ME/CFS in the UK (where "ME" persisted alongside "CFS" in clinical practice) vs the US (where "CFS" was adopted wholesale by CDC/medical education) may provide a partial natural experiment — did UK patients diagnosed under "ME" have different clinical experiences than US patients diagnosed under "CFS"? This would require comparative sociological research.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.35 (formally untestable; comparative analysis provides indirect evidence; contingency in medical naming is understudied)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (Approximation) In the UK, where "ME" and "CFS" have been used concurrently, patient outcomes (diagnostic delay, treatment received, perceived physician credibility) differ between patients whose initial diagnosis was labeled "ME" vs "CFS," controlling for severity. If no difference is found, the counterfactual naming hypothesis is weakened.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If disease naming is contingent and causal, then medical institutions (CDC, WHO, NIH) should have formal criteria for disease naming — not ad hoc decisions — and current names that use trivializing descriptors should be revisited systematically.

#### 1.7 The Ramsay-McEvedy Debate as Archetype: The Epistemological Structure of Disease Contestation

**Epistemic rationale:** The Ramsay-McEvedy debate (1970–1986) is not merely a historical episode — it is a perfect archetype of the structure of disease contestation. The debaters represent two positions: Ramsay (clinician, first-hand observation, organic model) and McEvedy (epidemiologist, desk reinterpretation of published data, psychosomatic model). The same structure repeats across contested diagnoses: clinician-observers argue for organicity based on direct patient contact; epidemiologists/reviewers argue for psychogenicity based on aggregate data and negative tests.

This structure is generalizable. In fibromyalgia: rheumatologists treating patients argued for biological basis; reviewers at a distance argued for psychosocial explanation. In chronic Lyme: Lyme-literate physicians argued for persistent infection; IDSA reviewers argued for "post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome" (psychosomatic). In long COVID: frontline physicians treating long COVID clinics argue for biological basis; editorialists argue for anxiety/deconditioning.

The hypothesis: this is not a coincidence of personalities but a structural feature of medical epistemology. Clinicians who see individual patients update their priors on organicity (because they observe the consistency of the phenotype and the human cost). Epidemiologists who see aggregate data without patient contact maintain their priors on psychogenicity (because aggregate data without lab abnormalities is indistinguishable from somatic symptom disorder at the population level). The resolution of the debate requires bridging this epistemological gap — which is why the IOM 2015 committee included both clinicians and epidemiologists, and why patient testimony was included in their review.

**Evidence link:** Ramsay-McEvedy as described in chapter. Fibromyalgia: Hadler vs Wolfe debates (1990s). Chronic Lyme: ILADS vs IDSA. Long COVID: editorial dismissals (BMJ 2021: "long COVID may be driven by anxiety") vs clinic-based reports. The pattern repeats across diseases.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.60 (pattern well-documented across multiple diseases; structural explanation is plausible; alternative: clinician "advocacy bias" explains the pattern equally well)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a systematic review of diseases with contested biological vs psychosomatic status, the proportion of "organic" attribution will be positively correlated with the proportion of authors who have direct patient contact (r > 0.5). (b) Epidemiologists/public health researchers will be more likely to endorse psychosomatic explanations than clinicians for any given contested disease, controlling for disease-specific knowledge and publication volume. (c) Inclusion of patient testimony in guideline committees (as IOM did) will increase the probability of a biological disease conclusion compared to committees that rely exclusively on published literature.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Guideline committees for contested diseases should include patients and frontline clinicians as full voting members, not as observers or testimonial witnesses, because their epistemological position (direct observation) provides evidence not captured in the published literature of aggregate data.

### Category 7 — Mathematical Model Extensions (continued)

#### 7.4 Latent Class Model of ME/CFS Populations Defined by Successive Criteria

**Epistemic rationale:** The multiplicative filter model (7.2) is a first-order approximation. A more sophisticated approach: model the patient population as a mixture of latent classes defined by symptom patterns (PEM, cognitive dysfunction, pain, sleep disturbance, autonomic symptoms, immune symptoms, energy metabolism impairment). Each case definition selects a different mixture of these latent classes. The Fukuda definition selects classes 1–3 (fatigue-dominant and PEM-dominant and mixed); Oxford selects classes 1–2 (fatigue-dominant only); CCC selects classes 2–4 (PEM-dominant with neurological features); ICC selects class 4 only (severe PEM with energy metabolism features).

This can be estimated using latent class analysis (LCA) on a large patient-reported symptom dataset. The resulting latent classes can then be mapped to existing case definitions to identify which definitions capture which classes. The model would show that the heterogeneity of ME/CFS research is not random noise — it is predictable structure reflecting the mixture of latent classes sampled by each definition.

**Evidence link:** Latent class analysis is well-established in psychiatry (e.g., PTSD symptom clusters, depression subtypes) and has been applied to ME/CFS by Jason 2014 (J Clin Psychol) who identified 3 latent classes in ME/CFS patients: severe, moderate with pain, and moderate without pain. No LCA has been published that maps latent classes to the major case definitions simultaneously.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.45 (methodology established; source data exists from large cohort studies; class-definition mapping is novel and requires validation)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) LCA of ME/CFS symptoms will identify 3–5 latent classes, with at least one class characterized by PEM+neurological+autonomic symptoms (the "core ME/CFS" class) and at least one class characterized by fatigue without PEM (the "chronic fatigue" class). (b) Oxford criteria will select primarily from the non-PEM classes; Fukuda from a mixture; CCC from PEM-dominant classes; ICC from the most severe PEM+energy metabolism class. (c) Biological differences (transcriptomic, metabolomic) between latent classes will be larger than biological differences between Oxford-vs-Fukuda-vs-CCC selected populations, because latent classes cut at biological joints while criteria definitions cut at arbitrary thresholds.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If latent classes exist and map to distinct biology, the goal of ME/CFS research shifts from "find a biomarker for ME/CFS" to "find a biomarker for each latent class" — personalized medicine starts with recognizing that one diagnostic label may capture multiple biological entities.

### Category 8 — Cross-Disease Bridges (continued)

#### 8.6 Chronic Lyme Disease: The Contested Post-Infectious Syndrome

**Epistemic rationale:** Chronic Lyme disease (also called post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome, PTLDS) follows the same psychosomatic trajectory as ME/CFS but with a distinct nuance: the pathogen is known (Borrelia burgdorferi), the acute phase is treatable (antibiotics), but the chronic phase is contested. The IDSA position: PTLDS is likely autoimmune or post-infectious, not persistent infection, and further antibiotics are harmful. The ILADS position: persistent infection causes chronic symptoms, and long-term antibiotics are beneficial. This debate has been unusually public and contentious, with state-level legislation in the US mandating insurance coverage for long-term antibiotic treatment and the IDSA facing antitrust investigation.

The Lyme/ME/CFS comparison: both are post-infectious syndromes with fatigue, pain, and cognitive dysfunction; both are contested as "real" diseases; both have patient communities that actively challenge institutional medical authority. But Lyme has a known pathogen and an FDA-approved diagnostic test (two-tier serology), while ME/CFS has neither — yet both remain contested. This suggests that knowing the pathogen is not sufficient to resolve the psychosomatic debate. The debate is about the chronic phase, not the acute trigger.

**Evidence link:** IDSA guidelines: Wormser 2006 (Clin Infect Dis), Lantos 2021. ILADS guidelines: Cameron 2004 (Expert Rev Anti Infect Ther). State legislation: Connecticut, Rhode Island, Massachusetts, and others require insurance coverage for long-term Lyme treatment. Antitrust investigation: Connecticut Attorney General 2006–2008. ME/CFS comparison: chapter content.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.55 (parallel well-documented; known pathogen vs unknown pathogen comparison is informative; chronic Lyme's political/legal dimension is different from ME/CFS's primarily medical dimension)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) If knowing the acute trigger (Borrelia for Lyme) does not resolve psychosomatic contestation of the chronic phase, then identifying a causative agent for ME/CFS (if one exists) will not resolve ME/CFS contestation either — the psychosomatic dynamic operates at the chronic-phase level, not the acute-trigger level. (b) PTLDS and ME/CFS patients will show overlapping biological signatures (cytokine profiles, gene expression, PEM physiology), consistent with a shared post-infectious mechanism independent of the specific pathogen. (c) Diseases with known pathogens but contested chronic phases (Lyme, post-EBV, long COVID) will all show measurable PEM — the shared systemic response to post-infectious states.

**Non-specialist consequence:** The chronic Lyme experience suggests that the ME/CFS community should not invest excessive hope in finding "the pathogen" — knowing the trigger does not resolve the chronic-phase debate. The chronic phase's contested status is a political/epistemological problem, not a microbiological one.

### Category 10 — Reasons This Historical Framing May NOT Matter for ME/CFS

#### 10.1 Patients Need Treatments, Not History

**Argument:** The chapter meticulously documents 90 years of epistemological evolution — from 1934 Los Angeles to 2025 DecodeME. ### Category 11 — Null Hypothesis Assessment

#### 11.1 H₀: The History of ME/CFS Does Not Constrain Current Research

**Null hypothesis:** The 90-year history of ME/CFS does not constrain or inform current research directions, treatment development, or clinical practice. All actionable knowledge comes from current molecular biology (NIH 2024), genetics (DecodeME 2025), and systematic reviews (NICE 2021, IOM 2015). The historical narrative — outbreaks, psychosomatic era, PACE trial, nomenclature evolution — is epiphenomenal to actual research progress. A researcher who knew only the current molecular evidence and clinical guidelines would make all the same research decisions as a researcher who also knew the full history.

**What would falsify this null:**
- A research direction that was enabled specifically by historical analysis (e.g., outbreak re-analysis identified a pattern missed by molecular studies)
- A clinical decision that depends on knowing the PACE trial history rather than just the NICE 2021 conclusion
- A funding allocation that was changed by historical scholarship rather than current molecular evidence

**Assessment:** The null is partially falsified by the case definition heterogeneity problem — understanding why different studies produce different results requires knowing that different definitions capture different populations, which requires knowing the history of case definition evolution. A researcher who only knew the IOM 2015 definition would not automatically understand why a 1995 Fukuda-definition study produced different results. However, this is methodological history (case definition evolution), not outbreak history — the outbreak history (1934–1985) is the most vulnerable to the null hypothesis, because it provides context but not constraint on current research.

**Preliminary certainty:** H₀ partially true for outbreak history; partially false for case definition history. 0.40 that the outbreak history independently constrains current research; 0.70 that the case definition history independently constrains current research.

#### 11.2 H₀: The Outbreak Pattern Represents Random Clustering, Not a Single Disease Entity

**Null hypothesis:** The 14 epidemics synthesized by Acheson (1959) represent random clustering of unrelated illnesses — a mixture of viral epidemics (enterovirus, EBV, unknown viruses) affecting different organ systems in different patients — rather than instances of a single disease entity. The apparent consistency (female predominance, neurological involvement, prolonged recovery, negative testing) is an artifact of retrospective pattern-matching: Acheson selected outbreaks that fit his template and excluded those that didn't, and the features he identified (female predominance, prolonged recovery) are common to many post-infectious states, not specific to ME/CFS.

If this null is true, then: (a) the outbreak history provides no evidence for the existence of ME/CFS as a distinct disease, (b) each outbreak was a different infection producing a different post-infectious syndrome, and the apparent unity is a classification error, (c) current ME/CFS patients are also heterogeneous — some have post-EBV syndrome, some post-enterovirus, some post-SARS-CoV-2 — and the disease concept is a convenient diagnostic label for diverse post-infectious states rather than a unified biological entity.

**What would falsify this null:**
- Modern multi-omics comparison of patients from different outbreak eras (impossible — samples don't exist)
- Demonstration that current ME/CFS patients (regardless of trigger) share a common molecular signature that is distinct from other post-infectious fatigue states
- Epidemiological modeling showing that the outbreak pattern is statistically unlikely under random clustering (difficult — requires knowing the base rate of post-infectious fatigue after different infections)

**Assessment:** This null cannot be definitively tested because pre-1988 outbreak samples do not exist for molecular comparison. The DecodeME 2025 GWAS suggests a shared genetic architecture regardless of trigger (brain tissue enrichment), but GWAS identifies population-level associations, not individual-disease biomarkers. The null remains viable — the outbreak history is consistent with both a single disease entity and with heterogeneous post-infectious syndromes sharing common phenotypic features. The chapter presents one interpretation (consistent disease entity) as established fact when it is one of at least two viable interpretations.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.35 that the null is true (outbreaks as random clustering); 0.65 that outbreaks represent a consistent disease entity. The evidence favors Acheson's synthesis over the null, but the null is not disproven — it is less parsimonious (14 unrelated outbreaks with identical phenotypes is less likely than one recurring disease) but cannot be rejected with available evidence.

#### 11.3 H₀: The McEvedy Hypothesis Was Falsified, But the Psychosomatic Framing It Represented Survived Because It Met Institutional Needs

**Null hypothesis:** The McEvedy 1970 paper was individually weak (desk reinterpretation, no new data, sexist assumptions) and was refuted by Ramsay 1986 with clinical evidence. But the McEvedy paper succeeded not because it was convincing but because it met institutional needs: it provided a framework for clinicians to explain symptoms they couldn't understand, it required no resources (no lab tests, no treatments, no specialists), it shifted responsibility from the medical system to the patient ("it's in your head"), and it was self-reinforcing (once a patient is labeled psychosomatic, all future normal labs are interpreted as confirming the label).

If this null is correct, then: (a) the defeat of the McEvedy hypothesis was never about evidence — it was about institutional incentives — and (b) the current biological evidence (IOM 2015, DecodeME 2025) may also fail to change clinical practice if the institutional incentives for psychosomatic framing remain in place. The chapter's implication that "the evidence has now proven the biological model" assumes that evidence, not incentives, drives clinical behavior — an assumption that the persistence of psychosomatic framing after IOM 2015 undermines.

**What would falsify this null:**
- Demonstration that clinical behavior changed after IOM 2015 and DecodeME 2025 in proportion to the strength of the evidence (if evidence drives behavior, stronger evidence should produce stronger behavior change)
- Evidence that the psychosomatic framing persisted after 2015 primarily in older clinicians trained before 2015 and not in younger clinicians (suggesting generation turnover, not institutional incentives, is the mechanism of change)

**Assessment:** The null has strong support. The psychosomatic framing persisted for 40 years despite weak evidence (McEvedy 1970 was methodologically vulnerable on arrival). The IOM 2015 report provided strong evidence of biological basis, yet the clinical behavior change has been partial and slow. If evidence drove clinical behavior, the transition should have been faster. The persistence of psychosomatic attitudes in frontline medicine after IOM 2015 suggests that institutional incentives, not evidence quality, is the binding constraint.

**Preliminary certainty:** 0.70 that institutional incentives, not evidence quality, is the primary determinant of clinical acceptance of ME/CFS as a biological disease.

### Category 12 — Evidence Quality Concerns

#### 12.1 Retrospective Diagnosis Bias in Pre-1988 Outbreak Reports

**Concern:** All pre-1988 outbreak diagnoses are retrospective. We cannot confirm that the 1934 Los Angeles patients, the 1948 Akureyri patients, or the 1955 Royal Free patients met CCC, IOM, or NICE criteria because those criteria did not exist and the primary reports did not collect the necessary data. PEM was not systematically assessed. Cognitive dysfunction was noted qualitatively but not formally tested. The apparent coherence of the epidemic pattern may be an artifact of Acheson's 1959 synthesis imposing a template on heterogeneous clinical reports.

This is a standard problem in historical epidemiology. Modern readers see "female predominance + neurological symptoms + prolonged fatigue + negative polio testing" and recognize ME/CFS — but these four features are also consistent with: post-enteroviral syndrome, post-EBV syndrome, multiple sclerosis (pre-MRI era), systemic lupus erythematosus (pre-ANA testing era), and other conditions. The outbreak reports are primary historical sources with all the limitations of pre-modern clinical documentation: diagnostic technology was primitive, selection bias in which patients were reported is unknown, and the investigators were not blinded to their own diagnostic hypotheses.

**Mitigation:** The consistency across 14 outbreaks on three continents is unlikely under the null of random heterogeneous illnesses — but "unlikely" is not "impossible." The chapter should acknowledge the retrospective diagnosis limitation explicitly rather than presenting the outbreak phenotype as equivalent to a modern CCC-diagnosed case.

#### 12.2 Survivorship Bias and Source Quality in Historical ME/CFS Literature

**Concern:** The historical sources cited in the chapter are not uniform in quality:

- **Gilliam 1938:** A USPHS bulletin — not a peer-reviewed journal article. Written by government epidemiologists with incentives to identify a known pathogen (polio) and to minimize uncertainty. May understate uncertainty or overstate the "atypical polio" classification because "we don't know" was not an acceptable government report conclusion.

- **Sigurdsson 1950:** Published in Am J Hygiene — a reputable journal, but pre-modern epidemiological standards. No blinding, no standardized case definition, no systematic follow-up beyond the acute phase. The outbreak was identified because it was large and concentrated, not because systematic surveillance detected it.

- **Royal Free 1957 (BMJ):** A hospital staff publication by clinicians treating their own colleagues — potential for bias toward organic causation (clinicians do not want to believe their colleagues are hysterical) and toward publication (an outbreak that closes a hospital is inherently publishable).

- **Ramsay 1986:** A book, not a peer-reviewed study. Ramsay was a participant in the Royal Free outbreak response — he had a professional stake in defending his clinical judgment against McEvedy. His long-term follow-up data, while valuable, may have been subject to selection bias (patients who remained ill with objective findings were more likely to remain in contact with Ramsay).

- **McEvedy 1970:** Published in BMJ — a high-impact journal, but "desk reinterpretation" without new data. The paper's influence was disproportionate to its evidence quality because it aligned with prevailing psychiatric frameworks.

The chapter treats Ramsay as the hero and McEvedy as the villain — a framing that is probably correct on the merits but assigns asymmetric evidentiary scrutiny. Ramsay's primary source (first-hand observation) is treated as intrinsically credible; McEvedy's secondary source (desk reinterpretation) is treated as intrinsically suspect. But first-hand observation is not immune to bias — clinicians can be wrong about the organicity of symptoms they observe, especially when they have a personal stake in the diagnosis.

**Mitigation:** Apply the same evidentiary standards to Ramsay that the chapter applies to McEvedy — acknowledge Ramsay's conflict of interest (professional reputation), selection bias in follow-up, and the limitations of pre-modern clinical documentation — rather than presenting Ramsay as an unimpeachable primary source.

#### 12.3 Narrative Arc as Confirmation Bias: The Psychosomatic-to-Biological Story Selects for Vindication

**Concern:** The chapter's narrative structure — psychosomatic model was wrong → biological model was right → science has vindicated patients — is a form of confirmation bias in historical writing. The structure selects evidence that supports the arc and treats contrary or ambiguous evidence as artifacts of the psychosomatic era to be explained away:

- The Walitt 2024 NIH study found biological abnormalities (CSF catecholamines, CPET, B cells, gene expression) AND proposed an "effort preference" interpretive framing. The chapter treats the biological findings as evidence for the biological model and dismisses the effort preference framing as contaminated by psychosomatic residual bias. But the same investigators collected both — if their interpretive framing is biased, why trust their data collection? The chapter is selectively reading the same paper: accepting the parts that fit the narrative, rejecting the parts that don't.

- The McEvedy 1970 paper made one correct observation: female predominance. The chapter treats this observation as evidence of McEvedy's sexism (he interpreted it as psychogenic vulnerability) rather than acknowledging that female predominance is a genuine epidemiological feature of ME/CFS that requires explanation. A critic of the chapter's framing could argue: McEvedy correctly identified female predominance and incorrectly attributed it to psychology; Ramsay correctly identified organic basis and incorrectly minimized female predominance as an artifact of exposure — both were partially right and partially wrong, but the chapter treats one as villain and one as hero.

- The IOM 2015 report is treated as an unambiguous vindication. But the IOM report did not find a biomarker, did not identify a mechanism, and chose a name (SEID) that was not widely adopted — it was an institutional pronouncement, not a scientific discovery. The chapter's certainty score of 0.85 for IOM 2015 (vs 0.30 for White 2011 PACE) may reflect alignment with the chapter's narrative rather than objective evidence quality — IOM was a literature review of 9,000+ studies that concluded "this is real," which is a different epistemic product than a clinical trial that produced specific (if methodologically flawed) data.

**Mitigation:** The chapter should explicitly identify the narrative arc as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — "the evidence supports a biological disease model" rather than "the biological model has been proven" — and should apply symmetric scrutiny to evidence that supports and challenges the arc.

#### 12.4 Asymmetrical Methodological Scrutiny: PACE vs Biological Evidence Studies

**Concern:** The chapter applies stringent methodological criticism to PACE (mid-trial threshold changes, unblinded design, subjective outcomes, Oxford criteria, failure to report harms) but does not apply the same stringency to studies supporting the biological model:

- **Walitt 2024 NIH:** n=17 patients. The chapter acknowledges "small n" but cites the study's findings as evidence for the biological model without applying the same methodological skepticism: n=17 means all subgroup analyses are underpowered, all biological associations are sensitive to outliers, and the "post-infectious only" inclusion criterion limits generalizability to non-post-infectious ME/CFS. A trial with n=17 claiming a treatment effect would be dismissed — but n=17 claiming biological abnormalities is accepted.

- **DecodeME 2025:** This is a preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. The chapter cites 8 genome-wide significant loci and brain tissue enrichment as established facts, but preprints can change substantially during peer review. GWAS methodology also has known limitations: population stratification, winner's curse (effect sizes in discovery cohorts are inflated), and the "missing heritability" problem (9.5% heritability leaves 90.5% unexplained).

- **Brurberg 2014:** This systematic review of case definitions is the most balanced source — it documents the heterogeneity problem without taking a position on organicity vs psychogenicity. But the chapter uses Brurberg primarily to attack broad definitions (Fukuda, Oxford) and implicitly endorses narrow definitions (CCC, IOM, NICE). Brurberg found that only 2 of 20 definitions had adequate content validity, but did not conclude that narrow definitions are necessarily better — they concluded that content validity needs to be demonstrated, not assumed.

Asymmetrical skepticism is epistemically suspicious. If the same methodological standards used to critique PACE were applied to the biological evidence, the chapter's conclusions would need to be more qualified. This does not mean the biological model is wrong — it means the evidence for the biological model is weaker than the chapter's narrative suggests, and the evidence against the psychosomatic model is stronger than a single trial's failure.

**Mitigation:** Apply the same methodological checklist (sample size, blinding, objective outcomes, generalizability, conflict of interest, pre-registration, replication status) to every study cited, regardless of whether it supports or challenges the chapter's narrative.
