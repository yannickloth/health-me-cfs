# Brainstorm: Adult Red-Flags & Safety-Netting Protocol for Medical-Emergency Decompensation in Severe ME/CFS

**Topic:** Consolidated adult protocol for medical-emergency decompensation — when ME/CFS becomes a different disease. Explicit "go to ED now" trigger list and safety-netting for dangerous complications of severe/very-severe ME/CFS: weight loss, fever, focal neurology, sudden severe pain, TIA/cardiac-syncope risk, POTS collapse, refeeding risk, DVT, sepsis-from-immobility.

**Date:** 2026-08-14

**Phase:** 4 — Creative Brainstorming (clinical-safety topic)

**Integration decision (Phase 2):** PROCEED. Active Caps limit brainstorm to **categories 1–2 + 10–12 only**. Categories 3–9 (drug/supplement/intervention/math/cross-disease-diagnostic) are SKIPPED — this is a clinical-safety protocol topic, not a mechanistic or therapeutic-brainstorm topic.

**Evidence anchor:** The consolidated adult medical-emergency protocol is a genuine literature gap. ME/CFS-specific emergency literature is sparse: only one paper directly addresses CFS in the ED (Timbol & Baraniuk 2019, 0.45). The eight safety domains are largely **transferred** from adjacent at-risk populations (anorexia nervosa for refeeding; immobility/catatonia for VTE + pressure-ulcer + aspiration; general primary care for serious-disease red flags; general stroke/TIA for focal-neurology). ME/CFS-specific anchors: ED presentation (Timbol2019; Bowden2026, 0.60), orthostatic decompensation (corpus), differential diagnosis (Kobayashi2022, 0.48).

**Evidence gaps this brainstorm targets (from Phase 2 / literature summary):**
1. POTS cardiac-vs-neurally-mediated syncope emergency threshold in ME/CFS — NO ME/CFS-specific data.
2. Severe/very-severe ME/CFS mortality and cachexia/failure-to-thrive — clinically recognized, unquantified beyond Roberts2016/McManimen.
3. ED misdiagnosis / dismissal rates and outcomes of severe medical decompensation in ME/CFS — under-studied.
4. Incidence of refeeding syndrome, VTE, pressure-ulcer sepsis specifically in severe bedbound ME/CFS — unmeasured.
5. Whether a distinct ME/CFS-specific emergency-threshold protocol improves ED recognition/outcomes.

## Category 1: Novel Hypotheses / Clinical-Safety Insights

### 1.1 The Orthostatic-Intolerance Masquerade Hypothesis — the Most Common ED Driver Is Also the Best Mask for Acute Emergencies

**Rationale:** Timbol2019 found orthostatic intolerance accounts for roughly 1/3 of CFS ED presentations, and 42% of patients are dismissed as psychosomatic. The danger is that the *most common* benign explanation in this population (orthostatic/collapse) is exactly the symptom that also accompanies genuine medical emergencies (cardiac syncope, sepsis, pulmonary embolism, stroke-related autonomic instability, hemorrhage). A patient who "always collapses with POTS" may present a syncope that is actually the first signal of a different disease, and the habitual label ("just POTS again") forecloses the emergency work-up. The hypothesis: in severe ME/CFS, a presentation "typical of" the patient's baseline orthostatic intolerance is *more* dangerous than an atypical one, because it is the form most likely to be dismissed. This is a genuine clinical-safety insight with no direct ME/CFS data.

**Evidence link:** Timbol2019 (1/3 orthostatic-intolerance ED presentations; 42% dismissed); Kobayashi2022 (serious disease misattributed to a syndromic label, CFS one such label); general principle from ElHusseini2013 (in a symptom-heavy/bedbound population, the "mimic" default is dangerous). No study has tested whether a syncope "typical of POTS" in a known ME/CFS patient has a different rate of true underlying emergency than an atypical syncope.

**Provisional certainty:** 0.30

**Falsifiable prediction:** In a cohort of severe ME/CFS patients presenting to the ED with syncope/collapse, those with a documented prior diagnosis of orthostatic intolerance will have a *higher* (not lower) rate of missed acute medical emergency on blinded ED chart review, and a longer time-to-emergency-diagnosis, compared with those without such a label.

**Non-specialist consequence:** A patient who faints a lot may be at greatest risk of having a serious emergency missed precisely because the fainting is so familiar to everyone — so caregivers should treat "usual collapse" with the same caution as an unusual one.

### 1.2 The "Normal-Range-for-a-Frail-Bedbound-Patient" Hypothesis — Why Decompensation Is Under-Recognised

**Rationale:** Routine laboratory "normal ranges" are derived from ambulant, well-nourished populations. In a severe bedbound ME/CFS patient, an absolute lab value that falls within the reference range may still represent a large *deviation from that individual's own baseline* — e.g., a phosphate or potassium at the low end of "normal" during refeeding initiation, or a heart rate that is "tachycardic but within normal limits." The hypothesis: the conventional reference-range framing is itself a mechanism of under-recognition in this population, because it uses the wrong comparison standard (population range instead of within-patient trajectory). A rapid delta from baseline is a stronger decompensation signal than an absolute value crossing a population threshold.

**Evidence link:** AraujoCastro2018 / Proulx-Cabana2022 (electrolyte monitoring is the refeeding-safety standard; hypophosphatemia hallmark); Ridout2016 (routine absolute-value daily labs were of poor value — because, on this hypothesis, they measure population-threshold crossing, not within-patient delta); Clinebell2014 (immobility complications often silent). This reframes the Ridout "null" as a measurement-standard problem rather than a monitoring-is-unnecessary conclusion.

**Provisional certainty:** 0.35

**Falsifiable prediction:** Among severe bedbound ME/CFS patients undergoing refeeding or on chronic immobility, within-patient serial deltas (e.g., >20% drop in phosphate, potassium, or orthostatic blood-pressure change) will predict clinical decompensation events (arrhythmia, syncope, infection, altered mental state) better than the number of days a lab value falls outside the population reference range.

**Non-specialist consequence:** A blood test that looks "normal" by the standard chart may still signal real danger for a very ill bedridden patient — change over time, not the printed normal range, is the better warning sign.

### 1.3 Resting Tachycardia / Elevated Baseline Heart Rate as a Threshold-Blunting Sign

**Rationale:** Severe ME/CFS commonly carries an elevated resting heart rate and orthostatic tachycardia. If a patient's baseline heart rate is chronically elevated, then acute hemodynamic compromise (hypovolemia, sepsis, PE, hemorrhage) may fail to trigger the tachycardia alarm that a clinician expects — the "high normal" resting rate blunts the perceived urgency of a further rise. Conversely, a bradycardic event (e.g., neurally-mediated or vasovagal severe bradycardia, or cardiac-syncope bradyarrhythmia) may be a *more* discriminating emergency signal in this population precisely because it departs from the usual elevated baseline. The hypothesis: in ME/CFS, *bradycardia*, not tachycardia, is the under-recognised emergency-red-flag, and the population's tachycardia-normalization makes acute rises less alarming.

**Evidence link:** Corpus POTS/orthostatic substrate (BouHolaigah1995NMH, Novak2026, Rowe2001FludrocortisoneNMH); Proulx-Cabana2022 (bradycardia <30 monitored as a danger sign in refeeding); refeeding-syndrome hypoglycemia/bradycardia risk (Kohn2011). No ME/CFS-specific study examines whether a departure *downward* from the elevated baseline is a missed acute emergency signal.

**Provisional certainty:** 0.30

**Falsifiable prediction:** In severe ME/CFS, acute medical decompensation (sepsis, hypovolemia, PE, refeeding-related arrhythmia) will be associated with a *decrease* or *lack of increase* in measured heart rate relative to that patient's documented resting baseline, whereas it will be preceded by a rise in the general comparison population; and clinicians reviewing de-identified tracings will rate the urgent decompensating ME/CFS events as lower triage urgency than matched general events.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Because a bedbound patient's heart is often already beating fast, the truly dangerous episodes may be the ones where the heart rate falls or fails to rise — the opposite of the usual warning.

### 1.4 POTS Cardiac-Vs-Neurally-Mediated Syncope Emergency Threshold: Recovery-Time as the Discriminating Signal

**Rationale:** The corpus has no ME/CFS-specific data separating cardiac syncope from neurally-mediated vasovagal syncope at the emergency threshold (gap 1). The general-cardiology transferable discriminator is premonitory features (prodrome, posture, trigger), but these are unreliable in a population with chronic orthostatic instability. The novel candidate signal: **recovery time and recovery quality** — a neurally-mediated syncope typically recovers quickly once supine with full return of orientation; a cardiac/sustained-arhythmia syncope tends to have slower, incomplete, or post-event-lingering recovery (ongoing hemodynamic instability, persistent confusion, chest pain, palpitations on return of consciousness). In ME/CFS, where prodromal autonomic symptoms are chronic and non-discriminative, recovery dynamics may be the more transferable and more ME/CFS-relevant emergency threshold than pre-syncopal features.

**Evidence link:** Transfer from general cardiology (cardiac syncope = higher mortality, needs urgent work-up); Timbol2019 (orthostatic intolerance as dominant ED driver — so prodrome-based discrimination is poor in this population); corpus NMH/tilt-table substrate (BouHolaigah1995NMH, Jason2024TiltTable, Razumovsky2003). No ME/CFS study has measured recovery-time after collapse as an emergency discriminator.

**Provisional certainty:** 0.25

**Falsifiable prediction:** Among ME/CFS patients with recurrent collapse, an episode followed by recovery-to-baseline within 5 minutes of supine positioning with full orientation and no chest pain/palpitations will have a near-zero rate of true cardiac emergency, whereas an episode with recovery >15 minutes, persistent confusion, chest pain, or palpitations will have a significantly higher rate of identified cardiac/sustained-arhythmia cause on urgent evaluation.

**Non-specialist consequence:** For a POTS patient, how quickly and completely they come around after fainting may be a more trustworthy sign of whether to call an ambulance than the faint itself.

### 1.5 The "Silent-Complication" of Bedbound ME/CFS: Disuse DVT, Aspiration, and Pressure-Ulcer Sepsis Present Without Their Classic Signs

**Rationale:** The complications that kill severe bedbound patients are silent by the very mechanism of disuse: (a) a disused, immobile leg may not swell or be painful, so DVT presents atypically (sudden dyspnea/PE without preceding leg signs); (b) aspiration may be clinically silent (no cough reflex in a profoundly deconditioned/sedated-like state), presenting as "sudden confusion" or low-grade fever rather than choking; (c) pressure-ulcer sepsis can be occult because the ulcer is hidden, the patient cannot report pain, and "sepsis-from-immobility" begins with non-specific deterioration that is misread as an ME/CFS crash. The hypothesis: in severe bedbound ME/CFS, the classic prodromes of these three emergencies are systematically absent, so the "go to ED now" triggers for this population must be *non-classical* — sudden unexplained change in mental status, unexplained fever, unexplained dyspnea — not the textbook symptom pairs.

**Evidence link:** Clinebell2014 (immobility complications framework — pressure ulcers, DVT/PE, contractures; silent progression); Hull2010/EXCLAIM (immobility VTE risk, 2.5–4.0% even with recent immobility); Kwiatkowska2025 (refeeding causing respiratory/circulatory/neurological/convulsive failure); Timbol2019 (non-specific ED presentations dismissed). No ME/CFS study has catalogued whether these complications present non-classically in this population.

**Provisional certainty:** 0.35

**Falsifiable prediction:** In severe bedbound ME/CFS, incident DVT/PE, aspiration pneumonia, and pressure-ulcer sepsis will present with non-classical features (no leg swelling/pain, no coughing episode, no localized ulcer complaint) in a majority of cases, and a protocol that triggers on "any unexplained change in mental status, unexplained fever, or unexplained dyspnea" will detect these events earlier than one that triggers on classical symptom pairs.

**Non-specialist consequence:** For a bedbound patient, the danger signs may be quiet ones — a change in alertness, a new low fever, or harder breathing — rather than the classic pain and swelling symptoms people expect.

## Category 2: Research Directions

### 2.1 Ambulatory Cardiac Monitoring Cohort for Syncope in Severe/Orthostatic ME/CFS — Resolving the Cardiac-vs-Neurally-Mediated Emergency Threshold

**Rationale:** This is the single most direct study to close gap 1 (POTS cardiac-vs-neurally-mediated syncope emergency threshold in ME/CFS). Equip a cohort of severe/orthostatic ME/CFS patients (with and without documented collapse episodes) with prolonged ambulatory ECG (patch/loop recorder, ideally 14–30 days or symptom-triggered), paired with a structured collapse diary (recovery time, prodrome, orientation, chest pain, palpitations). Correlate recorded rhythm at collapse with the clinical features, and with any downstream emergency diagnosis. This produces the first ME/CFS-specific data on whether collapses are arrhythmic/cardiac vs neurally-mediated, and empirically derives a recovery-time threshold (cf. Idea 1.4). It is non-invasive, uses existing validated technology, and directly supplies the missing cardiac-syncope emergency-threshold anchor the protocol currently must transfer from general cardiology.

**Evidence link:** Gap 1 explicitly flagged in Phase 2 as NO ME/CFS-specific data; corpus provides the orthostatic substrate (BouHolaigah1995NMH, Novak2026, Jason2024TiltTable). Ambulatory ECG/loop-recorder syncope evaluation is validated general-cardiology practice.

**Provisional certainty (design/feasibility):** 0.60

**Falsifiable prediction:** If cardiac/sustained-arhythmia syncope is genuinely rare in this population, the loop-recorder cohort will find that >95% of recorded collapse episodes are sinus/neurally-mediated with recovery <10 min, and the derived recovery-time threshold will not discriminate any cardiac events (because there are effectively none) — which would itself be a null result with safety-netting value (confirming benignity for the majority).

**Non-specialist consequence:** Wearing a small heart monitor during their usual collapses would finally tell POTS patients which fainting episodes are truly dangerous versus harmless, instead of relying on guesswork.

### 2.2 Severe-ME Mortality / Cachexia / Failure-to-Thrive Registry — Quantifying the Clinically-Recognized-But-Unquantified Outcome

**Rationale:** Closes gap 2. Severe/very-severe ME/CFS mortality, cachexia, and failure-to-thrive are clinically recognized but unquantified beyond Roberts2016 and McManimen. A dedicated severe-ME registry (or a rigorous cohort nested in existing registries — e.g., Bowden2026-style nationwide benefit-linkage, or clinic-based severe cohorts) would capture: (a) weight trajectory and time-to-cachexia threshold, (b) incidence of the specific decompensation complications (refeeding, VTE, pressure-ulcer sepsis), (c) cause-of-death and circumstance-of-death (was a "go to ED" trigger present and acted on?), and (d) avoidable-mortality signals (deaths where a red flag was documented but the patient was dismissed — cf. Timbol2019 42% dismissal). This would convert a documentation gap into a quantified baseline and provide the denominator needed for all incidence questions (gap 4).

**Evidence link:** Gap 2 flagged in Phase 2; Roberts2016 (no overall excess mortality but elevated suicide), McManimen (mortality series) already in corpus; Bowden2026 demonstrates nationwide registry linkage is feasible for ME/CFS (n=1902).

**Provisional certainty (design/feasibility):** 0.55

**Falsifiable prediction:** If severe/very-severe ME/CFS mortality from cachexia/failure-to-thrive is genuinely elevated, the registry will show a dose-response with severity and with weight-loss trajectory, and a detectable fraction of deaths will have had a documented, but dismissed or un-acted-on, emergency red flag in the preceding days; if not, severe-ME cachexia mortality will be comparable to general bedbound illness after controlling for immobility duration.

**Non-specialist consequence:** A registry that tracks how severely ill patients fare would reveal whether any deaths were preventable and give families and doctors hard numbers instead of anecdotes.

### 2.3 ED-Recognition Intervention Study: Protocol + Training → Measured ED Outcomes in ME/CFS

**Rationale:** Closes gap 5 — the core question of whether a distinct ME/CFS-specific emergency-threshold protocol improves ED recognition/outcomes. Design: cluster-randomized or stepped-wedge trial at EDs, where intervention sites receive (a) a printed/electronic adult red-flags trigger card for severe ME/CFS (the "go to ED now" list: unexplained weight loss, fever, focal neurology, sudden severe pain, sudden dyspnea, new/atypical collapse, refeeding after re-initiation of feeding) and (b) brief staff education on the 42%-dismissal problem. Outcomes: rates of ED mis-triage (dismissed vs admitted), time-to-appropriate work-up (cardiac, stroke, sepsis, refeeding), and downstream emergency diagnosis (stroke, PE, MI, sepsis, RS) among presenting severe ME/CFS patients, compared with control EDs. This directly tests whether the framing problem (dismissal) is addressable by an explicit protocol, and quantifies the clinical value of the entire topic.

**Evidence link:** Gap 5; Timbol2019 (42% dismissal, ED staff "not knowledgeable" — the intervention target); Bowden2026 (ED is a real, frequent ME/CFS contact point — 18.8% ED visits, the intervention venue); ElHusseini2013 (mimic-assumption danger in ill populations).

**Provisional certainty (design/feasibility):** 0.50

**Falsifiable prediction:** Compared with control EDs, intervention EDs will show a lower rate of psychosomatic-dismissal of severe ME/CFS presentations and a shorter time-to-appropriate emergency work-up for red-flag presentations, but NO change in overall admission rate for non-red-flag presentations (i.e., the protocol raises recognition without raising over-investigation) — the latter being the key safety test against Category 10 over-treatment concern.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Giving ER staff a simple card listing the genuine emergency signs for severe ME/CFS, plus training that "it's not just in their head," could measurably reduce how often dangerously ill patients are sent home.

### 2.4 Prospective Incidence Cohort for Refeeding Syndrome, VTE, and Pressure-Ulcer Sepsis in Severe Bedbound ME/CFS

**Rationale:** Closes gap 4. The incidence of refeeding syndrome, VTE, and pressure-ulcer sepsis specifically in severe bedbound ME/CFS is unmeasured — all current estimates are transferred from anorexia (refeeding), general immobile medical patients (VTE), and catatonia (pressure/aspiration). A prospective cohort of severe/very-severe bedbound ME/CFS patients (either in-home with periodic review, or during any admissions/institutional care) with structured surveillance would yield the first population-specific incidence rates for each complication, and allow derivation of the risk-stratification that the Ridout-vs-monitoring-guidance contradiction (Phase 2) requires: identify which bedbound patients warrant electrolyte/phosphate monitoring and which do not.

**Evidence link:** Gap 4; refeeding transfer papers (Kohn2011, Proulx-Cabana2022, AraujoCastro2018, Kwiatkowska2025); VTE transfer (Hull2010/EXCLAIM); immobility framework (Clinebell2014); Ridout2016 (null for routine monitoring — motivates risk-stratification).

**Provisional certainty (design/feasibility):** 0.50

**Falsifiable prediction:** The incidence of clinically significant refeeding syndrome, VTE, and pressure-ulcer sepsis in severe bedbound ME/CFS will be measurable and will cluster within a defined high-risk subset (marked weight loss, new feeding initiation, prolonged unrelieved immobility, malnutrition); if these complications are vanishingly rare (<1%) across the entire cohort, then population-specific monitoring beyond standard care is not warranted (feeding directly into Category 10).

**Non-specialist consequence:** This study would give real numbers on how often bedbound ME/CFS patients actually develop dangerous feeding, blood-clot, and bed-sore complications — and which ones need watching — instead of borrowing estimates from other diseases.

### 2.5 ED Misdiagnosis / Dismissal Outcomes Study for Severe ME/CFS Medical Decompensation

**Rationale:** Closes gap 3. Timbol2019 is a self-report survey (42% dismissal) with no linkage to outcomes. A study that links ED visits of severe ME/CFS patients to outcomes — return visits within 72 hours, missed emergency diagnoses (stroke, PE, MI, sepsis, refeeding), and downstream adverse events — would quantify the actual harm of dismissal and identify which red-flag presentations were most often sent home in error. Design: retrospective or prospective linkage of ME/CFS-coded ED encounters (or patient-reported severe ME/CFS ED visits) to subsequent admissions and diagnoses, with blinded chart review for "missed emergency" events. This gives the protocol's core motivation a hard-evidence base rather than a self-report base.

**Evidence link:** Gap 3; Timbol2019 (self-report only, no outcomes — the limitation this closes); Bowden2026 (registry linkage infrastructure, elevated ED use 18.8%); Kobayashi2022 (serious disease misattributed to syndromic label); ElHusseini2013 (mimic-default danger).

**Provisional certainty (design/feasibility):** 0.50

**Falsifiable prediction:** If dismissal is truly harmful, the study will show a higher 72-hour ED return rate and a higher rate of delayed emergency diagnosis among ME/CFS patients dismissed with a red-flag presentation than among matched non-ME/CFS patients; if dismissal is not harmful, return/readmission rates will be comparable and the 42% figure reflects benign presentations being appropriately discharged.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Linking what actually happens after ER visits would reveal whether dismissed ME/CFS patients come back worse or with a missed emergency — turning a concerning survey number into proof of real harm.

### 2.6 Risk-Stratified Laboratory-Monitoring Trial During Refeeding in Severe ME/CFS

**Rationale:** Directly resolves the Phase 2 internal contradiction between Ridout2016 (routine daily lab monitoring poor value — 0 RS cases, 3960 labs) and the monitoring guidance (Proulx-Cabana2022, AraujoCastro2018, Kohn2011 — electrolyte/phosphate monitoring is the refeeding-safety standard). The reconciliation the synthesis reached — risk-stratified monitoring — is testable: randomize or compare strategies where only high-risk severe bedbound ME/CFS patients (marked weight loss, low baseline phosphate, new feeding initiation, prolonged starvation) receive electrolyte/phosphate monitoring with a within-patient-delta trigger (cf. Idea 1.2), versus routine monitoring in all, versus symptom-only. Outcomes: refeeding-syndrome events, clinically significant electrolyte disturbance, and cost/lab burden.

**Evidence link:** Ridout2016 (null — motivates against routine); Kohn2011, Proulx-Cabana2022, AraujoCastro2018 (refeeding monitoring standard); Kwiatkowska2025 (RS consequences); gap 4 (no ME/CFS-specific refeeding data).

**Provisional certainty (design/feasibility):** 0.45

**Falsifiable prediction:** A risk-stratified monitoring strategy (targeted to high-risk + within-patient delta triggers) will detect refeeding-syndrome/pre-hypophosphatemia events with no more missed events than routine monitoring, but with substantially fewer labs and lower cost; if high-risk-targeted monitoring misses events that routine catches, risk-stratification is unsafe in this population.

**Non-specialist consequence:** This trial would settle how often a bedridden ME/CFS patient starting to eat again actually needs blood tests — sparing many unnecessary needle pokes while still catching the dangerous cases.

## Category 10: Reasons the Safety-Netting Mechanism May NOT Be Relevant / Not the Right Framing

### 10.1 If Severe ME/CFS Complications Are No More Common Than General Bedbound Illness, the Protocol Only Restates Standard of Care

**Rationale:** The entire protocol's distinct value rests on the premise that severe/very-severe ME/CFS faces a *ME/CFS-specific* or *elevated* burden of refeeding, VTE, pressure-ulcer sepsis, and ED dismissal. If, instead, a severe bedbound ME/CFS patient's risk of these complications is the same as any equally-bedbound, equally-malnourished general patient, then the "red-flags for severe ME/CFS" protocol may be redundant with generic immobility/malnutrition standards of care — the topic would add a label but not new triggers. Under this framing, the correct product would be a generic "care of the severely bedbound patient" checklist, not a ME/CFS-specific one, and the ME/CFS-specific framing could even mislead by implying the risks are unique when they are universal.

**Evidence to disprove:** A comparative incidence cohort (Idea 2.4) showing severe ME/CFS bedbound patients have a higher or *differently-timed* incidence of these complications than matched non-ME/CFS bedbound patients; or evidence that ME/CFS patients receive *less* of the generic standard care (e.g., dismissal, fewer preventive measures) than equally-ill comparison patients.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If bedbound ME/CFS patients face exactly the same dangers as anyone else equally bedbound, then the value is in making sure they get the standard care everyone deserves — not in a special ME/CFS rulebook.

### 10.2 Red-Flag Framing Risks Over-Investigation and Over-Treatment of a Genuinely Low-Risk Population

**Rationale:** The red-flag framing, if applied too broadly across ALL severity levels rather than restricted to severe/very-severe bedbound patients, could produce the opposite harm to under-recognition: over-investigation and over-treatment of a population in which serious organic disease is actually uncommon. Stadje2016 (0.53) shows malignancy is only 0.6% and serious somatic disease 4.3% in primary-care tiredness; Levine1992 (0.45) shows no excess malignancy in ME/CFS. If the "go to ED now" triggers are read as a routine screening impulse rather than as narrow, specific, high-pretest-probability triggers, the protocol could drive unnecessary ED visits, testing, anticoagulation (with its bleeding risk — Hull2010's trade-off), and refeeding-monitoring in patients whose baseline risk does not warrant it. The Phase 2 reconciliation (targeted red-flag triggers, NOT blanket screening) is correct but fragile: a red-flag list is easily over-read.

**Evidence to disprove:** An implementation study (Idea 2.3) or post-implementation audit showing the protocol raises ED admission/work-up rates for NON-red-flag presentations (the over-investigation signature), or increases ED visits without increasing detection of true emergencies.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If a warning list is used too loosely, otherwise-stable ME/CFS patients could be sent for unnecessary ER visits and tests — trading one harm (missed emergency) for another (needless alarms).

**`origin: brainstorm`**

### 10.3 ED-Avoidance Means the Protocol May Not Reach the Highest-Risk Patients

**Rationale:** Timbol2019 shows a substantial fraction of CFS patients *avoid* the ED entirely ("nothing could be done" / "not taken seriously"). If the patients at highest risk of severe decompensation are precisely those who have learned to avoid emergency care, then a protocol that lives in the ED (staff training, ED-presentation triggers) cannot help them — the highest-risk patients never present. Under this framing, the safety-netting mechanism is mis-located: the "go to ED now" message must reach patients and caregivers in the home before decompensation, not only arm ED staff. A protocol that is ED-centric may be irrelevant to the very population it most needs to protect.

**Evidence to disprove:** Data showing that severe/very-severe (highest-risk) ME/CFS patients DO present to EDs at rates comparable to ambulant patients, or that ED-based intervention reduces downstream mortality/morbidity even for those who historically avoided care; or a demonstrated pathway (home-visit, telemedicine, caregiver activation) by which the protocol reaches non-presenters.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If the sickest patients refuse to go to the ER because of past mistreatment, then a protocol that only helps people who already show up does nothing for the ones who stay home — so the warning must reach them (and their families) before a crisis.

**`origin: brainstorm`**

### 10.4 If Most Severe ME/CFS Patients Never Develop These Complications, the Trigger List Overstates Urgency

**Rationale:** The protocol's urgency framing ("go to ED now" for many triggers) rests on an unstated assumption that these complications are common enough to warrant a high-index-of-suspicion posture. If, in reality, most severe ME/CFS patients do NOT develop refeeding syndrome, VTE, or pressure-ulcer sepsis (e.g., if their immobility is chronic/well-managed rather than acute; if their caloric intake, though low, is stable and not re-feeding-initiated), then the trigger list would be a low-yield alarm: frequently-present triggers (unexplained fatigue flare, mild weight fluctuation, orthostatic collapse) that are rarely associated with true emergency. This could cause alarm-fatigue in patients/caregivers and erode the credibility of the genuinely important triggers (focal neurology, fever, sudden dyspnea). The framing may be correct only if restricted to a genuinely high-risk subset.

**Evidence to disprove:** Incidence data (Idea 2.4) showing the specified complications occur at a meaningful rate in the severe bedbound population, OR that the high-yield triggers (focal neuro, fever, sudden dyspnea) are indeed associated with emergency disease when they occur; the concern is specifically about the low-yield, frequently-triggered items.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If most bedbound patients never hit these dangers, then listing too many "rush to the ER" warnings makes the truly important ones lose their impact — like a smoke alarm that goes off for every burned piece of toast.

## Category 11: Null Hypothesis Assessment

### 11.1 Null: The Safety Complications Are NOT More Common in Severe ME/CFS Than in the General Bedbound Population

**Statement:** Severe/very-severe bedbound ME/CFS patients experience refeeding syndrome, VTE, pressure-ulcer sepsis, aspiration, and focal-neurologic emergencies at rates no higher than equally-bedbound, equally-malnourished patients of the same age/sex without ME/CFS, after controlling for immobility duration and feeding status.

**What existing claims would need revision:** The core premise of the protocol — that severe ME/CFS faces a distinct/elevated burden of decompensation complications — would be weakened. The protocol would have to be reframed from "ME/CFS-specific emergency triggers" to "generic care of the severely bedbound patient, applied to ME/CFS," and the ME/CFS-specific framing in ch23 emergency-decompensation and the cross-reference in ch06 would need softening. The refeeding/VTE/aspiration content would survive only as standard transferred safety-netting, not as ME/CFS-distinct risk. The dismissal/ED-access claims (Timbol2019, Bowden2026) are independent and would survive.

**What would need to be true:** (1) Incidence rates (Idea 2.4) indistinguishable from matched general bedbound patients; (2) no dose-response of complication risk with ME/CFS severity beyond what immobility duration alone predicts; (3) ME/CFS patients receive the same preventive care (repositioning, VTE prophylaxis consideration, feeding guidance) as comparably-ill non-ME/CFS patients.

**Likelihood:** Medium (0.40). The complications are plausibly driven by immobility and malnutrition *per se*, which severe ME/CFS shares with other bedbound conditions; the ME/CFS-specific increment is plausible but unproven. The most defensible framing is that the protocol's value lies in ensuring this population receives the standard care (rather than a unique risk).

**Non-specialist consequence:** If ME/CFS patients face the same dangers as anyone equally bedbound, then the honest message is that they deserve the same protection — not that they are uniquely at risk.

**`origin: brainstorm`**

### 11.2 Null: ED Misdiagnosis / Dismissal Is NOT Worse in ME/CFS Than in Other Chronic-Illness Populations

**Statement:** ME/CFS patients presenting to the ED with a medical-emergency red flag are dismissed or mis-triaged at rates no higher than patients with other chronic, ill-understood, symptom-heavy syndromes (e.g., fibromyalgia, chronic pain, functional neurologic disorder, autoimmune fatigue), after controlling for presentation acuity and objective findings.

**What existing claims would need revision:** The 42%-dismissal figure from Timbol2019 would lose its ME/CFS-specific framing — it would become a general problem of ill-understood chronic illness in emergency medicine rather than a ME/CFS-specific safety gap. The protocol's "arm patients/caregivers to communicate triggers credibly" rationale would still stand but would be a general patient-empowerment intervention, not a ME/CFS-specific one. The severity-specific mortality claims (gap 2) are independent.

**What would need to be true:** (1) A controlled study (Idea 2.5) showing dismissal/return/missed-diagnosis rates comparable to matched chronic-illness controls; (2) ED triage decisions explained by objective acuity markers, not the ME/CFS label; (3) no association between a documented ME/CFS diagnosis and lower triage urgency among identical presentations.

**Likelihood:** Low–Medium (0.35). Self-report (Timbol2019) suggests dismissal is real and common, but no controlled comparator exists; the hypothesis that dismissal is a general chronic-illness phenomenon rather than ME/CFS-specific is plausible. The safety-netting content survives regardless (dismissal is harmful either way), but the *distinctiveness* of the ME/CFS framing would be reduced.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If ME/CFS patients are dismissed at the same rate as patients with other "mystery" illnesses, the problem is broader than ME/CFS — and the fix must help all of them, not just this group.

### 11.3 Null: A ME/CFS-Specific Emergency Protocol Does Not Improve ED Outcomes Over Generic Emergency Protocols

**Statement:** Severe ME/CFS patients presenting with a red-flag trigger have the same ED recognition, time-to-work-up, and downstream emergency-diagnosis rates whether EDs use a ME/CFS-specific trigger card or only standard generic protocols (stroke, sepsis, PE, cardiac, and general "acutely unwell adult" pathways).

**What existing claims would need revision:** The central hypothesis of gap 5 (that a distinct ME/CFS-specific emergency-threshold protocol improves recognition) would be falsified. This would not invalidate the safety-netting *content* (the triggers are standard) but would mean the protocol adds no marginal value over existing generic emergency pathways once the patient is in the ED — the real value would shift entirely to pre-ED (patient/caregiver recognition and access, Idea 10.3) and to ensuring the generic pathway is *applied* rather than foreclosed by the ME/CFS label.

**What would need to be true:** (1) The intervention study (Idea 2.3) shows no difference between protocol and generic-protocol EDs on recognition/time-to-work-up/missed-diagnosis; (2) presenting severe ME/CFS patients are already routed correctly through generic pathways; (3) the observed dismissal (Timbol2019) is driven by factors a label-specific card cannot change (e.g., staff skepticism, resource constraints).

**Likelihood:** Medium (0.45). If dismissal is driven by the ME/CFS label itself ("it's psychosomatic"), a ME/CFS-specific card that says "this is a real illness with real emergencies" directly addresses it and would plausibly help; but if dismissal is driven by generic symptom-heavy-presentation skepticism, a generic pathway improvement might suffice and the ME/CFS-specific product would add little. This is the single most important null to pre-empt with the intervention study (Idea 2.3).

**Non-specialist consequence:** It is possible that ER staff already have all the warning lists they need — and that the real fix is convincing them to apply those lists to ME/CFS patients, not printing a new list.

## Category 12: Evidence Quality Concerns

### 12.1 Transfer of Anorexia-Nervosa Refeeding Evidence to ME/CFS Is Physiologically but Not Psychologically or Behaviorally Equivalent

**Rationale:** The refeeding-syndrome evidence (Kohn2011, Proulx-Cabana2022, AraujoCastro2018, Kwiatkowska2025, Norrington2012) transfers from anorexia nervosa (AN), a population that differs from severe bedbound ME/CFS in key ways: AN patients are typically ambulatory at the moment of refeeding initiation and are being *re-fed after self-imposed restriction*, whereas the ME/CFS scenario is a bedbound patient whose low intake may be stable, may be involuntary (unable to prepare/consume food), and whose refeeding is usually *not* a discrete, monitored programmatic event. The carbohydrate-proportion and electrolyte physiology is transferable, but the *incidence* and *circumstance* of refeeding syndrome in ME/CFS cannot be inferred from AN cohorts, where caloric re-initiation is structured and monitored. The certainty scores (0.44–0.48) reflect population weight but not this behavioral/circumstantial mismatch.

**Impact:** The refeeding-syndrome risk for severe bedbound ME/CFS is likely real but its *prevalence and typical trigger* (spontaneous intake changes at home, not monitored re-initiation) are unknown; the AN-derived monitoring standards may be both over- and under-applied. Mitigation: flag that refeeding guidance is a physiological transfer, not an incidence transfer, and anchor incidence to ME/CFS-specific data (Idea 2.4).

**Non-specialist consequence:** The feeding-danger warnings borrowed from eating-disorder care point in the right direction, but nobody yet knows how often bedbound ME/CFS patients actually hit them in everyday home life.

### 12.2 Transfer of Catatonia / Acute-Immobility Evidence Overstates the VTE and Pressure-Ulcer Risk Profile of Chronic ME/CFS Bedbound Care

**Rationale:** The immobility-complication framework (Clinebell2014, from catatonia) and the VTE-risk anchor (Hull2010/EXCLAIM, from *acute* reduced mobility with *recent* immobilization) are both transfers from states that differ from chronic ME/CFS bedbound care. EXCLAIM's immobility was acute/recent, level-graded, in patients ≥40 y with acute medical illness — not the years-long, managed bedrest of severe ME/CFS, where caregivers may already provide repositioning, support surfaces, and passive range-of-motion. Catatonia is a treated, hospitalized, usually reversible immobility. Applying EXCLAIM's 2.5–4.0% VTE rate or Clinebell's preventive-intensity directly to chronic home-bound ME/CFS may overstate both risk and the required prophylactic intensity — particularly the anticoagulation trade-off (Hull2010's bleeding risk), which is dangerous to apply to a population that is often anticoagulation-sensitive.

**Impact:** The VTE/anticoagulation safety-netting for bedbound ME/CFS risks over- (or mis-) treating if the acute-immobility anchor is applied to a chronic, already-managed setting. Mitigation: present EXCLAIM/Clinebell as *upper-bound* risk framing, explicitly note the acute-vs-chronic immobility difference, and anchor prophylaxis decisions to actual ME/CFS bedbound immobility characteristics (Idea 2.4).

**Non-specialist consequence:** Blood-clot warnings taken from recently-hospitalized patients may overstate the danger for someone who has been bedbound at home for years with good care — so the clot-prevention advice must be read as a maximum, not a certainty.

### 12.3 Self-Report and Selection Bias in Timbol2019 Undermine Its Status as the Protocol's Primary ME/CFS-Specific ED Anchor

**Rationale:** Timbol2019 — the *only* ME/CFS-specific ED paper — is an anonymous online survey (n=282, self-report, single center, US, physician-diagnosed CFS) with selection and social-desirability bias. Its headline figures (59% ED use; ~1/3 orthostatic-intolerance presentations; 42% dismissed; ED "not knowledgeable"; favorability 3.6/10) drive several protocol claims, but they are unfiltered self-reports with no verification against ED records, triage notes, or outcomes. The 42% dismissal figure could be inflated by recruitment of a self-selected, treatment-seeking, dissatisfied sample, or by recall/attribution bias (patients attributing any negative ED experience to being "dismissed as psychosomatic"). Basing a safety-netting protocol's central motivation on an unvalidated self-report is a quality risk not captured by the 0.45 certainty score alone.

**Impact:** The dismissal statistic is used to justify the protocol's communication/empowerment framing (and Category 11.2). Its reliability as a factual anchor is low. Mitigation: treat Timbol2019 as hypothesis-generating; anchor dismissal/ED-experience claims to objective record-linked data (Idea 2.5); present the 42% figure as a self-reported signal, not a measured rate.

**Non-specialist consequence:** The alarming "nearly half dismissed" figure comes from what patients remember and chose to report online — real, but not yet a measured, verified statistic.

### 12.4 Referral-Centre / Label-Specific Selection Bias in Kobayashi2022 Overstates the "Serious Disease Hidden Behind a Syndromic Label" Risk

**Rationale:** Kobayashi2022 (n=1261, 0.48) — used to support the "don't let a ME/CFS label foreclose a red-flag search" claim — is a single-centre retrospective series of patients referred for *possible Lyme*, of whom 84% had no active Lyme and a subset received serious diagnoses (MS, malignancy, Parkinson, sarcoidosis, ALS) misattributed to syndromes including CFS. Its serious-disease yield reflects a *Lyme-referral, tertiary-centre* denominator, which inflates the pretest probability of exotic serious disease far above a general ME/CFS or primary-care population. Generalizing its "serious disease hidden behind CFS label" rate to all ME/CFS overstates the risk that a given red-flag search will find organic disease — feeding directly into the over-investigation concern (Category 10.2).

**Impact:** Kobayashi2022 supports *differential vigilance* (don't foreclose the search) but not a *high yield* of serious disease in ME/CFS. Combined with Stadje2016 (low yield) and Levine1992 (no excess malignancy), the balance favors targeted red-flag triggers over broad search — a reconciliation the synthesis reached but which the referral-centre bias makes fragile if Kobayashi is over-weighted. Mitigation: cite Kobayashi for the vigilance principle, not for prevalence; anchor yield expectations to primary-care/ME/CFS-appropriate denominators (Stadje2016, Levine1992).

**Non-specialist consequence:** The cases where a dangerous disease hid behind a mislabel came from a specialized clinic, so they overstate how often this happens for an average ME/CFS patient — the warning to stay vigilant is right, but the scare level should be lower.

### 12.5 Absence of ME/CFS-Specific Incidence Data Requires Heavy Population-Weight Discounting That the Certainty Scores Only Partly Convey

**Rationale:** Every one of the protocol's *incidence*-bearing claims (refeeding syndrome, VTE, pressure-ulcer sepsis, aspiration, focal-neurologic emergency yield, cardiac-syncope threshold) is transferred from a non-ME/CFS population (anorexia, general immobile/catatonia, general TIA/stroke, general primary care). The population-weight discount (0.75–0.80) embedded in each certainty score captures *population relevance* but not the *absence of direct ME/CFS measurement* — a qualitatively stronger gap than a weight penalty. For the core protocol claim that severe ME/CFS specifically faces elevated decompensation risk, there is no direct ME/CFS incidence number at all. This means the protocol's certainty about *which* triggers matter, and *how urgently*, rests entirely on transferable analogy (gap 4), and the cardiac-syncope threshold has no anchor at all (gap 1).

**Impact:** The protocol is defensible as *transferable safety-netting* (the mechanism is standard of care), but its *population-specific risk quantification* is unsupported. The Phase 2 decision to flag ME/CFS-specific gaps as `#open-question`/`#limitation` (rather than assert them) is the correct mitigation and must be maintained — any draft that implies ME/CFS-specific incidence where none exists would be fabricating. Mitigation: ensure every incidence-bearing claim carries an explicit "population-relevance transfer, no direct ME/CFS incidence data" provenance marker.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Much of what doctors will tell bedbound ME/CFS patients about these dangers is borrowed from other illnesses, because there are no direct studies of ME/CFS patients themselves — so the advice is sound standard care but the "how likely is this" numbers are not yet measured.

**Category 10 (not relevant):** The two most serious framing risks are (a) the protocol may only restate generic bedbound standard-of-care if severe ME/CFS complication incidence is not elevated (10.1), and (b) over-investigation/overtreatment if the red-flag list is applied beyond the genuinely high-risk subset (10.2).
