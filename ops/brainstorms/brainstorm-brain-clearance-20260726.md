## Category 1 — Novel Hypotheses

Hypotheses NOT currently in chapter, NOT in May 2026 brainstorm.

---

### H16: Glymphatic Reserve Capacity as Onset Risk Modifier

**Mechanistic rationale:** If brain clearance impairment is pathogenic in ME/CFS, then identical triggers (e.g., EBV infection, SARS-CoV-2, major stressor) should produce ME/CFS only in individuals whose baseline glymphatic-lymphatic reserve is already low. Genetic variants in AQP4 (aquaporin-4), VEGFC, VEGFR3 (FLT4), PROX1, FOXC2 — genes regulating meningeal lymphatic development, AQP4 polarization, and CSF production — may determine an individual's pre-morbid clearance reserve. A person with low-reserve variants could tolerate normal clearance demands but decompensate when an additional hit (sleep loss from acute illness, neuroinflammation from viral infection) tips them below functional threshold.

**Evidence link:** AQP4 SNPs associated with glymphatic function variability in human MRI studies (not yet done for ME/CFS). VEGF-C/VEGFR3 required for meningeal lymphatic development (Aspelund2015). PROX1 master regulator of lymphatic fate. ME/CFS has familial clustering and GWAS signals (though none yet in glymphatic genes). The "hit-and-run" onset pattern (sudden post-infectious onset) is consistent with a threshold-crossing model.

**Certainty:** 0.25 (genetic determinants of glymphatic capacity plausible; no ME/CFS genetic data in this pathway).

**Falsifiable prediction:** ME/CFS patients will show enrichment of loss-of-function or decreased-expression variants in AQP4, VEGFC, VEGFR3, PROX1 compared to healthy controls and to individuals with history of the same triggering infection who did NOT develop ME/CFS. Polygenic "glymphatic reserve score" will predict disease risk with OR > 2.0 in post-infectious onset cases. (Origin: brainstorm)

### H17: Circadian Phase Misalignment as Clearance Disruptor Independent of Sleep Duration

**Mechanistic rationale:** The chapter emphasizes reduced SWS as the mechanism linking sleep dysfunction to glymphatic impairment. But circadian biology provides an orthogonal pathway: glymphatic clearance is gated not just by sleep but by circadian phase. AQP4 expression is circadian-regulated; meningeal lymphatic permeability oscillates with a ~24h rhythm. If ME/CFS involves circadian phase delay (documented: delayed melatonin onset, shifted cortisol rhythm, delayed sleep phase common in patient surveys), clearance may occur at the wrong biological time even during sleep — desynchronized from the molecular clock that optimizes AQP4 polarization and vasomotion efficiency. This would explain why "sleeping enough hours" often fails to produce refreshing sleep: it's not the sleep, it's the timing.

**Evidence link:** Circadian disruption documented in ME/CFS (delayed melatonin, cortisol flattening). AQP4 expression shows circadian oscillation in rodent brain. Meningeal lymphatic function tied to circadian immune rhythms. Shift workers (circadian misalignment model) show impaired glymphatic clearance and elevated dementia risk. This hypothesis is mechanistically distinct from the "reduced SWS" mechanism — a patient could have normal sleep architecture at the wrong circadian phase and still have impaired clearance.

**Certainty:** 0.30 (circadian AQP4 regulation established; phase misalignment in ME/CFS documented; direct glymphatic-circadian coupling in humans untested).

**Falsifiable prediction:** ME/CFS patients with delayed sleep phase will show worse DTI-ALPS glymphatic index when sleeping at their habitual (delayed) time vs when experimentally advanced to an earlier bedtime (under controlled lab conditions). The phase angle between DLMO (dim light melatonin onset) and sleep onset will predict glymphatic efficiency more strongly than SWS duration alone. (Origin: brainstorm)

### H18: Meningeal Lymphatic Endothelial Glycocalyx Degradation by Systemic Oxidative Stress

**Mechanistic rationale:** The meningeal lymphatic endothelial glycocalyx — a carbohydrate-rich layer lining the luminal surface of lymphatic vessels — regulates permeability, prevents inappropriate immune cell adhesion, and maintains directional flow. Systemic oxidative stress (well-documented in ME/CFS: elevated 8-OHdG, reduced glutathione, lipid peroxidation) is a known glycocalyx-degrader across vascular beds. In ME/CFS, glycocalyx degradation could produce a "leaky lymphatic" phenotype: non-specific permeability to proteins and immune cells that paradoxically impairs *directional* clearance toward cervical lymph nodes while allowing CSF contents to leak into dural interstitial spaces or vice versa. This is distinct from the Chayama 2026 "inflammatory rerouting vs trapping" dichotomy — it's a structural degradation of the lymphatic barrier itself, not a functional routing switch.

**Evidence link:** Oxidative stress in ME/CFS (Chapter @ch:energy-metabolism). Glycocalyx degradation by ROS documented in vascular endothelium; lymphatic glycocalyx less studied but analogous. Glycocalyx shedding markers (syndecan-1, heparan sulfate) elevated in sepsis and inflammatory conditions; not measured in ME/CFS.

**Certainty:** 0.22 (vascular glycocalyx-ROS mechanism established; lymphatic glycocalyx extrapolated; no ME/CFS data).

**Falsifiable prediction:** ME/CFS patients will show elevated plasma glycocalyx shedding markers (syndecan-1, hyaluronan, heparan sulfate) correlating with oxidative stress measures (8-OHdG, F2-isoprostanes). Glycocalyx shedding will correlate with DTI-ALPS impairment. Antioxidant interventions (NAC, ALA) that reduce oxidative stress will also reduce glycocalyx shedding markers and improve DTI-ALPS. (Origin: brainstorm)

### H19: Qualitative Waste Composition Shift — Not Just Accumulation, But What Accumulates

**Mechanistic rationale:** The chapter treats clearance impairment as a quantitative problem (too much waste accumulating). But Chayama 2026's dual-speed glymphatics and exit-route specificity imply that different clearance pathways handle different molecular species: fast advective flow clears small solutes; slow diffusive exchange handles larger proteins; specific exit routes may have molecular size-selectivity via endothelial barrier properties. If ME/CFS impairs some pathways more than others, the *composition* of retained waste shifts — preferentially accumulating larger, more immunogenic proteins (e.g., S100B, HMGB1, extracellular mtDNA fragments) that activate TLR4/RAGE on microglia, while small metabolites (lactate, glutamate) continue to clear via preserved fast-flow routes. This qualitative shift toward DAMPs (damage-associated molecular patterns) would explain why neuroinflammation persists despite what should be only mild quantitative impairment — the retained waste is disproportionately inflammatory.

**Evidence link:** Chayama 2026 dual-speed architecture implies size-selectivity (not yet explicitly demonstrated for each route). DAMPs (HMGB1, S100B, mtDNA) are well-established microglial activators. ME/CFS shows microglial activation and elevated CSF cytokines without gross neurodegeneration (consistent with DAMP-driven rather than debris-driven inflammation).

**Certainty:** 0.20 (molecular size-selectivity of exit routes is theoretical; DAMP accumulation in ME/CFS CSF is unmeasured).

**Falsifiable prediction:** ME/CFS CSF will show disproportionate elevation of high-molecular-weight proteins (S100B dimer ~21kDa, HMGB1 ~25kDa, NfL ~68kDa) relative to small solutes (lactate ~90Da, glutamate ~147Da) compared to controls. The ratio of high-MW to low-MW CSF markers will correlate with microglial activation (TSPO-PET signal) and with PEM severity. (Origin: brainstorm)

### H20: Venous Backflow Counter-Current to Glymphatic Flow in Orthostatic Intolerance

**Mechanistic rationale:** Glymphatic CSF influx is driven by arterial pulsatility along periarterial spaces toward the brain parenchyma. This forward flow requires a pressure gradient: arterial > capillary > venous. In ME/CFS with orthostatic intolerance/POTS, venous pooling in the lower body during upright posture reduces venous return to the heart, which can increase cerebral venous pressure and produce retrograde venous pulsatility. If cerebral venous pressure oscillates in phase opposition to arterial pulsatility, the net pressure gradient driving perivascular CSF flow could be reduced or even reversed — venous backflow acting as a counter-current to glymphatic clearance. This mechanism would be posture-dependent (worse when upright), explaining why many patients report cognitive worsening when standing and improvement when supine beyond simple cerebral hypoperfusion.

**Evidence link:** POTS/OI documented in ME/CFS (Chapter @ch:autonomic-dysfunction). Cerebral venous hypertension documented in some POTS patients by MR venography. Glymphatic flow depends on arterial minus venous pressure gradient (mathematical models). Counter-current impairment is a fluid dynamics prediction not yet tested.

**Certainty:** 0.28 (venous hemodynamics in POTS established; glymphatic pressure-gradient coupling plausible; no direct measurement).

**Falsifiable prediction:** ME/CFS-POTS patients will show worse DTI-ALPS glymphatic index during upright (sitting/standing) MRI vs supine MRI, while ME/CFS patients without POTS and healthy controls will show no postural effect on DTI-ALPS. Internal jugular vein cross-sectional area (a proxy for cerebral venous pressure) will inversely correlate with DTI-ALPS in the upright position. Compression garments or leg muscle tensing that improve venous return will acutely improve upright DTI-ALPS. (Origin: brainstorm)

### H21: Dysregulated CSF Production as a Parallel Clearance Deficit

**Mechanistic rationale:** The chapter focuses on *drainage* impairment (glymphatic flow, lymphatic efflux) but not on CSF *production*. CSF is produced primarily by the choroid plexus at ~500 mL/day. Choroid plexus function is regulated by autonomic innervation (sympathetic reduces, parasympathetic increases production) and by inflammatory cytokines. In ME/CFS, sympathetic dominance (documented) combined with chronic low-grade inflammation could reduce CSF production, reducing the volume of fluid available to flush the brain regardless of drainage pathway patency. This is a supply-side failure — even with normal drainage, reduced CSF production would impair clearance. Notably, reduced CSF production would also reduce intracranial pressure and CSF turnover, both of which are independently associated with cognitive impairment.

**Evidence link:** Autonomic regulation of choroid plexus documented. Sympathetic dominance in ME/CFS documented. Choroid plexus inflammation and reduced CSF production documented in aging and some neuroinflammatory conditions. CSF production rate can be measured non-invasively by phase-contrast MRI at the cerebral aqueduct.

**Certainty:** 0.25 (choroid plexus autonomic regulation established; reduced CSF production in ME/CFS untested).

**Falsifiable prediction:** ME/CFS patients will show reduced CSF production rate (measured by phase-contrast MRI aqueductal flow) compared to controls, correlating with sympathetic tone (elevated plasma norepinephrine, reduced HRV). CSF production rate will correlate with DTI-ALPS more strongly than sleep quality, suggesting that reduced CSF supply is an independent contributor to clearance impairment. (Origin: brainstorm)

## Category 2 — Research Directions

Experiments NOT covered in May 2026 brainstorm (R1-R5).

---

### R6: GWAS/PRS of Glymphatic-Lymphatic Genes in ME/CFS Cohorts

**Objective:** Test H16 (glymphatic reserve hypothesis) by determining whether ME/CFS patients carry an excess of variants in genes regulating brain clearance capacity.

**Methods:**
- Extract genotype data from existing ME/CFS GWAS (DecodeME n>10,000; UK Biobank ME/CFS phenotypes) or new genotyping
- Target genes: AQP4, VEGFC, FLT4 (VEGFR3), PROX1, FOXC2, PDPN, LYVE1, CCL21, ACKR4
- Compare allele frequencies: ME/CFS vs healthy controls, ME/CFS vs infection-exposed-no-ME/CFS controls
- Construct polygenic "glymphatic reserve score" (GRS) from variants with functional annotation (eQTL, protein-truncating, missense predicted damaging)
- Secondary: test whether GRS predicts DTI-ALPS glymphatic index in a subset with imaging data

**Feasibility:** Medium. Existing GWAS data available. Gene list is defined. Need replication cohort.

**Impact:** Would establish whether genetic predisposition to low glymphatic capacity is an ME/CFS risk factor, providing orthogonal evidence (genetic, not inferential) for the brain clearance hypothesis.

**Timeline:** 2-3 years.

**Funding estimate:** $0.5M (genotyping if not available, analysis).

---

### R7: Pharmacological Challenge — Acute Glymphatic Enhancement as Diagnostic Test

**Objective:** Test whether enhancing glymphatic clearance produces acute cognitive and symptom improvement in ME/CFS — a within-subject pharmacological challenge that addresses the causal-vs-epiphenomenal question directly.

**Methods:**
- Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover: n=30 ME/CFS patients
- Intervention: single dose dexmedetomidine (α2-agonist, known to enhance glymphatic flow by promoting slow-wave-like EEG state and NE oscillation stabilization) at sub-sedative dose — OR — a sleep extension protocol (10h time-in-bed for 2 nights in lab)
- Pre- and post-intervention: DTI-ALPS, cognitive testing (processing speed, working memory), VAS symptoms
- Key: measure whether acute clearance enhancement (verified by pre-to-post DTI-ALPS change) produces acute symptom improvement

**Feasibility:** Medium. Dexmedetomidine requires IV administration and monitoring (ICU-level drug). Sleep extension protocol is lower risk but harder to enforce. Both feasible in sleep lab settings.

**Impact:** If enhancing clearance produces no symptom improvement, the brain clearance hypothesis loses the causal claim — consistent with epiphenomenal or compensatory interpretations. If it DOES produce improvement, this is the strongest evidence yet for a causative role.

**Timeline:** 2 years.

**Funding estimate:** $0.8M.

---

### R8: Simultaneous EEG-fMRI During Sleep with DTI-ALPS — Coupling Integrity Study

**Objective:** Test whether the normal coupling between slow-wave EEG activity and glymphatic flow is intact in ME/CFS, or whether there is a *decoupling* — normal-appearing SWS that fails to drive clearance.

**Methods:**
- n=40 ME/CFS patients, n=40 controls
- Overnight EEG-fMRI with DTI-ALPS sequences timed to sleep stages (wake, N2, N3/SWS, REM)
- Primary measure: within-subject correlation between delta power (0.5-4 Hz EEG) and DTI-ALPS glymphatic index across sleep stages
- Secondary: test whether NE oscillation proxy (pupillometry during wake, or skin conductance during sleep) predicts DTI-ALPS within each sleep stage
- Compare coupling coefficients: ME/CFS vs controls

**Feasibility:** Low-Medium. Simultaneous EEG-fMRI with glymphatic sequences is technically challenging (motion, sequence timing). Requires specialized MRI-compatible EEG and expertise.

**Impact:** Would determine whether the SWS-glymphatic coupling is intact or broken in ME/CFS. Intact coupling would suggest sleep optimization alone should help; broken coupling would suggest the problem is downstream of SWS and sleep interventions alone will be insufficient — requiring pharmacological vasomotion enhancement.

**Timeline:** 3-4 years.

**Funding estimate:** $1.5M.

---

### R9: Multisite CSF:Blood Ratio Validation in Existing ME/CFS Biobanks

**Objective:** Test the CSF:blood ratio biomarker hypothesis using existing stored samples without requiring new lumbar punctures.

**Methods:**
- Identify biobanks with paired CSF and plasma from ME/CFS patients and controls (NIH intramural study, Hornig 2017, SolveCFS Biobank, UK ME/CFS Biobank)
- Measure in paired CSF+plasma: NfL, GFAP, S100B, tau (total and p-tau181), UCH-L1
- Calculate CSF:blood ratio for each protein
- Compare ME/CFS vs controls, early vs late disease, mild vs severe
- Correlate with available clinical data (SF-36, cognitive testing, PEM frequency)
- Validate against DTI-ALPS in subset with imaging

**Feasibility:** High. Most assays are commercially available (Simoa for NfL/GFAP/tau). Paired CSF+plasma exists from prior studies. Does not require new lumbar punctures.

**Impact:** Would provide the first direct test of whether brain clearance is impaired in ME/CFS using a validated (in other diseases) biomarker. Positive result would transform the brain clearance hypothesis from inference to measurement. Negative result would severely constrain the hypothesis.

**Timeline:** 1-2 years.

**Funding estimate:** $0.3M (assay costs, sample access).

---

### R10: Meta-Analytic DTI-ALPS Across Chronic Diseases — Where Does ME/CFS Land If Measured?

**Objective:** Establish expected DTI-ALPS effect sizes across conditions with known glymphatic impairment, providing a benchmark for what ME/CFS DTI-ALPS studies should look like BEFORE collecting new data.

**Methods:**
- Systematic review of all published DTI-ALPS studies in human disease (AD, PD, MS, TBI, Long COVID, sleep disorders, hypertension, diabetes)
- Extract: DTI-ALPS index, sample size, MRI parameters, disease severity, control values
- Meta-regression: what is the DTI-ALPS effect size (Cohen's d) per condition? What is the minimum detectable effect in a typical n=50 study?
- Calculate: if ME/CFS clearance impairment is comparable to Long COVID (d ≈ 0.6-0.8), what sample size is needed for 80% power? If comparable to early AD (d ≈ 1.0-1.5), what sample size?

**Feasibility:** High. Literature review only. No new data collection.

**Impact:** Would provide statistical justification for sample size in future ME/CFS DTI-ALPS studies, preventing underpowered negative studies (which would be misinterpreted as absence of clearance impairment rather than inadequate sample). Also identifies methodological variability (DTI-ALPS protocols differ across sites — standardization needed before multisite ME/CFS study).

**Timeline:** 6-12 months.

**Funding estimate:** $0.05M (systematic review, meta-analysis).

---

### R11: Naturalistic Glymphatic Monitoring — 7-Day at-Home Proxy Protocol

**Objective:** Develop and validate a low-cost, at-home proxy for glymphatic function that can track day-to-day variability correlated with PEM, sleep quality, and activity — overcoming the single-timepoint limitation of MRI.

**Methods:**
- n=30 ME/CFS patients, 7-day at-home protocol
- Daily morning: cognitive test battery (processing speed, N-back), orthostatic vitals, VAS symptoms
- Wearable: sleep tracking (EEG headband — Dreem or similar — for SWS quantification), HRV (chest strap), continuous accelerometry (activity)
- Glymphatic proxy: morning-to-evening change in pupil light reflex (a putative LC-NE proxy via locus coeruleus-pupil coupling), combined with overnight HRV-derived autonomic oscillation power
- Validate against single-session DTI-ALPS at end of 7 days

**Feasibility:** Medium-High. Consumer wearables + cognitive apps + pupillometry app (smartphone). Low cost. High patient burden (7 days of daily testing).

**Impact:** Would create an accessible, scalable, longitudinal proxy for glymphatic function — enabling within-subject PEM-coupling studies that a single MRI cannot provide. Even if imperfect, a validated proxy would dramatically accelerate brain clearance research in ME/CFS.

**Timeline:** 1-2 years.

**Funding estimate:** $0.2M.

## Category 10 — Reasons Brain Clearance May NOT Be Relevant to ME/CFS

Arguments against relevance that should constrain the chapter's claims.

---

### NEG1: Symptom Topography Mismatch — Brain Fog Is Not Universal

**Why it may not be relevant:** If brain clearance failure were a central pathogenic mechanism, cognitive symptoms should be universal, dominant, and correlated with overall severity. But many ME/CFS patients — approximately 30-40% depending on cohort — report physical fatigue, post-exertional myalgia, and orthostatic intolerance as primary symptoms, with intact or minimally impaired cognition. Pure physical PEM (muscle pain, flu-like malaise without cognitive worsening) is incompatible with a brain-centric clearance model. Clearance failure would affect the entire brain, producing global cognitive impairment — yet ME/CFS cognitive profiles are heterogeneous (some patients show normal neuropsychological testing). If clearance impairment explains only a subset of symptoms in a subset of patients, it cannot be the unifying mechanism the chapter implies.

**Limits of this argument:** Brain clearance impairment could be regional (per Chayama 2026), affecting non-cognitive regions preferentially in some patients. Also, peripheral clearance systems (muscle glymphatic analogues?) could be co-impaired, producing physical without cognitive symptoms.

---

### NEG2: Normal Neurodegenerative Biomarker Trajectories Over Decades

**Why it may not be relevant:** Chronically impaired brain clearance should produce progressive accumulation of neurodegeneration-associated proteins (NfL, tau, amyloid-β, α-synuclein), eventually reaching levels comparable to Alzheimer's or Parkinson's disease — especially after decades of illness. Yet ME/CFS is not a progressive dementia. Cognitive decline in ME/CFS, while reported, is typically non-progressive and fluctuates with PEM rather than showing monotonic deterioration. Longitudinal studies (e.g., the NIH intramural study) have not reported progressive brain atrophy or biomarker accumulation exceeding normal aging. If the brain clearance hypothesis were correct, ME/CFS patients with 20+ years of illness should have NfL levels approaching those of mild cognitive impairment. This prediction is not borne out in available (limited) data.

**Limits of this argument:** CSF NfL and tau have not been systematically measured longitudinally in ME/CFS. The absence of documented elevation may reflect absence of measurement rather than absence of elevation. Also, if the clearance impairment is functional (reversible during remission periods) rather than structural (permanent), accumulation may be episodic and partially reversible, explaining the non-progressive course.

---

### NEG3: Spontaneous Recovery Rate Incompatible with Irreversible Structural Clearance Damage

**Why it may not be relevant:** Spontaneous recovery occurs in 5-12% of ME/CFS patients according to natural history studies. Full recovery implies that whatever pathophysiological process was present reversed completely. If brain clearance were impaired due to structural changes (perivascular fibrosis, AQP4 depolarization, meningeal lymphatic sclerosis), spontaneous reversal is unlikely — these are slowly reversible at best and often irreversible structural changes. The existence of recovery suggests that at least in some patients, the pathogenic mechanism is functional/reversible — but if it's functional, why does it not recover during the extended rest periods that characterize severe ME/CFS?

**Limits of this argument:** Recovery could occur through compensatory upregulation of alternative clearance routes (H5) or through resolution of the upstream drivers (sleep normalizes, autonomic function recovers, neuroinflammation resolves) that secondarily restores clearance. The chapter's open question already acknowledges the causal/compensatory/epiphenomenal distinction.

---

### NEG4: Normal Waking EEG Despite Predicted Global Encephalopathy

**Why it may not be relevant:** Chronic waste accumulation in the brain interstitial space — particularly of neuroactive metabolites (glutamate, lactate, adenosine) and inflammatory proteins — should produce EEG slowing (increased theta/delta power during wake), as seen in metabolic encephalopathies (hepatic, uremic), dementias, and even sleep deprivation. Resting EEG in ME/CFS, while sometimes showing modest changes (increased theta in frontal regions in some studies), is typically within normal limits or shows only subtle deviations. If years of impaired clearance were producing substantial interstitial metabolite accumulation, the EEG should look encephalopathic — it does not.

**Limits of this argument:** Quantitative EEG with source localization may detect abnormalities not visible in clinical EEG. Also, if clearance impairment is mild/moderate rather than severe, EEG changes may be below detection threshold. The comparison to metabolic encephalopathies may be inappropriately extreme — ME/CFS clearance impairment could be partial, analogous to mild sleep restriction rather than total sleep deprivation.

---

### NEG5: Temporal Mismatch Between Acute Onset and Gradual Clearance Failure

**Why it may not be relevant:** Approximately 50-75% of ME/CFS cases begin with an acute infectious episode, with onset over hours to days. Brain clearance impairment, at least the structural form (fibrosis, lymphatic sclerosis, AQP4 depolarization), should develop over weeks to months. Functional clearance impairment (from acute neuroinflammation disrupting routing, per H3) could be faster, but would also be expected to resolve as the triggering infection resolves — which it does not in ME/CFS. The sudden-onset pattern is more consistent with a threshold-crossing or bistable-system model (a normal physiology pushed into an abnormal steady state) than with a gradual accumulation of clearance deficits. Clearance impairment may be a consequence of entering that state, not the trigger.

**Limits of this argument:** Immune-mediated mechanisms (autoantibodies against AQP4, for example) could produce acute functional clearance impairment on the same timescale as infectious onset. Also, the "PEM ratchet" model proposes that clearance impairment develops after onset through repeated cycles of exertion-triggered waste accumulation — the trigger is infection, but clearance failure develops through subsequent PEM cycles.

---

### NEG6: Animal Model Prediction Failure — No PEM-Like Phenotype from Combined Clearance Insults

**Why it may not be relevant:** If the brain clearance hypothesis is correct, then experimentally combining known clearance-impairing factors (sleep deprivation + autonomic dysfunction via chemical sympathectomy or chronic NE infusion + neuroinflammation via LPS or poly(I:C)) should produce a behavioral phenotype resembling PEM: delayed symptom exacerbation after exertion, reduced activity, cognitive impairment, and inflammatory surges after challenge. No such animal model has been demonstrated. While individual components have been studied (sleep deprivation does impair glymphatic function; LPS does impair meningeal lymphatics), the combined-insult model producing PEM-like delayed behavioral responses has not been shown. This is a missing experimental link in the entire framework.

**Limits of this argument:** Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — no one has attempted this specific combined-insult model. PEM is complex and may require CNS-immune coupling that rodent models cannot fully capture. Also, the timescale of PEM (12-48 hours) is long relative to typical rodent behavioral experiments, which are usually conducted over hours, not days.

---

### NEG7: Alternative Convergence — Every Organ System Converges Given the Same Inputs

**Why it may not be relevant:** The chapter's central rhetorical move is to show that multiple documented ME/CFS features (sleep dysfunction, autonomic impairment, neuroinflammation) "converge" on glymphatic impairment. But this is reverse-engineered reasoning. The same features converge on mitochondrial dysfunction (sleep regulates mitophagy, autonomic tone regulates mitochondrial biogenesis, inflammation impairs OXPHOS), on endothelial dysfunction (sleep regulates endothelial repair, autonomic tone controls vascular tone, inflammation damages endothelium), on immune dysfunction, on HPA axis dysfunction, and so on. The "convergent" argument is not specific to brain clearance — it's a generic property of any physiological system that is sensitive to the same set of upstream dysfunctions. The argument does not differentiate brain clearance from other candidate mechanisms.

**Limits of this argument:** Specificity is a valid critique, but the brain clearance hypothesis provides testable predictions (DTI-ALPS, CSF:blood ratio, PEM waste accumulation) that other convergent hypotheses do not. The value of the hypothesis is in its falsifiable predictions, not in the rhetorical strength of the convergence argument.

## Category 11 — Null Hypothesis Assessment

---

### Formal Null Hypothesis

**H₀:** Brain clearance impairment has no causative, contributory, or mediating role in ME/CFS symptoms, disability, or pathophysiology. Observed associations between ME/CFS features (sleep dysfunction, autonomic impairment, neuroinflammation, cervical pathology) and glymphatic-lymphatic pathways are either epiphenomenal or consequences of inactivity/deconditioning rather than disease-specific mechanisms.

**Alternative hypothesis (H₁):** Brain clearance impairment is a genuine pathophysiological feature of ME/CFS that contributes to symptom generation, particularly PEM, brain fog, and unrefreshing sleep, and that can be detected by direct measurement (DTI-ALPS, CSF:blood ratio) and modulated by intervention.

---

### What the World Looks Like Under H₀

| Domain | Prediction under H₀ |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Imaging** | DTI-ALPS glymphatic index normal in ME/CFS vs activity-matched controls (i.e., no difference after controlling for physical inactivity); any observed differences explained by deconditioning and supine time |
| **Biomarkers** | CSF:blood ratio of brain-derived proteins normal in ME/CFS; NfL, GFAP, tau, S100B at normal concentrations in both CSF and plasma |
| **Sleep-glymphatic coupling** | Glymphatic function (measured by DTI-ALPS) normalizes to control levels when ME/CFS patients are given adequate sleep opportunity; no decoupling between SWS and clearance |
| **PEM** | Post-exertional symptom exacerbation is NOT accompanied by accumulation of CNS-derived proteins in plasma; no correlation between post-exertional CNS protein levels and PEM severity |
| **Intervention** | Enhancing glymphatic clearance (sleep extension, pharmacological vasomotion enhancement, osmotic agents) produces no improvement in ME/CFS symptoms beyond placebo |
| **Natural history** | Recovery from ME/CFS is NOT associated with improvement in DTI-ALPS or CSF:blood ratio; clearance metrics stable regardless of clinical trajectory |
| **Genetics** | ME/CFS patients show no enrichment of variants in AQP4, VEGF-C, or other glymphatic-lymphatic genes |
| **Neurodegeneration** | CSF NfL, tau, amyloid-β trajectories in ME/CFS identical to healthy aging — no evidence of accelerated accumulation |

---

### Prior Probability Assessment

**Step 1: Is glymphatic-lymphatic impairment present in ME/CFS?**
- Prior: moderate (~0.50). The convergent-dysfunction argument is plausible but entirely inferential. Other conditions with similar features (sleep disorders, dysautonomia, neuroinflammation) DO show glymphatic impairment. But the argument could be non-specific (NEG7).
- Evidence needed: DTI-ALPS or CSF:blood ratio data in ME/CFS. None exists yet.

**Step 2: If present, is it a contributor to symptoms rather than an epiphenomenon?**
- Prior: lower (~0.25). Even if glymphatic function is impaired, it could be downstream of sleep dysfunction, inactivity, and autonomic impairment — a readout of the sick state, not a driver.
- Evidence needed: interventional study showing that clearance enhancement improves symptoms; or longitudinal study showing clearance impairment precedes symptom worsening (temporal priority). Neither exists.

**Step 3: If contributory, is it the dominant mechanism or one among many?**
- Prior: low (~0.10). ME/CFS affects multiple organ systems; a single-system mechanism is unlikely to explain the full phenotype.
- Evidence needed: mediation analysis showing that clearance impairment explains a large proportion of symptom variance. Does not exist.

**Combined prior that brain clearance is BOTH impaired AND causally contributes to symptoms: ~0.50 × 0.25 = ~0.13.** This is low but not negligible — it reflects the complete absence of direct evidence rather than evidence against.

---

### Study That Would Definitive Address the Null

**Design:** Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial of glymphatic enhancement.

- **Intervention arm:** Sleep extension to 10h time-in-bed for 7 days + low-dose clonidine (0.05mg at bedtime, NE oscillation stabilizer) + head-of-bed elevation 15°
- **Control arm:** Habitual sleep + placebo + flat bed
- **Outcomes:** DTI-ALPS (pre and post each arm), cognitive testing (processing speed, N-back), VAS fatigue and brain fog, daily PEM diary
- **n=50**, crossover, 2-week washout
- **Key result:** If active arm increases DTI-ALPS by >15% (expected from sleep extension + clonidine) but produces NO clinical improvement beyond placebo, the null hypothesis survives — clearance enhancement does not translate to clinical benefit. If active arm produces BOTH DTI-ALPS improvement AND clinical improvement (>0.5 SD on primary outcome), the null is rejected.

**Cost:** ~$1.2M. **Value:** This single study would answer the central question the chapter leaves open — causal vs epiphenomenal.

## Category 12 — Evidence Quality Concerns

Systematic weaknesses in the evidence base underlying the chapter.

---

### EQ1: Complete Absence of ME/CFS-Specific Data

**Nature:** The entire chapter is built on inference from general physiology and other diseases. Zero studies have measured any aspect of brain clearance — glymphatic flow, meningeal lymphatic function, CSF:blood ratio, DTI-ALPS, CSF tracer kinetics — in ME/CFS patients. This is not a minor evidence gap; it is the complete absence of direct evidence. The chapter's most confident statements (e.g., "sleep dysfunction in ME/CFS would predictably reduce glymphatic waste clearance") use language of inevitability that the evidence does not support — "would predictably" should be "might, if the extrapolation is valid."

**Consequence:** The chapter should be read as a hypothesis document, not an evidence review. Every mechanistic claim is conditional on an unmeasured premise.

---

### EQ2: Animal-to-Human Extrapolation Unvalidated for Glymphatic-Me/CFS Bridge

**Nature:** Most glymphatic-lymphatic physiology derives from rodent studies using invasive methods (intrathecal tracer injection, in vivo two-photon imaging through cranial windows, post-mortem histology) that have no human equivalent. Translating from mouse to human involves multiple uncertain steps:
- Brain size difference (~500× volume) alters clearance distances and timescales
- Upright posture in humans creates gravitational gradients absent in rodents
- Human sleep architecture differs (polyphasic rodent sleep vs monophasic human sleep)
- Rodent studies are typically young adult animals; ME/CFS patients span ages 10-80
- Anesthesia used in many rodent glymphatic studies may artifactually enhance or suppress clearance

**Consequence:** Quantitative predictions from rodent data (e.g., "fast flow ~3 μm/s") may not apply to humans. The chapter appropriately cites human studies where available (DTI-ALPS, CSF oscillations in sleep) but much of the mechanistic detail is rodent-derived.

---

### EQ3: DTI-ALPS as a Proxy — What Does It Actually Measure?

**Nature:** DTI-ALPS (diffusion tensor imaging along the perivascular space) measures water diffusivity along the direction of deep medullary veins, interpreted as a proxy for perivascular CSF flow. However:
- DTI-ALPS has not been validated against gold-standard glymphatic measures (intrathecal tracer) in the same human subjects
- The signal may reflect perivascular space geometry (width, tortuosity) rather than actual flow
- DTI-ALPS can be influenced by factors unrelated to glymphatics: white matter integrity, interstitial edema, vascular pulsatility, head motion
- Test-retest reliability is moderate (ICC ~0.6-0.7), limiting its use for within-subject tracking
- Different MRI scanners and protocols produce systematically different DTI-ALPS values, complicating multisite studies

**Consequence:** DTI-ALPS is the best available non-invasive measure, but it remains an imperfect proxy. Claims about "glymphatic function" based solely on DTI-ALPS should specify that the measure is an indirect proxy of uncertain specificity.

---

### EQ4: Confounding by Physical Inactivity and Supine Time

**Nature:** ME/CFS patients spend more time resting, supine, and inactive than healthy controls. These behavioral differences are consequences of the disease but could affect glymphatic measures in either direction:
- **Masking true impairment:** Supine position maximizes glymphatic flow (gravitational advantage). If DTI-ALPS is measured supine (as all MRI is), the postural confound could artificially elevate DTI-ALPS in ME/CFS patients relative to their habitual upright DTI-ALPS, masking true impairment.
- **Creating spurious impairment:** Prolonged inactivity and bed rest may decondition the vascular system, reducing arterial pulsatility independent of disease-specific processes. An apparent glymphatic impairment could reflect deconditioning, not primary pathophysiology.
- **Selection bias:** Severe ME/CFS patients (most inactive) may be unable to tolerate MRI, excluding the patients most likely to show extreme clearance impairment from studies.

**Consequence:** Any future DTI-ALPS study must include activity-matched controls (e.g., patients with other fatiguing conditions with similar activity levels) or objectively measure activity (accelerometry) for covariate adjustment. Without this, DTI-ALPS differences between ME/CFS and healthy controls are uninterpretable.

### EQ5: Chayama 2026 as Single-Source Dependency — Concentration Risk

**Nature:** The chapter's most specific mechanistic claims derive from a single model:
- Nearest-exit routing → Chayama 2026
- Mid-cervical transit node → Chayama 2026
- Bioelectric dural entry → Chayama 2026
- Skull-border B-cell tolerance → Chayama 2026 (with Yang 2026 contribution)
- Dual-speed glymphatics → Chayama 2026 + Toscano 2026 (only 2 sources)

If Chayama 2026 fails independent replication — a nontrivial risk for any paradigm-shifting, recently published model — the chapter would lose approximately 60% of its mechanistic specificity. The earlier brainstorm (May 2026) heavily cites Chayama/Yang/Toscano as sources. The field needs 3-5 years to independently replicate (or refute) this model.

**Consequence:** The chapter's lim_Chayama appropriately flags this. But the brainstorm text should be even more explicit: ideas that depend on nearest-exit routing, mid-cervical node, or bioelectric entry should be labeled as Chayama-model-dependent, not as established brain clearance physiology.

### EQ6: Publication Bias in the Young Glymphatic Field

**Nature:** The glymphatic field emerged in 2012 and has grown rapidly. Positive glymphatic-disease associations are exciting and publishable; null results (e.g., "glymphatic function is normal in condition X despite plausible reasons for impairment") may go unpublished or be relegated to low-impact journals. This inflates the apparent robustness of glymphatic-disease associations. Known publication bias indicators:
- Small study sizes dominate the field (typical n=20-40 per group)
- Few preregistered studies
- High proportion of "first report" findings
- Rapidly growing literature with few systematic reviews or meta-analyses

**Consequence:** The literature the chapter draws on may overestimate the strength and consistency of glymphatic-disease associations. The chapter should note that the field is young and that publication bias may inflate the apparent evidence base.

---

### EQ7: Reverse Causation — Symptoms → Clearance Impairment, Not Vice Versa

**Nature:** The chapter's causal direction is uniformly: brain clearance impairment → symptoms. But the reverse is equally plausible and more parsimonious:
- If you have severe fatigue and cannot be physically active → reduced arterial pulsatility → reduced glymphatic flow
- If you have unrefreshing sleep → reduced SWS → reduced glymphatic clearance
- If you have POTS and autonomic dysfunction → impaired vasomotion → reduced fast advective flow
- If you spend most time supine → altered CSF hydrodynamics → DTI-ALPS changes

In this interpretation, brain clearance impairment is a secondary consequence of the core symptoms, not a contributor. This interpretation does not require any new mechanism — it is simply a downstream readout of the known symptom profile. The chapter's open question (causal/compensatory/epiphenomenal) acknowledges this but the rest of the chapter's prose defaults to causal language.

**Consequence:** Until longitudinal studies demonstrate temporal priority (clearance impairment precedes symptom worsening) or interventional studies show symptom improvement with clearance enhancement, reverse causation cannot be excluded.

---

### EQ8: Convergent-Narrative Construction as Confirmation Bias Amplifier

**Nature:** The chapter's central argument (Section "Brain Clearance in ME/CFS: Convergent Drivers of Impairment") enumerates five features (sleep dysfunction, autonomic dysfunction, neuroinflammation, cervical pathology, immune activation) and shows that each can impair glymphatic-lymphatic function. This is reverse-engineered reasoning:
1. Start with the desired conclusion (brain clearance is impaired)
2. Find documented ME/CFS features
3. For each feature, find a pathway by which it could impair clearance
4. Present the ensemble as converging evidence

The problem: this can be done for ANY candidate mechanism. The same five features impair mitochondria, endothelium, HPA axis, immune function, and so on. The "convergent" narrative does not discriminate between mechanisms — it provides equal rhetorical support for all of them. This is not evidence; it's hypothesis-generating pattern-matching.

**Consequence:** The chapter should explicitly note that the convergent-drivers argument supports the *plausibility* of the brain clearance hypothesis but does not provide *evidence for* it over alternative mechanisms. The argument establishes that brain clearance deserves investigation, not that it is probably impaired.

---

### EQ9: Missing Negative Control Predictions

**Nature:** A well-formed scientific hypothesis should specify not only what it predicts but what it predicts should NOT be true. The chapter does this only implicitly. Explicit negative control predictions would include:
- If brain clearance is the mechanism, then interventions that improve clearance should improve ME/CFS symptoms — and interventions that improve ME/CFS symptoms via non-clearance mechanisms should NOT improve clearance metrics
- If the PEM ratchet is correct, then ME/CFS patients who have been in prolonged remission (years without PEM cycles) should show normal or normalized DTI-ALPS, not persistently impaired
- If regional routing failure drives symptom topography, then DTI-ALPS in brain regions unrelated to a patient's symptom profile should be normal

**Consequence:** Without negative control predictions, the hypothesis is unfalsifiable in practice — any pattern of results can be accommodated post hoc. The chapter (and this brainstorm) should include predictions of what should NOT happen if the hypothesis is correct.
