### H1: The Epidemiological Transition Decoupling Gap

**Category:** 1 — Novel Hypothesis
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Countries undergo epidemiological transition from high infectious-disease burden to high non-communicable-disease (NCD) burden as they develop. ME/CFS spans both: triggered by infections (infectious-era burden) but managed as a chronic condition (NCD-era healthcare). This creates a structural decoupling: the infections that trigger ME/CFS peak during the infectious-disease phase of a country's transition, but the diagnostic infrastructure to recognise ME/CFS arrives 15–25 years later, during the NCD phase. The result is a moving diagnostic blind spot — the peak trigger exposure cohort has aged or died by the time diagnostic capacity exists, and the "current" prevalence estimate counts only the residual cases detected by the new diagnostic infrastructure. The gap size is proportional to the speed of transition: rapid transition (China, Brazil) → large gap (trigger burden high, diagnostic infrastructure catching up); slow transition (Sub-Saharan Africa) → unknown gap (trigger burden still high, diagnostic infrastructure not yet arrived). Iran — an upper-middle-income country mid-transition with emerging research capacity — provides the first empirical datapoint: 17.5% post-COVID CFS prevalence matches HIC rates, suggesting convergence as diagnostic infrastructure arrives @Simani2021iranCFS. The hypothesis predicts that India, Brazil, South Africa, and Indonesia — countries in mid-transition — are where the gap between true prevalence and diagnosed prevalence is maximal, because trigger burden is still high and diagnostic infrastructure is exactly half-built.

**Evidence link:** Epidemiological transition theory (Omran 1971, refined by Frenk 1989). Condé2026dengueFatigue documents trigger burden in LMICs. Vester2026burdenOfDisease documents zero non-HIC burden estimates. Simani2021iranCFS provides the single datapoint at a transition point. The decoupling lag duration (15–25 years) is a hypothesis, not an estimate — no empirical transition-duration study for post-infectious syndromes exists. The prediction is structurally analogous to the "cancer transition" — as infectious mortality falls, cancer diagnosis rates rise, but the true incidence may not be changing.

**Certainty:** 0.40 (transition theory is well-established; the decoupling mechanism is logically sound; Iran datapoint is consistent with convergence but is a single study; the 15–25 year lag is speculative; requires distinguishing between increased diagnosis (detection artefact) and increased true incidence (transition effect) — which the hypothesis itself predicts are conflated).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In countries classified as upper-middle-income by World Bank, ME/CFS diagnosis rates increase with time since the country's peak infectious-disease mortality year, with a lag of 15–25 years — controlling for healthcare expenditure per capita and neurologist density. (b) Lower-middle-income countries that have not yet transitioned show flat near-zero diagnosis rates regardless of healthcare expenditure — the diagnostic infrastructure hasn't crossed the detection threshold. (c) Post-dengue fatigue cohorts aged 40–60 in MICs (Vietnam, Brazil) who meet ME/CFS criteria will report that no healthcare provider ever mentioned ME/CFS — the gap is in the diagnostic system, not in the patients. (d) Falsified if: ME/CFS diagnosis rates in MICs are predicted by healthcare expenditure alone, with no transition-phase interaction — then the decoupling hypothesis is unnecessary; simple resource-limitation explains the gap.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Countries with the most ME/CFS-triggering infections have the least capacity to diagnose ME/CFS — and by the time they build that capacity, the patients who need it have already lived decades without a name for their illness.

### H2: The ICD-11 Empty-Vessel Hypothesis

**Category:** 1 — Novel Hypothesis
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** ME/CFS was coded as G93.3 in ICD-10 and 8E49 in ICD-11. The WHO's classification expansion is a global-level policy achievement. But the pathway from ICD code to patient benefit requires: (a) national health information systems adopting ICD-11, (b) physicians trained to recognise ME/CFS and assign the code, (c) diagnostic criteria deployable without laboratory exclusion work-up, (d) disability systems that recognise the code and provide benefits, and (e) clinical management guidelines that give physicians something to do after assigning the code. In most LMICs, none of these conditions hold. The ICD code is globally available; the implementation infrastructure that converts a code into a patient benefit is not. The "empty vessel" hypothesis: ICD-11 8E49 produces zero measurable change in diagnosis rates, disability recognition, or clinical management for patients in countries below a minimum implementation threshold (defined by at least 3 of the 5 conditions above being met). The code exists on paper. It changes nothing on the ground. It is not merely insufficient — it is an example of global-health theatre: a policy instrument that appears to address a gap while being structurally incapable of doing so in the settings where the gap is largest.

**Evidence link:** Podell2020documentingDisability documents the documentation burden in the US even with SSDI — i.e., a code is not enough even in the best-resourced disability system. Sarker2024bangladeshRCTprotocol uses Fukuda criteria (not IOM 2015) — the ICD code's existence didn't determine criteria choice; pragmatic deliverability did. No study has audited ICD-11 8E49 adoption rates by country. The "global-health theatre" concept is drawn from critical global-health literature (Kenworthy 2017, Storeng 2014 on policy without implementation) but has not been applied to ME/CFS.

**Certainty:** 0.55 (the implementation-gap argument is structural — codification without capacity building is a known global-health pattern; the claim that ICD adoption produces "zero" change is strong and likely observable within 5–10 years as ICD-11 adoption data becomes available; in countries where NONE of the five conditions hold, the prediction is that diagnosis rates remain at zero — which is nearly tautological).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Five years post-ICD-11 adoption in a country, ME/CFS diagnosis rates per 100,000 population are unchanged from pre-adoption rates if the country lacks physician training + diagnostic-algorithm adaptations + disability-system recognition — testing the empty-vessel claim directly. (b) Countries that adopt ICD-11 AND implement at least 3 of the 5 infrastructure conditions show a detectable increase in diagnosis rates — the code is not empty when it has infrastructure. (c) ME/CFS patient advocacy organisations in LMICs report that ICD-11 adoption has had "no effect" or "don't know" at higher rates than HIC organisations — patient-reported null impact. (d) Falsified if: ICD-11 adoption alone (without infrastructure) produces a ≥20% increase in coded ME/CFS diagnoses in any country — then the code has independent signalling power.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Getting ME/CFS into the ICD-11 was a victory — but if no one in the countries with the most patients can use the code, the victory is a monument, not a functioning door.

### R1: Sentinel PEM Surveillance in Post-Arbovirus Cohorts

**Category:** 2 — Research Direction
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Objective:** Generate the first systematic prevalence data on post-infectious ME/CFS in dengue- and chikungunya-endemic LMICs by adding a minimal PEM-screening module to existing post-arbovirus cohort follow-up protocols.

**Rationale:** The chapter documents that post-dengue and post-chikungunya fatigue is real (Condé2026dengueFatigue, Duvignaud2018chikungunyaFatigue) but no study has applied ME/CFS diagnostic criteria to post-arbovirus cohorts in LMICs. Existing dengue and chikungunya cohort studies in Brazil, India, Vietnam, and Thailand already conduct follow-up visits for joint pain, neurological sequelae, and mortality. Adding a 5-item PEM screen (DSQ-PEM subscale or adapted single-item PEM question validated for translation) plus a 1-minute sit-to-stand test (low-tech orthostatic intolerance proxy) would cost <US$5 per participant and require no lab infrastructure. Sentinel surveillance at 5–10 sentinel sites in 3–4 arbovirus-endemic countries would generate the first prevalence estimates for post-arboviral ME/CFS — transforming the chapter's claim from "hidden burden inferred from chikungunya fatigue data" to "prevalence estimate with a confidence interval."

**Methods:** (a) Identify existing post-dengue/chikungunya/Zika cohort studies with planned follow-up visits (contact PIs at FIOCRUZ Brazil, AIIMS India, OUCRU Vietnam, Mahidol Thailand), (b) add a 10-minute module: DSQ-PEM (5 items, translated + back-translated), orthostatic symptoms questionnaire, 1-minute sit-to-stand with pre/post heart rate (manual pulse or smartphone PPG), (c) apply IOM 2015 SEID criteria retrospectively from the collected data, (d) report prevalence with 95% CI stratified by infecting pathogen, sex, age, and time since acute infection (6, 12, 24 months), (e) fund via small-grant mechanisms (NIH R21 or equivalent) — total cost estimate US$150K–300K for 5 sites.

**Evidence link:** Condé2026dengueFatigue (dengue fatigue prevalence data, identifies post-dengue PEM gap), Duvignaud2018chikungunyaFatigue (Reunion Island post-chikungunya fatigue at 30 months). DSQ-PEM validated for post-infectious PEM screening (Cotler2018, Jason2020). 1-minute sit-to-stand as orthostatic intolerance proxy validated against tilt-table testing (Sutton2022). Sentinal surveillance model adapted from WHO's Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) framework — adding a chronic outcome to an acute-disease surveillance system.

**Certainty of feasibility:** 0.60 (cohorts exist; module is minimal, low-cost, and non-invasive; PEM screening in translated instruments is methodologically challenging — cultural equivalence of "exertion" and "worsening" not established for all settings; PI buy-in depends on competing priorities for limited follow-up visit time; but the cost is low enough that a pilot at 1–2 sites is achievable with modest funding).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Post-dengue ME/CFS prevalence (IOM 2015 criteria, ≥6 months post-infection) is ≥5% — comparable to post-EBV ME/CFS rates in HICs. (b) Post-dengue PEM prevalence is higher than post-dengue fatigue-without-PEM prevalence — the PEM criterion distinguishes post-infectious ME/CFS from generic post-infection fatigue. (c) Prevalence does not differ significantly between dengue, chikungunya, and Zika post-infectious cohorts — a common post-viral pathway rather than pathogen-specific fatigue. (d) Falsified if: post-dengue ME/CFS prevalence (IOM criteria) is <1% across all sentinel sites — then post-arboviral ME/CFS is rare despite post-arboviral fatigue being common, and the chapter's hidden-burden thesis for arboviral regions is wrong.

**Non-specialist consequence:** For US$300K — less than the cost of one NIH R01 — we could answer whether millions of dengue survivors in Asia and Latin America have unrecognised ME/CFS. The fact this hasn't been done is the research-equivalent of the diagnostic gap the chapter describes.

### R2: Culturally Adapted PEM Screening Tool for the WHO South-East Asia Region

**Category:** 2 — Research Direction
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Objective:** Develop and validate a PEM-screening questionnaire in Hindi, Bengali, and Swahili that accounts for cultural variation in exertion concepts, symptom expression idioms, and the rest/work boundary — generating the first culturally adapted ME/CFS screening instrument for non-Western settings.

**Rationale:** The chapter argues that Western ME/CFS criteria may undercount cases in non-Western settings because symptom expression is culturally moderated (Ware1992cultureSomatic, Lee1998neurastheniaChina). But no culturally adapted screening tool exists. The IOM criteria operationalise PEM as "worsening of symptoms after physical, cognitive, or emotional exertion that was previously tolerated." In agrarian/subsistence-livelihood settings, "exertion" has no discrete boundary — work is continuous, rest is minimal, and "previously tolerated" has no reference point because the demand never stopped. In South Asian idioms, "weakness" (kamzori) may substitute for "fatigue" as the primary complaint. In Swahili-speaking East Africa, "thinking too much" (kufikiri sana) may capture cognitive PEM better than "brain fog." The tool must be adapted, not just translated.

**Methods:** (a) Phase 1: Cognitive interviews (n=15–20 chronic fatigue patients per language, 3 languages, purposive sampling from primary care clinics in Delhi, Dhaka, and Nairobi) to elicit local idioms for PEM, fatigue, orthostatic intolerance, and cognitive impairment, (b) Phase 2: Draft culturally adapted PEM questions (back-translated, reviewed by bilingual ME/CFS clinicians and medical anthropologists), (c) Phase 3: Validation against clinical diagnosis (IOM 2015 criteria applied by a trained physician, gold-standard) in n=100 primary care patients per site with unexplained chronic fatigue, reporting sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and AUC with 95% CI per language, (d) Phase 4: Open-source release of validated instruments with implementation guides for primary care use.

**Evidence link:** Ware1992cultureSomatic (cultural idioms shape symptom expression), Lee2000HKPrimaryCare (different illness experience in Chinese primary care), Starcevic1999neurasthenia (conceptual mapping). DSQ-PEM (Cotler2018) provides the base instrument. WHO mhGAP adaptation methodology for mental health screening tools in LMICs provides a validated translation-adaptation protocol (Kohrt2018 cultural adaptation of psychological instruments). Translating "PEM" is not enough — the concept must be culturally calibrated.

**Certainty of feasibility:** 0.50 (cognitive interview methodology is standard in transcultural psychiatry and medical anthropology; the adaptation process is labour-intensive but low-cost; primary care sites with chronic fatigue patients exist in all three settings — the question is whether they will participate; the validation study requires a trained physician applying IOM criteria as gold-standard — the same diagnostic-capacity limitation the tool is designed to address; the gold-standard problem creates an inherent circularity: we need culturally adapted criteria to identify patients to validate the culturally adapted tool).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Sensitivity and specificity of the culturally adapted tool will be ≥0.80 against IOM criteria applied by a trained physician — the tool detects cases that would be missed by direct translation of the DSQ-PEM. (b) The culturally adapted tool identifies ≥20% more PEM-positive patients than the directly translated DSQ-PEM in the same population — the adaptation adds incremental detection. (c) The most discriminative PEM item differs by language/culture — e.g., "brain fog after reading" in English-validated DSQ may be replaced by "confusion after bargaining at market" in Hindi-adapted version. (d) Falsified if: the adapted tool performs identically to the translated tool — then cultural adaptation adds no detection value and direct translation is sufficient, implying the chapter's cultural-variation concern is practically negligible for screening.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Before we can count ME/CFS patients in India or Bangladesh, we need a way to ask about PEM that makes sense in Hindi, Bengali, and Swahili — not just a translation of "do you feel worse after exertion?" to someone who has never had the luxury of distinguishing exertion from survival.

### D1: WHO Essential Medicines List ME/CFS Symptom-Domain Repurposing Cascade

**Category:** 3 — Drug/Medication Idea
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** ME/CFS-specific pharmacological trials are concentrated in HICs, use drugs not on the WHO Essential Medicines List (EML), and have produced no licenced therapy. In LMICs, the pharmacological question is not "what novel compound shows efficacy?" but "which of the drugs already available through the EML can be repurposed for ME/CFS symptom domains?" The EML contains drugs with plausible mechanisms for each of the four cardinal ME/CFS symptom domains: (a) PEM/post-exertional malaise — low-dose naltrexone (naltrexone 50mg is on EML for opioid dependence; LDN 1.5–4.5mg uses the same drug at a fraction of the dose, costs ~US$0.10/day), (b) orthostatic intolerance/POTS — propranolol (EML, beta-blocker, US$0.01/day) or ivabradine (not on EML but generic and US$0.10/day), (c) sleep disturbance — amitriptyline 10–25mg (EML, tricyclic, US$0.02/day), melatonin (not EML but widely available), (d) pain — amitriptyline (already captured), gabapentin (EML, US$0.05/day). The repurposing cascade is: (1) systematic mapping of EML drugs to ME/CFS symptom domains by mechanistic plausibility and pharmacokinetic suitability, (2) pragmatic open-label n-of-1 trials (patient as own control) — the cheapest design that produces patient-level evidence — for the top 3–5 candidates, (3) if n-of-1 signals are positive, stepped-wedge cluster RCT in primary care settings in 2–3 LMICs, using the EML drug at EML pricing. This is not a novel-compound discovery pathway — it is a pragmatic repurposing pathway that asks: "what can we do with the drugs the system already buys?"

**Evidence link:** LDN in ME/CFS: patient-reported evidence (Bolton2019, Younger2019), no RCT data, but naltrexone is WHO EML-listed and LDN costs pennies. Propranolol for POTS: Raj2013 consensus, Shepherd2021 children, Raj2014 propranolol vs placebo — small trials but positive signal. Amitriptyline for sleep/pain in chronic fatigue: off-label use, some evidence in fibromyalgia. N-of-1 trial methodology: validated in chronic pain (Scuffham2010, Nikles2011) and increasingly applied in LMIC settings for NCD management. Stepped-wedge design: appropriate when equipoise exists (all patients should eventually receive the intervention) and logistical constraints prevent simultaneous rollout. EML systematic mapping: no ME/CFS-specific EML mapping exists — the closest is the WHO mhGAP guidelines mapping psychotropic medications to mental health conditions.

**Certainty:** 0.35 (EML mapping is straightforward desk research; n-of-1 trials in LMIC primary care are feasible but logistically intensive; LDN evidence is patient-reported, not RCT — using it in LMIC settings ahead of HIC RCT evidence inverts the standard evidence-translation pathway and raises ethical questions; the "drugs the system already buys" argument depends on actual EML procurement — which varies by country and facility level; amitriptyline and propranolol are generic and cheap but not necessarily stocked at primary care level).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) At least 3 WHO EML drugs have plausible mechanisms for ≥2 ME/CFS symptom domains — the repurposing pool is non-empty. (b) N-of-1 trials of LDN (1.5–4.5mg) in ME/CFS patients in an LMIC setting show a ≥30% reduction in PEM severity in ≥50% of completers — the signal translates across settings. (c) Stepped-wedge cluster RCT of EML-based symptom management vs usual care shows improved SF-36 physical function at 12 months — the repurposing cascade produces a measurable population-level benefit. (d) Falsified if: n-of-1 LDN trials in LMICs show effect sizes substantially smaller than those reported in HIC patient surveys — then the LDN signal either doesn't replicate or doesn't cross settings.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Instead of waiting for a billion-dollar drug that will never reach a rural clinic in Bangladesh, map the cheap, generic drugs the clinic already has to the ME/CFS symptoms patients already have — and test whether they work in those patients, in that clinic, with those resources.

### S1: Nutritional Deficiency-Corrected Fatigue Prevalence in LMIC Primary Care

**Category:** 4 — Supplement/Nutraceutical Idea
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Iron deficiency, B12 deficiency, and vitamin D deficiency are endemic in LMICs and cause fatigue that is clinically indistinguishable from ME/CFS fatigue in the absence of laboratory testing. In HICs, these deficiencies are ruled out as part of the standard ME/CFS diagnostic work-up (CBC, ferritin, B12, TSH, vitamin D). In LMICs, the same work-up is often unavailable, so patients presenting with chronic fatigue cannot have nutritional deficiencies excluded — and may be diagnosed with "chronic fatigue" that would resolve with supplementation. This is the unmeasured confound in every estimate of ME/CFS burden in LMIC settings: what proportion of "post-infectious chronic fatigue" is actually undiagnosed nutritional deficiency in a post-infectious host whose nutritional status was marginal before the infection and worsened during the acute illness? The hypothesis: if nutritional deficiency screening and correction were applied to a cohort of post-dengue fatigue patients in an LMIC setting, 20–40% of those meeting Fukuda CFS criteria would no longer meet criteria after iron/B12/D repletion — because their fatigue had a nutritional, not post-infectious, aetiology. This does not imply that ME/CFS is absent in LMICs — it implies that the denominator (all chronic fatigue patients) is inflated by treatable nutritional deficiencies, and the numerator (true ME/CFS) is smaller than raw prevalence estimates suggest.

**Evidence link:** Iron deficiency prevalence in South Asian women: 40–60% (WHO 2021 global anaemia estimates). B12 deficiency in Indian vegetarians: 50–80% (Refsum2001, Shobha2010). Post-infectious anorexia and catabolism worsen pre-existing nutritional deficiencies — but this interaction has not been studied in post-arboviral settings. The chapter's diagnostic-access environment (@ach:clinical-diagnosis-without-testing) notes that "the exclusionary workup is less available" in LMICs — but does not quantify how much of the "hidden burden" might be nutritionally mediated fatigue rather than ME/CFS. The nutritional-confounding hypothesis provides a quantitative lower-bound on the hidden-burden thesis: if 30% of post-infectious fatigue resolves with supplementation alone, then the upper-bound ME/CFS prevalence is 70% of the raw post-infectious fatigue prevalence — not 100%.

**Certainty:** 0.40 (nutritional deficiency prevalence is well-documented; the interaction between infection, catabolism, and deficiency in producing chronic fatigue is plausible but unquantified; the 20–40% estimate is speculative — the actual proportion depends on baseline deficiency prevalence in the specific cohort; the hypothesis provides a boundary condition for the hidden-burden thesis rather than falsifying it).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a cohort of post-dengue fatigue patients (Fatigue Severity Scale ≥4, ≥6 months post-infection) in an LMIC setting, ≥25% have ferritin <15 ng/mL, B12 <200 pg/mL, or 25(OH)D <20 ng/mL. (b) After 12 weeks of targeted supplementation (iron, B12, vitamin D as per deficiency profile), ≥50% of deficient patients show clinically significant improvement in Fatigue Severity Scale (≥1 point reduction) — the fatigue was at least partly nutritional. (c) Post-supplementation, the proportion meeting Fukuda CFS criteria declines by ≥20% in the full cohort — the denominator shrinks. (d) Falsified if: <5% of post-dengue fatigue patients have nutritional deficiencies AND supplementation produces no fatigue improvement — then nutritional confounding is negligible and the hidden-burden thesis stands undiluted.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Some of the "hidden ME/CFS burden" in poor countries is probably treatable anaemia and vitamin deficiency. Finding and fixing those doesn't make ME/CFS disappear — but it stops us from counting every nutritionally depleted dengue survivor as a ME/CFS patient.

### N1: Community Health Worker-Delivered PEM-Aware Pacing Education

**Category:** 5 — Non-Pharmacological Intervention
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Pacing (energy management within the energy envelope) is the standard-of-care recommendation for ME/CFS in HIC guidelines (NICE NG206, Bateman2021Mayo). But pacing education requires a trained clinician — typically an occupational therapist or ME/CFS specialist — and multiple sessions to teach heart-rate monitoring, activity logging, and energy budgeting. In LMICs, neither the specialists nor the sessions are available. Task-sharing to community health workers (CHWs) is the WHO-endorsed model for delivering non-specialist interventions in LMICs (mhGAP for mental health, PEN for NCDs). CHW-delivered pacing would adapt: (a) replace heart-rate monitoring with a simplified 1–10 RPE (rate of perceived exertion) scale — validated against HR monitoring in low-resource settings, (b) replace activity logging in a smartphone app with a simple paper diary (or mental accounting — CHWs train patients to recognise the "PEM threshold" by symptom recall rather than quantitative tracking), (c) deliver pacing education in 3 home visits over 6 weeks (CHW travel avoids patient PEM from clinic travel), (d) involve family members in the education — in LMIC settings, caregiving is family-delivered, and family understanding of PEM may be the most important determinant of patient energy management, (e) CHW supervision by a remote ME/CFS specialist via monthly phone/Zoom consultation (hub-and-spoke model). The intervention is PEM-aware pacing, not graded exercise — the core education is "stop before you crash," not "push through."

**Evidence link:** Pacing evidence: NICE NG206 meta-analysis (pacing superior to no treatment for function, inferior to CBT for symptom reduction but safer), Goudsmit2012 pacing trial (n=60, pacing vs specialist medical care, pacing group improved). CHW task-sharing: WHO mhGAP (mental health Gap Action Programme) demonstrated feasibility and effectiveness across 90+ LMICs. Family involvement: Thornton2025energy co-production study (family understanding identified as critical). Home-based delivery: Chu2020 remote monitoring feasibility. No CHW-delivered ME/CFS intervention exists. The adaptation from specialist-delivered to CHW-delivered pacing is novel but methodologically straightforward — it is a delivery-channel change, not an intervention-content change.

**Certainty:** 0.35 (pacing evidence is moderate; CHW task-sharing is established for other conditions; PEM awareness training for CHWs is untested — can a CHW with 2 weeks of training recognise PEM better than a physician with 7 years of training?; home-visit delivery solves PEM-travel barrier but creates CHW travel burden; family-involvement component is culturally dependent — in some settings, family pressure to "push through" is the primary obstacle to pacing; the hub-and-spoke supervision model requires a ME/CFS specialist — which may not exist in the LMIC setting).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) CHW-delivered pacing education produces ≥20% improvement in SF-36 physical function at 12 weeks compared to a waitlist control — effect size comparable to or smaller than specialist-delivered pacing but positive. (b) PEM crash frequency (self-reported PEM episodes/week) decreases by ≥30% from baseline in the intervention group — the mechanism (PEM avoidance) is working. (c) Family-reported understanding of PEM increases from baseline and mediates the patient's PEM-frequency improvement — family involvement is a mechanism, not just a contextual factor. (d) CHW fidelity to pacing education (assessed by audio-recorded sessions rated by a ME/CFS specialist) is ≥70% — CHWs can be trained to deliver pacing. (e) Falsified if: no difference between CHW pacing and waitlist control on any outcome — then pacing cannot be task-shared below the specialist level, or pacing itself is ineffective in this population.

**Non-specialist consequence:** A community health worker with two weeks of pacing training and a bicycle may be able to deliver what a ME/CFS specialist in Boston delivers — not because the CHW knows more, but because the patient cannot travel to Boston, and a CHW at the bedside is better than a specialist on another continent.

### C1: WHO PEN Integration — Embed ME/CFS Screening in the Package of Essential NCD Interventions

**Category:** 6 — Combinations + Access
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** WHO PEN (Package of Essential NCD Interventions) is the WHO's standardised protocol for NCD management at primary care level in LMICs, covering cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic respiratory disease, and cancer screening. It is implemented in 40+ countries. ME/CFS is absent from PEN — meaning that a patient presenting with chronic fatigue and PEM to a PEN-trained primary care clinician will be screened for diabetes (glucose), cardiovascular disease (blood pressure), and respiratory disease (peak flow) but not for ME/CFS. The integration proposal: add a one-page ME/CFS screening module to PEN protocols, consisting of: (a) a 3-question PEM screen (adapted from DSQ-PEM, culturally validated per R2 above), (b) a 1-minute sit-to-stand test for orthostatic intolerance (pulse before/after, manual or smartphone), (c) a clinical decision algorithm: if PEM + orthostatic intolerance positive → ME/CFS suspected → refer for diagnostic confirmation (where specialist exists) or manage symptomatically per simplified PEN management card (pacing education, EML drugs for POTS/sleep/pain per D1 above, nutritional screening per S1 above). The integration is designed to be zero-cost beyond printing one page — because PEN is already funded, the marginal cost of adding ME/CFS screening is near-zero. The goal is not to diagnose ME/CFS definitively at primary care — it is to ensure that ME/CFS is in the differential diagnosis for chronic fatigue, rather than being invisible to the system.

**Evidence link:** WHO PEN implementation: 40+ countries, standardised training materials, existing M&E frameworks. PEN integration for mental health (mhGAP-PEN linkage) provides a precedent — adding a condition to an existing PEN card. DSQ-PEM screening items: Cotler2018, Jason2020. 1-minute sit-to-stand: Sutton2022 (validated orthostatic intolerance proxy). The zero-marginal-cost argument depends on PEN already being funded and implemented — in countries where PEN is not yet active, the integration adds no cost but also reaches no patients. The referral pathway for "ME/CFS suspected" is the weak link — if no specialist exists to confirm the diagnosis, the screening identifies patients who then wait for a nonexistent referral.

**Certainty:** 0.40 (PEN integration is logistically simple — it requires WHO endorsement but no new infrastructure; marginal cost is near-zero in PEN-active countries; the weak link is the post-screening pathway — screening without treatment is harm (anxiety without resolution) unless the management card provides actionable guidance; the 1-page screening module must balance sensitivity (not missing cases) against specificity (not overwhelming a non-existent referral pathway with false positives); political feasibility — adding ME/CFS to WHO PEN requires advocacy at WHO Geneva, which has not prioritised ME/CFS).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a pilot district where PEN-ME/CFS screening is added to standard PEN, ≥2% of PEN-eligible patients screen positive for PEM + orthostatic intolerance — the screen detects cases. (b) PEN-ME/CFS screening increases ME/CFS awareness among primary care clinicians (measured by pre/post knowledge test) — the intervention trains clinicians as it screens patients. (c) Of patients who screen positive, ≥50% follow the management card recommendations (pacing, EML symptom management) and report symptom improvement at 6 months — the screening-to-action pathway works. (d) Falsified if: the screen identifies <0.1% of PEN patients as possible ME/CFS, OR the screen identifies patients but no management action is taken — then screening adds no value and may add harm (labelling without management).

**Non-specialist consequence:** ME/CFS screening could be added to the WHO's primary care NCD protocol for the cost of printing one extra page — and in doing so, make ME/CFS visible to primary care clinicians in 40+ countries who currently have no reason to think about it. The question is whether anyone at WHO will push for it.

## Category 7 — Mathematical Model Extensions

Global prevalence and surveillance models.

---

### M1: Diagnostic-Censored Global Prevalence Extrapolation Model

**Category:** 7 — Mathematical Model Extension
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The chapter's central argument is that the absence of ME/CFS data from a country does not imply the absence of ME/CFS. This is a censorship problem in the statistical sense: the true prevalence in country i (p_i) is observed only when diagnostic infrastructure (D_i) exceeds a detection threshold. The observed prevalence is p_obs,i = p_i × P(detect | D_i), where P(detect) → 0 as D_i → 0. The ratio r_i = p_i / p_obs,i is the "censorship inflation factor" — how much larger the true prevalence is than the observed prevalence. If r_i is estimable, the global true prevalence N_true = Σ_i r_i × n_obs,i. The model: (a) define D_i as a composite index from observable proxies — neurologist density per 100,000 (WHO Global Health Observatory), health expenditure per capita (World Bank), ICD-10/ICD-11 adoption status (WHO), ME/CFS research output per country (PubMed count), presence of an ME/CFS patient organisation (binary), (b) estimate P(detect | D_i) from countries where both D_i is high and prevalence data exists (UK, Norway, US, Japan, Australia — the "calibration set"), fitting a logistic or exponential detection function, (c) extrapolate p_i for unobserved countries by assuming a uniform latent prevalence p_latent (from HIC population-based studies: 0.2–0.4% per Nacul2011UK, Jason1999Chicago, Reyes2003Wichita), (d) compute r_i = p_latent / p_obs,i and the global N_true with a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (Monte Carlo sampling of p_latent ± 1 SD, detection-function uncertainty). The model's output is not a point estimate but a distribution — "there is a 90% probability that global ME/CFS prevalence is between X and Y million."

**Evidence link:** Statistical censorship models are standard in ecology (species detection probability), demography (undercount adjustment in censuses), and infectious disease modelling (under-ascertainment in COVID-19). The composite D_i index is novel but methodologically analogous to the WHO's composite health-system performance indices. Calibration set: 5 HICs with population-based ME/CFS prevalence data (Nacul2011UK 0.2%, Jason1999Chicago 0.42%, Reyes2003Wichita 0.24%). The key assumption — uniform latent prevalence across all countries — is the model's weakness and its explicit sensitivity analysis: if true prevalence varies by infectious-disease burden, nutritional status, or genetics, the uniform-p_latent assumption systematically biases the result. The model is designed to be transparent about its assumptions — the sensitivity analysis IS the finding.

**Certainty:** 0.35 (censorship-model methodology is standard; the composite D_i index is plausible but unvalidated — we cannot validate it because we lack ground-truth data in unobserved countries; the uniform-p_latent assumption is known to be wrong but is the least-bad starting assumption in the absence of data; the model's output is properly qualified with wide credible intervals — the point is not precision but bounding; the model's value is that it formalises the chapter's qualitative claim into a quantitative framework that can be iteratively refined as data arrives).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) The censorship-inflated global prevalence estimate is 2–5× the naive estimate (extrapolating HIC prevalence × world population) — the detection-function adjustment is quantitatively meaningful. (b) Countries in the lowest quintile of D_i have estimated r_i > 10 — the detection probability is <10% of the true prevalence. (c) As new data arrives from previously unobserved countries (e.g., sentinel surveillance per R1), the model's posterior p_i estimates converge toward the observed data — the model is Bayesian-updatable. (d) Falsified if: the detection function is flat — D_i does not predict observed prevalence — then the censorship model adds no information, and the gap between true and observed prevalence cannot be estimated from infrastructure proxies; the chapter's hidden-burden thesis remains qualitative because the infrastructure→detection link is not empirically strong enough to quantify.

**Non-specialist consequence:** Right now, the "17–24 million ME/CFS patients globally" figure is a back-of-the-envelope multiplication. This model would give us a range with a confidence interval — not because the model is confident, but because it explicitly models the uncertainty. "We don't know" is a better answer than a made-up number.

## Category 8 — Cross-Disease Bridges

ME/CFS parallels with other disease frameworks for policy and research leverage.

### B1: ME/CFS as a Neglected Tropical Disease (NTD) Structural Analogue

**Category:** 8 — Cross-Disease Bridge
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** The WHO's Neglected Tropical Disease (NTD) framework identifies 21 diseases that share a structural profile: concentrated in poor populations, under-researched relative to disease burden, no market incentive for drug development, diagnosed by clinical criteria without laboratory confirmation, and amenable to mass drug administration or community-based interventions. ME/CFS shares five structural features with NTDs: (a) concentrated in populations with infectious-disease exposure (LMICs → post-arbovirus, post-TB, post-HIV) — even though the current diagnosed population is skewed toward HICs, the trigger exposure is LMIC-concentrated, (b) under-researched — the ratio of research output to DALYs is plausibly worse than many NTDs, though no formal comparison exists, (c) no market incentive — the diagnosed population in HICs is not large enough to attract major pharma investment; the undiagnosed LMIC population is invisible to pharma, (d) diagnosed by clinical criteria — ME/CFS shares with NTDs the "no lab confirmation" diagnostic model, which is both an access barrier (requires trained clinician) and a programme barrier (no point-of-care test for surveillance), (e) amenable to community-based intervention — pacing education, family support, and symptom management can be delivered by CHWs (per N1), analogous to community-directed treatment for NTDs.

The chapter mentions "neglected disease" (Arron2024neglectedDisease) but frames it as a neglected disease in the generic sense — underfunded, understudied. The structural-NTD-analogue framing goes further: it argues that ME/CFS meets the formal criteria for an NTD and could be considered for inclusion in the WHO NTD portfolio — which would unlock NTD-specific funding mechanisms (drug donation programmes, R&D subsidies, integrated surveillance), advocacy platforms (NTD community mobilisation), and research frameworks (NTD implementation research models). This is a policy-proposal hypothesis wrapped in a cross-disease structural comparison.

**Evidence link:** WHO NTD criteria: "disproportionately affect populations living in poverty, cause significant morbidity and mortality, and are amenable to control or elimination." Arron2024neglectedDisease identifies the neglected-disease framing but not the NTD-specific classification. Condé2026dengueFatigue (dengue-endemic LMIC burden), Duvignaud2018chikungunyaFatigue (tropical setting). The WHO NTD list has been revised to include non-communicable conditions (snakebite envenoming added in 2017) — establishing that NCDs can qualify as NTDs if they meet the structural criteria. ME/CFS has never been formally assessed against WHO NTD criteria. The policy pathway from structural analogy to WHO portfolio inclusion is long (years, political) — the analogy's practical value is in the framing: it justifies applying NTD research tools (implementation science, community-directed intervention, integrated surveillance) to ME/CFS in LMIC settings.

**Certainty:** 0.30 (the structural analogies are accurate; the policy pathway is speculative — ME/CFS is not on any WHO NTD consideration list and advocacy for inclusion would face competition from actual tropical diseases with higher mortality; the analogy is strongest for post-infectious ME/CFS in LMICs and weakest for ME/CFS in HICs — it is a LMIC-specific framing; NTD inclusion requires demonstrating that the disease is "amenable to control" — ME/CFS has no disease-modifying treatment, so "control" would need to be defined as "diagnosis + symptom management," which is a lower bar than NTD programmes typically aim for).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Formal application of WHO NTD inclusion criteria to ME/CFS finds that ≥4 of 6 criteria are met — the structural-analogy hypothesis is quantitatively supported. (b) NTD implementation-research frameworks (e.g., TDR implementation research toolkit) when applied to ME/CFS in an LMIC setting identify intervention delivery models (community-directed distribution, school-based screening, mass information campaigns) that are not in the current ME/CFS research repertoire — the framing generates novel research approaches. (c) If a WHO NTD application for ME/CFS is submitted and rejected, the rejection criteria identify which structural features are missing — converting the analogy from a claim to a research agenda (what data would we need for the analogy to hold?). (d) Falsified if: <3 WHO NTD criteria are met — the analogy is weak and ME/CFS is structurally different from NTDs in ways that matter for intervention design.

**Non-specialist consequence:** ME/CFS in poor countries looks more like river blindness or schistosomiasis than it looks like ME/CFS in Oslo — not because the biology is different, but because the delivery problem is the same: how do you reach patients with a non-fatal, chronic, clinically diagnosed condition when there's no lab test, no drug, and no market?

## Category 9 — Diagnostic/Biomarker Ideas

LMIC-deployable diagnostic tools.

### D2: Smartphone PPG Stand Test as Zero-Infrastructure Orthostatic Intolerance Screen

**Category:** 9 — Diagnostic/Biomarker Idea
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Mechanistic rationale:** Orthostatic intolerance (OI) is a hallmark of ME/CFS — present in 60–90% of patients (Bateman2021Mayo, vanCampen2018). The gold-standard diagnostic test is head-up tilt-table testing — expensive, requires specialist equipment, and unavailable in LMICs. The 10-minute NASA lean test is the low-tech alternative: patient lies supine for 10 minutes, stands leaning against a wall for 10 minutes, heart rate and blood pressure measured at 1-minute intervals. It requires a blood pressure cuff and a pulse oximeter — still ~US$50–100, still requiring a healthcare worker to take repeated measurements. Smartphone photoplethysmography (PPG) — using the phone camera and flash to detect pulse via fingertip — can measure heart rate with an accuracy of ±2 bpm compared to medical-grade pulse oximetry in validation studies (Coppetti2017, Pipitkul2023). A smartphone-based NASA lean test would require: (a) a free app that guides the patient through the protocol (visual timer, voice prompts for position changes), (b) finger-on-camera heart rate measurement at 1-minute intervals, (c) a symptom diary (patient taps "dizzy," "lightheaded," "nauseous," "heart pounding" at each interval), (d) an automated summary: max ΔHR (standing – supine), whether ΔHR exceeds POTS threshold (≥30 bpm in adults), symptom–HR correlation. The entire test uses a device that is more common in LMICs than blood pressure cuffs — smartphone penetration in South Asia is 60–80%, in Sub-Saharan Africa 40–50%. The hypothesis: a smartphone NASA lean test can discriminate ME/CFS patients with OI from healthy controls with AUC ≥0.85 in an LMIC setting — a zero-infrastructure diagnostic screening tool for the most common objective ME/CFS abnormality.

**Evidence link:** Smartphone PPG validation against pulse oximetry: Coppetti2017 (mean difference 0.5 bpm, LoA ±3 bpm), Pipitkul2023 (AUC 0.97 for AFib detection from smartphone PPG — demonstrating diagnostic-grade accuracy). NASA lean test: validated against tilt-table testing, sensitivity 80–87% for OI (Hyatt2019, Plash2023). Symptom-finger photoplethysmography in ME/CFS: Bourke2018 demonstrated abnormal cerebral blood flow on standing using NIRS — smartphone PPG only measures heart rate, not cerebral blood flow, so the diagnostic signal is limited to heart rate response + symptom reporting. No smartphone-based NASA lean test for ME/CFS OI screening has been developed or validated.

**Certainty:** 0.45 (smartphone PPG accuracy is validated; NASA lean test is validated; the combination — smartphone-guided NASA lean test — is technically straightforward but unvalidated specifically for ME/CFS OI screening; the test detects OI but does not discriminate between OI from ME/CFS, POTS, deconditioning, dehydration, or anaemia — sensitivity will be high but specificity will be low unless combined with PEM screening; the symptom-diary component relies on patient self-report with no standardised OI symptom scale validated for smartphone delivery; in settings where smartphone ownership correlates with income, the screen may miss the poorest patients — a "zero-infrastructure" tool that requires a smartphone has a different access profile in different populations).

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Smartphone NASA lean test ΔHR discriminates ME/CFS patients (ICC criteria, OI-positive by clinical assessment) from sedentary healthy controls with AUC ≥0.85. (b) The combined classifier — ΔHR ≥30 bpm OR OI symptom score ≥3 — has sensitivity ≥0.90 and specificity ≥0.70 for clinical OI in ME/CFS patients. (c) The smartphone test is completed successfully (valid HR readings at ≥80% of timepoints) in ≥90% of patients in an LMIC urban setting, and ≥70% in a rural setting — smartphone usability is high but not universal. (d) Falsified if: AUC <0.70 — then smartphone PPG alone (without blood pressure) is insufficient for OI screening in ME/CFS, and the "POTS without BP" measurement limitation is fatal to the diagnostic value.

**Non-specialist consequence:** A phone camera and an app could do what a tilt table does — screen for orthostatic intolerance — for free, in any language, anywhere a patient has a smartphone. It won't replace a specialist diagnosis, but it could be the first objective abnormality a patient in a rural clinic can show a doctor.

## Category 10 — Reasons This Mechanism May NOT Be Relevant

What evidence would disprove the hidden-burden thesis.

---

### X1: Differential Post-Infectious Pathogen Profiles — Not All Infections Are Equal Triggers

**Category:** 10 — Reasons Hidden-Burden Thesis May NOT Be Relevant
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Counterargument:** The hidden-burden thesis assumes that all triggering infections produce ME/CFS at comparable rates — that post-dengue ME/CFS prevalence ≈ post-EBV ME/CFS prevalence ≈ post-COVID ME/CFS prevalence. If this assumption is wrong — if different pathogens produce post-infectious fatigue at different rates, with different phenotypes, or through different mechanisms — then HIC prevalence data (mostly herpesvirus- and COVID-triggered cohorts) cannot be extrapolated to LMIC settings (mostly arbovirus-, TB-, and HIV-triggered). EBV infects B cells and establishes latency in the lymphocytic compartment — a mechanism that plausibly produces lasting immune dysregulation. Dengue infects myeloid cells (monocytes, dendritic cells) and does not establish latency — the mechanism for lasting immune dysregulation after viral clearance is different and may be shorter-lived. HIV directly attacks CD4+ T cells and produces chronic immune activation through persistent viral replication — a fundamentally different trigger from self-limited acute infections. If the pathogen-specific ME/CFS conversion rate varies by an order of magnitude (e.g., EBV → 5% CFS, dengue → 0.5%, TB → <0.1%), then the hidden-burden thesis quantitatively overestimates LMIC prevalence by an order of magnitude — not because the patients aren't there, but because the triggers in LMICs are less potent ME/CFS producers. Post-chikungunya fatigue (Duvignaud2018, 38% at 30 months) is the counterexample — chikungunya produces chronic fatigue at rates comparable to EBV. But chikungunya may be the exception, not the rule. No systematic comparison of post-infectious fatigue rates by pathogen exists for ME/CFS criteria specifically.

**Evidence link:** EBV → CFS: Katz1999 (9% at 6 months), White1998 (6% by Fukuda criteria). Dengue → fatigue: Condé2026dengueFatigue (meta-analysis, fatigue prevalence varies 5–45% depending on definition and follow-up, but ME/CFS criteria not applied). TB → fatigue: Maguire2024 (post-TB lung disease includes fatigue, but PEM not assessed). HIV → fatigue: HIV-associated fatigue well-documented but attributed to chronic infection/medication, not post-infectious ME/CFS. No head-to-head comparison of pathogen-specific ME/CFS conversion rates exists. The hidden-burden thesis is vulnerable to this counterargument because it extrapolates from an untested assumption.

**Certainty that the counterargument is correct:** 0.25 (the assumption that all infections are equal triggers is untested — it could be wrong; the chikungunya data argue against large pathogen-specific variation — 38% post-chikungunya fatigue is at the high end of post-EBV fatigue; but dengue and TB data are too sparse to confirm or refute; the counterargument is plausible and damaging but empirically unsupported — it is the mirror image of the hidden-burden thesis: both rest on the same absence of data in opposite directions; the counterargument is most relevant for TB-triggered fatigue — TB involves prolonged illness, cavitary lung damage, and months of hepatotoxic antibiotics, making it causally distinct from self-limited viral infections.)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) In a head-to-head comparison of post-EBV, post-dengue, and post-TB cohorts at 12 months post-infection in a single LMIC setting, ME/CFS prevalence (IOM 2015 criteria) differs by <2× across pathogens — all three produce ME/CFS at comparable rates. If true, the hidden-burden thesis is robust to pathogen-type variation. (b) If post-TB ME/CFS is <1% while post-dengue is ≥5% in the same setting, the pathogen-type counterargument is correct — extrapolation from viral triggers to TB-triggered populations is unsupported. (c) Falsified (counterargument strengthened) if: a systematic review of post-infectious fatigue conversion rates by pathogen finds ≥5× variation — then the hidden-burden thesis needs to be pathogen-stratified, not all-infections lumped.

**Non-specialist consequence:** If dengue kills 20,000 people a year but gives 500,000 others permanent ME/CFS, the "hidden burden" is huge. If it only gives 5,000 people ME/CFS but gives 200,000 people chronic joint pain instead, the hidden-ME/CFS-burden narrative is missing the real post-dengue disability story — which might be rheumatological, not neurological.

## Category 11 — Null Hypothesis Assessment

What if ME/CFS is genuinely less prevalent in LMICs?

---

### N2: Protective Factors in High-Infectious-Disease Settings — The Genuine Lower-Prevalence Null

**Category:** 11 — Null Hypothesis Assessment
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Null hypothesis:** ME/CFS prevalence is genuinely lower in LMICs than in HICs — not because diagnostic access is worse, but because protective factors in LMIC populations reduce susceptibility to post-infectious ME/CFS. The chapter's hidden-burden thesis assumes that the null is false — that prevalence is equal or higher in LMICs and only detection varies. If the null is true, the chapter's central argument is wrong, and research and advocacy resources should not be directed toward closing an LMIC "detection gap" that doesn't exist.

**Possible protective mechanisms:** (a) Helminth co-colonisation — chronic helminth infection modulates immune function toward Th2/Treg dominance, suppressing the Th1/Th17 responses implicated in autoimmune and post-infectious syndromes. The "hygiene hypothesis" extended: in settings where the immune system is chronically occupied with helminths, it may not mount the dysregulated post-infectious response that produces ME/CFS. (b) Early-life infectious-disease exposure — repeated childhood infections may "train" the immune system away from hyperinflammatory post-infectious responses, analogous to the "old friends" hypothesis for autoimmune disease (Rook2014). (c) Younger population age structure — ME/CFS peaks at 30–50 years; LMICs have younger populations, so the age-adjusted prevalence is mechanically lower even if age-specific prevalence is equal. (d) Dietary patterns — lower ultra-processed food intake, higher fibre, different microbiome — may reduce systemic inflammation and post-infectious immune dysregulation. (e) Genetic selection — populations with long evolutionary histories in high-infectious-disease settings may have undergone selection for immune-regulatory alleles that reduce post-infectious autoimmunity — speculative and unmeasurable with current data. (f) Competing mortality — patients who would develop ME/CFS in an HIC die of the acute infection in an LMIC where ICU care is unavailable, removing susceptible individuals from the denominator before they can develop ME/CFS.

**Evidence link:** Helminth-immune modulation: Maizels2014 (helminth Treg induction), Wammes2014 (helminth treatment reduces atopy — atopy is an ME/CFS comorbidity). "Old friends" hypothesis: Rook2014. Post-TB survival bias: 1.3 million TB deaths/year, mostly in LMICs — those who survive to develop post-TB fatigue are survivors of an infection that kills 15% of cases in the absence of treatment, creating a selection bias toward resilient hosts. Autoimmune-disease latitude gradient: MS, type 1 diabetes prevalence increases with latitude — partly hygiene/helminth-mediated, partly vitamin-D-mediated — and if ME/CFS follows an autoimmune pattern, true prevalence may be lower near the equator. All protective mechanisms are individually speculative and collectively untested — the null hypothesis is no more evidence-based than the hidden-burden thesis it challenges.

**Certainty that the null is correct:** 0.15 (competitive plausibility of helminth/Treg protection; early-life immune training is mechanistically plausible; age-structure adjustment is straightforward and would reduce the LMIC-HIC prevalence gap by 20–40% without any biological mechanism; BUT: Simani2021iranCFS shows post-COVID CFS in Iran at HIC-comparable rates — Iran is a MIC with intermediate helminth exposure and moderate age structure, and the null should predict lower rates there; the chikungunya data show high chronic fatigue in a tropical setting; the null requires that these two positive signals are exceptions rather than the pattern; the null is not currently falsifiable — same problem as the hidden-burden thesis.)

**Falsifiable prediction:** (a) Age-standardised ME/CFS prevalence in an LMIC that implements diagnostic capacity (sentinel surveillance per R1) is ≥50% of the HIC prevalence rate — the null is rejected (ME/CFS is not "much rarer" in LMICs). (b) If age-standardised prevalence is <25% of HIC rates after adequate diagnostic deployment, the null is supported — protective factors exist and the hidden-burden thesis overestimates LMIC prevalence. (c) Helminth co-infection status (stool ova-and-parasites) is inversely correlated with post-dengue ME/CFS in a dengue-endemic cohort — the helminth-protection mechanism is specifically supported. (d) Until diagnostic-capacity-equalized prevalence data exists, the null is a hypothesis on equal evidential footing with the hidden-burden thesis — both rest on inference from an absence of data.

**Non-specialist consequence:** It is possible — not likely, but possible — that ME/CFS really is rarer in poor countries. Maybe worms protect you. Maybe people who would get ME/CFS die of the infection instead. Until someone actually counts patients in a poor country with good diagnostic criteria, both "hidden burden" and "genuinely lower prevalence" are guesses — and the paper should admit that.

## Category 12 — Evidence Quality Concerns

Limitations not captured by certainty scores.

---

### E1: The Unfalsifiability Trap — The Hidden-Burden Thesis Predicts Its Own Invisibility

**Category:** 12 — Evidence Quality Concern
**Origin:** brainstorm

**Concern:** The chapter's central argument — that ME/CFS is prevalent but undetected in LMICs — has the structure of an unfalsifiable claim with current data infrastructure. Any study that fails to find ME/CFS in an LMIC setting can be attributed to: (a) the diagnostic criteria being culturally inappropriate (patients have the disease but the criteria don't capture it), (b) the diagnostic infrastructure being insufficient (the criteria would capture it but the exclusionary work-up can't be done), (c) PEM preventing patient participation (the study recruited mild-moderate patients but the disease is most severe in LMICs — wait, that's the opposite direction), or (d) the study using the wrong triggering infection (dengue doesn't trigger ME/CFS, but chikungunya does — try a different infection). The thesis has an endless reserve of auxiliary hypotheses to absorb falsification. Every null finding can be explained by the detection gap rather than true absence. This is not a flaw in the thesis — it is a structural feature of studying a disease without a biomarker in settings without diagnostic infrastructure. The thesis is unfalsifiable while the data infrastructure remains inadequate — and the only thing that would make it falsifiable is exactly the data the thesis argues should be collected. This is a bootstrapping problem: the thesis justifies collecting the data that would test the thesis, but until the data are collected, the thesis cannot be falsified.

**Specific unfalsifiability scenarios:** (a) Sentinel surveillance (R1) finds 1% post-dengue ME/CFS → "the surveillance used Fukuda, not IOM criteria — undercounts." → Surveillance repeats with IOM → finds 1.5% → "IOM criteria require self-reported PEM; cultural barriers to reporting PEM mean true prevalence is higher." → Culturally adapted tool (R2) deployed → still 2% → "the cohort excluded severe patients who couldn't attend follow-up — the hidden burden is in the bedbound patients." At each iteration, the thesis moves. This does not make the thesis wrong — it makes it non-falsifiable by methods short of population-based surveys with culturally adapted criteria, nutritional screening, orthostatic testing, and home-based severe-patient recruitment — a bar that no HIC study has met either.

**Impact on evidence quality:** The hidden-burden thesis should carry a lower certainty than its logical coherence would warrant because it cannot be empirically challenged with current infrastructure. This is a meta-epistemic concern: the Bayesian prior for a unfalsifiable claim should be lower than for a falsifiable claim of equal structural plausibility, because unfalsifiable claims are systematically overrepresented in scientific literatures (the "survivor bias" of hypotheses — falsifiable claims get tested and retired; unfalsifiable claims persist). The chapter should explicitly acknowledge that its central claim is currently unfalsifiable and specify what data would change its mind — not as a rhetorical gesture, but as a methodological requirement.

**Certainty of this concern:** 0.70 (unfalsifiability is a logical property, not an empirical finding — it can be assessed by examining the argument structure; the thesis demonstrably has unrestricted auxiliary hypotheses; the iterative-shift pattern is documented in the philosophy of science (Lakatos 1970 "research programmes," Popper 1959 "conventionalist stratagems") and applied to unfalsifiable epidemiological claims (Ioannidis2005WhyMostPublished); the concern does not mean the thesis is false — it means the thesis's certainty should be lower than the structural evidence alone would warrant.)

**Falsifiable (meta-) prediction:** (a) The chapter's hidden-burden thesis, as stated in its current form, has ≥3 auxiliary hypotheses that could explain a null finding without rejecting the core claim. The count of auxiliary hypotheses is a measure of unfalsifiability. (b) If sentinel surveillance (R1) finds post-dengue ME/CFS >3% — close to HIC post-infectious rates — a single positive finding would resolve more uncertainty than a dozen null findings — asymmetry of evidence. (c) The book chapter should specify a "stopping rule": what combination of negative evidence from LMIC primary-care-based screening, sentinel surveillance, and culturally adapted diagnostic tools would cause the chapter to lower its certainty in the hidden-burden thesis to <0.20 — i.e., what would falsify it. If the chapter cannot specify a stopping rule, the thesis is unfalsifiable and its certainty should be downgraded accordingly.

**Non-specialist consequence:** "We can't find it because we can't look" is a reasonable statement — but "we can't find it, therefore it's there" is not. The paper should be clear which one it is making, and if it can't tell the difference, that's the finding.
