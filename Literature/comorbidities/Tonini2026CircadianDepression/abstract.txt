Title: Clinical and genetic correlates of a circadian subtype of depression in the Australian Genetics of Depression Study
Authors: Emiliana Tonini, Jacob J. Crouse, Mirim Shin, Joanne S. Carpenter, Brittany L. Mitchell, Enda M. Byrne, Penelope A. Lind, Scott D. Gordon, Richard Parker, Samuel J. Journal: The British Journal of Psychiatry (2026)
DOI: 10.1192/bjp.2026.10798
PMID: 42901213 (published peer-reviewed version); preprint PMID 42051568 / PMCID PMC13119355

Abstract (peer-reviewed version):
Background: Although commonly accepted depressive subtypes reflect phenotypic differences, there has been minimal progress in identification of discrete pathophysiological pathways, biomarkers or differential therapeutic approaches.
Aims: To test the biological validity and clinical utility of a circadian subtype of depression on the basis of clinical course, differential medication response (self-reported) and genetic risk profile.
Method: Cross-sectional data from the nationwide, genetically informative Australian Genetics of Depression Study. Participants were classified as having a circadian or non-circadian subtype on the basis of at least three of six binary circadian features: social jetlag, seasonality, delayed sleep midpoint, evening chronotype, sleep inertia/morning tiredness and hypersomnia. Associations with antidepressant response and polygenic risk scores were investigated using logistic regression models.
Results: 2604 participants (23%; 80% female; mean age 37.9 ±13.6) had a circadian subtype. They reported earlier age at onset (p<0.001), more severe clinical features including hypo/manic-like and psychotic-like experiences, suicidality, psychological distress and somatic complaints (p's<0.001), weight gain during episodes (p<0.001), poorer response to SSRIs (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.82–0.94) and SNRIs (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.83–0.97), more side-effects, and higher polygenic risk for ADHD (OR 1.11), major depression (1.11), bipolar disorder (1.09), anxiety (1.10), BMI (1.09), triglycerides (1.10), insulin resistance (1.08), IL-6 (1.08), later sleep midpoint (1.15), insomnia (1.08) and evening chronotype (OR 0.68), versus non-circadian depression.
Conclusions: These findings support the face validity and potential clinical utility of a circadian subtype of depression as a clinical profile. Pending independent replication, investigation of its biology and predictive utility are warranted.
