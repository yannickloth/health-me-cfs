Long COVID (LC), or post‐COVID‐19 condition (PCC), is a heterogenous disorder characterized by persistence of symptoms >12 weeks after COVID‐19 infection that cannot be explained by alternative causes. 1 Common symptoms include breathlessness, fatigue and cognitive difficulties. 1 , 2 Although hospitalization with acute COVID‐19 increases the risk of LC, the majority of people with LC were never hospitalized. 2

An international research effort to understand the biological mechanisms driving symptom persistence, including breathlessness, has not identified a single or unifying explanation although most pathways converge on a dysregulated inflammatory response. 3 A prominent hypothesis is that individuals with LC are prothrombotic and develop “microclots” that lodge in the peripheral vasculature causing local tissue hypoxia and symptoms including breathlessness and post‐exertional malaise. 3 This hypothesis stems from observations in acute COVID‐19 of a hypercoagulable state with impaired fibrinolysis and increased thrombotic risk, together with reports of amyloid‐fibrin(ogen) microaggregates in LC.4, 5, 6 This prothrombotic hypothesis has been used to justify the use of antithrombotic medications and plasmapheresis outside of clinical trials, despite these treatments having established adverse effects. 6 , 7 However, the hypothesis had not been robustly tested in well‐defined never‐hospitalized cohorts with specialist‐confirmed LC. We therefore investigated whether established markers of coagulation/fibrin formation, platelet and endothelial activation, NETosis (neutrophil extracellular traps) and fibrinolysis, together with fibrin clot architecture and fibrin(ogen) microaggregates, were associated with symptoms, pulmonary gas transfer or perfusion abnormalities in participants with never‐hospitalized LC.
